# The Dead and the Rust
## The Slag Valley Siege
### By Gabriel Simon

# 01. The Assembly Line

## Chapter 1: Setup

The assembly plant in Windsor, Ontario, was a cavern of steel and mechanical roar, the air thick with the smell of heavy machine oil, grease, and the sharp ozone tang of electrical discharge. Although the city outside had been dead for months, the plant's massive backup diesel generators had kicked in automatically, running on a pre-programmed PLC loop that kept the main assembly line active. The sound was a rhythmic, deafening cadence of pneumatic cylinders and clacking gears, echoing off the corrugated metal walls of the high bays. The floor vibrated beneath the catwalks, a steady thrum that rattled Elena's teeth and made her grip the handrails tighter. It was a metal jungle of beams and high enclosures.
Elena, a twenty-eight-year-old industrial programmer of mixed heritage, stood in the catwalk doorway, looking down at the factory floor. Beside her was Marcus, a thirty-two-year-old millwright who carried a heavy, three-foot pipe wrench. Below them, the line was moving in a ghostly, mechanical routine. Conveyor belts carried half-finished car chassis along the track, and the massive, yellow robotic welding arms swung and clicked, firing bright showers of orange sparks as they welded invisible joints in the dark.
Elena adjusted her safety glasses, her eyes scanning the complex PLC terminals. Near the doorway, a stack of useless paper money—old hundred-dollar bills from the plant’s safe—was wedged under a loose vibration damper, acting as an impromptu shim to stop the metal cabinet from rattling. It was the ultimate class irony: the plant's former executives, who once worried about stock values, quarterly profit margins, and board meetings, had left their fortunes behind to rot in a world that no longer cared for numbers on a screen. The paper bills were now just recycled paper used by the surviving maintenance crews to stop the vibrations of the metal frames, while the elites themselves were either gone or shuffling aimlessly below in the oil-stained corridors, their expensive suits torn and stained with grease.
"The PLC is running on an infinite loop, Marcus," Elena said, her voice strained over the din. "The sensors are triggering, the conveyors are carrying empty frames, and the welding cells are still operating. We have to reach the main substation on the other side of the floor to redirect the generator power to the municipal water plant. The local shelter depends on those pumps."
"The floor is a maze, Elena," Marcus said, pointing his wrench toward the shadows beneath the conveyor tracks. "And the noise has kept them inside. The crew was working when the sirens went off. They’re still down there, trapped in the mechanical loop." He spat onto the metal catwalk.
"We use the yellow safety walks," Elena said, adjusting her tool belt. "If we stay within the yellow lines, the automated safety sensors won't trigger the heavy gates. But we have to move fast. If a conveyor jams, the line will stall, and the dead will hear our footsteps. Let's get moving."
Marcus nodded, his eyes scanning the mechanical wilderness below. The plant was a monument to industrial automation, but now it was a trap. The rhythmic clanking of the machinery echoed off the corrugated metal walls, masking the shuffling footsteps of the dead. They needed to move before the diesel fuel in the backup generators ran out, which would plunge the entire facility into darkness. Elena checked her tool belt, making sure her wire cutters and multimeters were secure. The municipal water plant down the road was the lifeline for the local survival camp, and without the power redirected from this substation, the pumps would remain frozen. They had to cross the factory floor, a journey of three hundred yards through active welding robots and mindless dead workers trapped in their mechanical routines.

## Chapter 2: Escalation

They descended the iron stairs, the vibration of the heavy machinery thrumming through the soles of their boots. The floor was a forest of steel pillars, hydraulic lines, and stacked pallets of engine blocks. The air was cold and damp, but the heat from the welding cells created pockets of warm, oily air that smelled of burnt paint and hot iron. As they walked past the main welding cell, a half-built sedan frame moved along the overhead track.
Elena gasped, halting.
Caught in the window frame of the moving chassis was a sluggish dead worker. He was still wearing his blue factory coveralls and safety harness, which was snagged on the frame's carrier hook. As the chassis entered the welding station, the sensors triggered.
The yellow robotic arms swung down in unison. With a sharp hiss of pneumatic pressure, the copper electrodes clamped onto the metal frame inches from the corpse's head, firing a blinding shower of sparks. The smell of burning fabric and scorched flesh filled the aisle. The zombie didn't scream. His milky eyes rolled in the glare, his frost-covered jaw snapping slowly in the sparks, his hands clawing at the safety harness as the conveyor carried him forward toward the paint ovens. He was caught in the loop, a mindless passenger on a journey that would never end.
"Don't look," Marcus said, pulling Elena's arm. "We’re almost at the substation cage. Keep your eyes on the walkway."
They rounded a stack of stamping dies, but their path was blocked. Three sluggish dead—former floor managers, their clip-on badges still hanging from their pockets—wandered the aisle. Their joints were locked by the cold and the dry grease, their movements stiff, but their path was direct. They moved with a slow, mechanical shuffle, their hands outstretched. One of them, a former regional director who once earned a seven-figure salary, still wore his gold-plated watch, now covered in grease and soot. He was clutching a clipboard, his fingers frozen around the plastic board.
"They're not fast," Marcus whispered, gripping his wrench. "But they don't stop. They'll block the walkway."
"We can't step off the yellow lines," Elena said, her voice shaking. "If we do, the pressure sensors on the floor will trigger the pneumatic fences, and we'll be trapped in the aisle. We have to squeeze past them."
They waited as the managers shuffled slowly, their boots scraping on the oily concrete. The noise of the welding cells was deafening, but the dead seemed to ignore it, focused only on the heat and the movement of the living. Elena could see the details of their faces—the skin leathery and dry, the lips pulled back over teeth in a silent, mindless gape. They were stiffened by the grease and the cold, their joints popping with every step. Marcus stepped forward, his wrench ready, his eyes locked on the director's gold watch. He felt a wave of disgust. The wealth that once defined these men was now useless, a joke in a world where a pipe wrench was worth more than a bank vault. They slipped past them, their shoulders brushing the dirty uniforms of their former bosses, who only turned their heads with slow, mindless clicks, their locked hips forcing them to pivot their entire bodies to track the living intruders. Their slow movements were a grim parody of their old plant patrols, walking forever on a floor they once owned.

## Chapter 3: Climax & Escape

"Inside the cage!" Elena shouted, running to the wire-mesh gate of the substation.
She opened the latch, slipping inside. Marcus followed, slamming the gate shut and locking the steel bolt just as the first manager reached the wire.
Inside the substation, the massive electrical transformers hummed, their cooling fans throwing a warm draft across the concrete floor. Elena ran to the main breaker cabinet, throwing the latch and opening the heavy steel doors. Inside were rows of black breaker levers and glowing red LEDs, the brain of the factory's electrical system.
Outside the gate, the three sluggish dead reached the wire mesh. One of them—a former supervisor—jammed his arm through the wire, his blue fingers clawing at Marcus's shoulder. His joints were stiff, but his grip on the wire was like an iron clamp, locked by the rigor of dead muscle. He didn't feel pain, and he didn't tire.
Marcus swung his heavy pipe wrench, striking the zombie's arm. The bone cracked, but the fingers didn't release the wire. The zombie thrashed, its jaws snapping against the mesh. The noise drew more dead from the engine assembly bays. Two more figures emerged from the dark, their boots scraping on the oily concrete. They were former line workers, their clothes stiff with grease, their faces blank.
"Elena! Throw the switch!" Marcus yelled, using the wrench to pin the supervisor's arm against the frame.
Elena found the main generator transfer switch—a massive, double-throw copper knife switch. She grabbed the insulated handle, threw her weight downward, and pulled the lever.
With a loud, metallic *clunk*, the switch parted.
A massive blue arc of electricity flashed inside the cabinet, and then, the factory fell into absolute silence. The generators in the yard died. The conveyor belts stopped. The yellow robotic welding arms froze mid-swing, their LEDs turning off, their pneumatic lines releasing with a long, dying sigh. The sudden silence was deafening, replacing the mechanical roar that had filled their ears for hours.
The zombie supervisor, no longer energized by the vibrations of the floor, fell limp against the wire gate. The other dead stopped, their mindless focus broken by the loss of sound and movement. They stood in the dark aisles, frozen like the machinery around them, their limbs lock-jawed and still.
Marcus and Elena scrambled through the rear exit door of the substation, stepping out into the cold, silent afternoon. The municipal water plant down the road hummed to life as the power redirected, a signal that their run had succeeded.
They walked away from the silent steel forest of the plant, leaving the frozen assembly line and its silent workers behind in the dark. The factory was a tomb of steel and grease, a monument to a world that had passed away. But for now, the water was flowing, and the camp was safe.
"We did it," Elena said, her breath vaporizing in the cold air.
"For today," Marcus replied, wiping his wrench with a dirty cloth. "Let's get back before the sun goes down. The night belongs to them, but today we kept our people alive."
They disappeared into the winter twilight, leaving the Windsor plant behind, its dark smokestacks rising like markers over a grave, cold and silent under the gray winter sky, their shadows stretching long across the frozen snow of the parking lot, away from the dead works.

# 02. The Pulpit

## Chapter 1: Setup

The blast furnace at the Hamilton Steel Works was a steel tower of heat and pressure, its massive iron shell groaning under a rising load of gas. Inside the mill, the air was hot, dusty, and smelled of coke gas, sulfur, and molten slag. The automated venting system had gone offline when the grid failed, and the pressure inside the gas collector lines was climbing toward critical levels. The noise was a low, constant vibration that made the metal floorplates hum, a warning that the giant was waking up. It was a cavern of red glare and black dust, a place where iron was melted and shaped by human sweat.
Devin, a forty-year-old African-Canadian millwright who had worked the furnace floor for fifteen years, stood at the base of the crane ladder. He wore heavy leather furnace clothing, a hard hat, and carried a three-foot steel spud wrench. Beside him was Wei, a thirty-five-year-old control room operator of Chinese-Canadian descent. She was clutching a schematic folder, her face pale in the orange glare of the hot metal runner below.
Wei looked at her gauge sheet, which was held together by a metal clip. In her pocket, she had a stack of useless paper bills—old hundred-dollar bills—which she used as grease wipes for the greasy dials. Once, this pocket money would have paid for a fine dinner in downtown Toronto; now, paper currency was only good for cleaning soot off glass dials or starting small fires in the survival camp’s stove. The former mill owners and bank directors who once collected dividends from these furnaces had fled in their private helicopters, leaving their stock certificates to rot in safe deposit boxes while working-class operators like Devin and Wei were left to keep the world from blowing up.
"If the collector line blows, Devin," Wei said, her voice strained over the high-pitched hiss of escaping gas. "It’ll tear the top off the furnace. The explosion will level the storage sheds where the community is sheltered. We have to reach the operator pulpit to manually throw the vent valves."
"The pulpit is thirty feet up," Devin said, pointing to the steel structure suspended above the casting floor. The pulpit was a reinforced glass and steel booth, designed to withstand the heat of the liquid iron. "The only access is the open metal stairs, and the catwalks are narrow. The crew on the afternoon shift... they didn't make it out. They're still up there."
"We have to go," Wei said, adjusting her safety harness. "The pressure gauge on the furnace shell is already in the red. We have ten minutes before the relief valves shear."
"Keep your head down," Devin said. "The dead up there are covered in coal dust and hydraulic oil. They’re slick, and they’ve got no grip, but they still remember the valves. Vestigial memory is a strange thing; they will keep pulling at the levers they pulled in life."
Wei nodded, her face grim. They had to climb the open steel stairs, exposed to the rising heat and the dead who patrolled the high walks. The furnace roared like an angry beast, venting small jets of flame from the seals, warning them that time was running out. They stepped onto the first rung of the ladder, their boots heavy, their hearts pounding in their chests. The metal rails were hot to the touch, and the air was thin, choking them with the taste of sulfur and dry ash.

## Chapter 2: Escalation

They climbed the open steel stairs, the heat from the furnace floor hitting them in waves. Below, the red-hot slag was slowly cooling in the pits, casting a dark, hellish light across the steel girders. The metal catwalks were slippery with oil and grease. As they reached the fifteen-foot level, they heard a rhythmic clinking sound coming from the darkness ahead.
*Clink. Clank. Clink.*
Devin stopped, raising his spud wrench.
A figure was standing at a secondary control station on the walkway. It was a former furnace operator, still wearing his silver, heat-reflective jacket and visor. His skin was gray, covered in grease and soot. In a display of vestigial muscle memory, the zombie’s hand was wrapped around the handle of a manual gas valve. He was slowly, rhythmically pulling the lever back and forth, the metal clanking against the stop brackets. He didn't turn to look at them until the vibration of their boots on the steel grating reached his platform.
"He's running the old routine," Wei whispered, her eyes wide as she pressed against the safety railing.
"His hands are locked on the valve," Devin said. "Keep moving, Wei. Don't touch the handrail near him."
As they passed, the silver-coated zombie released the valve, his joints crackling like dry twigs as he lunged toward Wei. His boots slipped on the oily steel, but his hands reached out, his gray fingers finding a grip on the strap of Wei's safety harness. His grip was an iron clamp, locked by the rigor of dead muscle.
Wei screamed, losing her footing on the slick walkway. She hung over the edge of the catwalk, thirty feet above the cooling slag pits, the weight of the zombie pulling her down. Her schematic folder slipped from her grasp, the papers fluttering down into the heat, burning to ash before they hit the ground.
"Devin!" she gasped, her fingers slipping on the oily railing.
The zombie's face was close to hers, his jaws snapping slowly, his breath smelling of sulfur and rot. He showed no intelligence, only the mindless, relentless pull of a machine that had been set to grab. Devin scrambled back down the stairs, his heavy boots clattering on the metal grates, his wrench raised to strike. He had to be careful; one slip on the grease-slicked steel and both of them would plunge into the darkness below. He planted his back foot and swung. He struck the zombie's wrists, trying to shatter the bones to force the grip to open, but the frozen muscles held fast. The dead man's visor was cracked, revealing the hollow, dry eyes beneath, staring blankly at nothing while his hands remained locked. Devin shifted his weight, using his wrench as a pry bar to force the fingers open one by one, sweat stinging his eyes as the furnace heat beat against his neck. The steel of the catwalk groaned under their combined weight, and Wei clung desperately to the slippery guardrail with her remaining hand, her boots kicking uselessly in the hot, rising drafts of the furnace pit. "Hurry, Devin!" she choked out, her grip beginning to fail as the heavy scent of sulfur threatened to overwhelm her senses. With a final grunt of effort, Devin wedged the steel tool under the rotting knuckles, using his body weight to break the dead lock. The dry bone splintered with a sickening sound, and the fingers fell away, releasing the pressure on the harness. The zombie slipped down the frame, falling away from the stairs.

## Chapter 3: Climax & Escape

"Hold on!" Devin shouted.
He lunged forward, driving the wedge-shaped end of his steel spud wrench into the gap between the zombie's fingers and the harness strap. Using the wrench as a lever, he pried the frozen fingers open with a wet crack. The dried joint snapped, and the grip released.
The zombie fell backward, his boots sliding on the greasy steel, and he tumbled over the handrail, falling thirty feet onto the casting floor below. He hit the concrete with a dull thud, his limbs twisted, but his head turned slowly, still trying to crawl.
"The pulpit door!" Wei gasped, scrambling up the final flight of stairs.
The door of the operator pulpit was a heavy steel door with a double-paned safety glass window. But the door was blocked. Two sluggish dead—former supervisors—were standing outside the glass, their hands resting on the emergency trip-cord that hung from the ceiling. In their slow, mechanical state, they were pulling the cord, which triggered a loud, pulsing warning siren that echoed through the mill, drawing more dead from the lower maintenance bays.
"I'll clear them!" Devin said.
He charged the walkway, swinging his heavy wrench. The steel struck the first supervisor’s shoulder, throwing it against the glass.
Wei slipped past, unlocking the pulpit door with her master key and diving inside. She ran to the main control console, her fingers flying over the physical toggle switches. She found the emergency vent valve—a large red lever.
She threw the lever.
With a deafening, high-pitched roar, the main relief valves at the top of the furnace sheared open. A massive plume of gray gas and steam vented into the sky outside, the pressure gauge needle instantly dropping back into the green. The furnace groaned, settling back into its frame.
The siren ceased.
Inside the pulpit, Wei collapsed against the console, her breath coming in ragged gasps. Outside the glass, Devin had pinned the second supervisor against the handrail, driving the spud wrench into its head. The figure went limp, sliding onto the deck.
They locked the pulpit door from the inside, the reinforced safety glass keeping the remaining three sluggish dead on the catwalk out. The dead stood outside, their soot-covered faces pressing against the glass, their jaws snapping silently in the hot, sulfurous air. One of them had a high-end corporate gold watch on his shriveled wrist, a useless luxury in a world that had burned.
Wei looked down at the storage sheds through the high window. The steam plume had cleared, and the shelters were safe.
"We vented the furnace, Devin," Wei said, her hand steady on the console.
"Yes," Devin said, looking at the glowing red metal below. "The plant is still here. We have a place to stand."
They sat in the secure pulpit, the heavy glass keeping the heat and the dead out, keeping their watch over the steel mill under the dark, smoky sky. The furnace hummed a lower tune now, its anger vented into the winter wind, leaving them in the warm safety of the cabin. Devin wiped his face with his sleeve, leaving a dark streak of coal dust across his forehead as they watched the steam dissipate in the cold sky outside. The steel cages below held the silent, unmoving dead, locked in their cold tombs. They were safe, at least for now, in the high, warm heart of the mill, protected by the heavy plate doors.

# 03. The Fulfillment Racks

## Chapter 1: Setup

The interior of the automated fulfillment center was a steel cathedral of shelving, stretching for acres under a high metal roof. The air was cold, dry, and filled with the low, electric hum of the automated shuttle tracks. The plant’s backup solar array on the roof kept the overhead logistics network running, even though the world outside was dead. The silence was heavy, punctuated only by the occasional clatter of mechanical runners shifting boxes from one sector to another.
Elena and Marcus stood at the base of Aisle 4. The steel racks rose thirty feet into the dark ceiling, filled with thousands of yellow plastic bin slots.
"He's up there, Marcus," Elena whispered, pointing her flashlight toward the top tier of the racks. "Kofi. He was working the inventory audit when the outbreak hit. He climbed the racks to escape, but the ground floor is overrun."
"How do we climb?" Marcus asked, looking at the vertical steel pillars. "There are no ladders."
"We use the shuttle rails," Elena said, pointing to the horizontal steel tracks that ran along each level of the racks. "The robotic picking units—the shuttles—run along these tracks. We can climb the steel frame, but we have to watch the sensors. If a shuttle detects us on the tracks, it’ll run us over."
Marcus spat on the floor. Near his boot was a discarded leather wallet belonging to a former warehouse manager. It was stuffed with credit cards and crisp bills, now completely worthless. Marcus kicked it aside, using a green hundred-dollar bill that had blown out of it to clean the grease from his flashlight lens before throwing the paper to the floor. It was a stark reminder of the class irony that defined their new life: the executives who had built these massive, automated distribution centers to bypass manual labor had fled to their private estates, yet their automated systems still ran on a mindless loop, and their money was now just trash. The working-class survivors had no use for paper wealth, only for tools and food. They needed wrenches, oil, and solid boots, not credit scores. The entire logistics network was powered by the solar roof inverters, running a pre-programmed distribution pattern that had no relevance to the freezing world outside, yet the machinery hummed on, blind and tireless. Elena checked the wiring on the side panel, noting how the copper contacts were covered in a thin layer of grime. She wiped it away with a piece of a discarded invoice, another relic of the old retail giant. They had to reach the summit of the racks before the main power grid cycling cut the lights, which would leave them in absolute pitch blackness.
From the top tier, a faint light flickered. "Elena? Marcus?" a young man's voice called down, trembling and dry. "Is that you? The battery in my radio is dead. I've been up here for three days."
"Hang on, Kofi!" Marcus called back. "We're coming up! Keep your head down!"
"Watch the line," Elena warned.
Above them, a robotic shuttle unit—a flat, rectangular machine with flashing blue lights—whirred along the Level 3 track. It carried a large yellow bin, moving with a high-speed hum. The machine stopped at Slot 112, its mechanical arms extending to pull a bin from the shelf. The hum of the system was constant, a digital heartbeat that ignored the rot below, sorting boxes and parcels as if the customers were still waiting for deliveries that would never come. Elena adjusted her climbing harness, pulling the straps tight to ensure she wouldn't slip during the vertical ascent.

## Chapter 2: Escalation

They began their climb, using the horizontal shuttle rails as footholds and the steel structural braces as hand-holds. Marcus went first, his boots finding grip on the narrow metal edges.
The height was dizzying. As they reached the fifteen-foot level, the ground floor looked like a dark maze. Through the mesh, they saw shapes—sluggish dead workers wandering the concrete aisles below, their white eyes reflecting the light. They moved in slow, purposeless patterns, their boots dragging on the floor. The cold air smelled of static electricity, dust, and decay.
"Look out," Elena said, halting on Level 2.
A shuttle unit was approaching along their track, its blue light flashing. They had to swing their bodies outward, hanging from the steel braces, as the machine whirred past, its wheels inches from their fingers. The vibration of the track shook their arms, making their grip slick with sweat.
As the shuttle passed, Elena looked inside the yellow bin it was carrying. The bin did not contain consumer goods. Tangled inside the plastic container was the torso of a former worker. He had fallen onto the sorting conveyor weeks ago, and the automated sorting sensors, unable to distinguish a human body from cargo, had packed him into the bin and sorted him into the high racks.
His limbs were mangled, and his skin was a waxy gray-blue. But he was still active. As the shuttle carried him past, his head turned slowly in the yellow box, his jaws snapping at the air, his fingers clawing at the plastic rim.
"The robots are still sorting them," Elena said, her voice shaking. "They treat them like inventory."
"They're storing them in the high racks," Marcus said. "It's a filing cabinet of the dead."
They continued their climb, their muscles aching from the strain. The cold air of the warehouse chilled their faces, but the physical effort kept them sweating. On the next level, another shuttle hummed by, carrying a bin filled with old, moldy corporate documents and invoices—worthless records of a global retail empire that had dissolved in a matter of weeks. The machinery cared nothing for the collapse of society; it only cared for barcodes and sensor lines. They reached Level 5, the high shelves stretching out around them like a maze of steel beams, the ground floor far below. They had to keep their balance on the narrow structural beams, knowing that one step back would mean falling into the dark. Elena reached out, her fingers catching the next steel frame, her eyes focused on the flashing lights above. The steel rails were cold and sharp, biting into their palms through their worn leather work gloves, and every shift of their weight required intense concentration to avoid slipping off the greasy metal edges. The darkness below was absolute, a pit of slow, scratching fingers. Marcus kept his eyes upward, refusing to look down at the floor. Marcus adjusted his grip on the girder, his boots finding a narrow brace. The height was one thing, but the slow, crawling movement of the dead below was another. He could hear their dry boots dragging on the concrete floor, a sound like autumn leaves scraping against a grave. The rails they climbed vibrated every time a shuttle unit cycled on the adjacent tracks. The smell of cold iron and dry dust was thick, drying out their throats as they gasped for breath.

## Chapter 3: Climax & Escape

They reached the top tier, Level 6, thirty feet above the concrete floor. Kofi was huddled on a wooden pallet inside Slot 604, his face pale, his lips chapped. Beside him was a crate of medical supplies he had managed to salvage.
"I've got the supplies, Elena," Kofi said, his voice cracking. "But the shuttle on this level... it’s stuck. It keeps cycling the bin next to me."
A shuttle unit on Level 6 was jammed. Its blue light was flashing a warning, and its mechanical arms were extending and retracting, trying to pull a yellow bin from the shelf. Inside that bin was a sluggish, bloated dead manager, his arm caught in the shuttle’s grip. Every time the machine tried to pull the bin, the zombie’s hand thrashed, blocking the sensor and causing the machine to reset.
*Whir. Click. Whir.*
The cycle was repeating every ten seconds.
"We have to clear the jam to get the crate down," Marcus said, crawling along the narrow steel beam.
"The wind-up is fast, Marcus," Elena warned, climbing to the control panel at the end of the aisle. "I'll try to bypass the PLC and override the shuttle. But you only have one cycle."
Marcus reached the jammed shuttle. The zombie manager’s eyes snapped open, his gray jaws snapping inches from Marcus’s glove. He was wearing an expensive, tailored shirt under his torn safety vest, a useless relic of his former status.
"Go!" Marcus yelled.
Elena typed an override code into the panel. The shuttle’s arm retracted, releasing the grip.
Marcus used his wrench, striking the zombie’s arm and pushing the bin back into the shelf. He then grabbed the crate of medical supplies, sliding it to Kofi.
But the override caused the shuttle to reset. The machine started its high-speed return loop, heading directly for Marcus on the track.
Marcus swung his body down, hanging by his hands from the steel beam as the shuttle whirred over his head. The wheels brushed the fabric of his sleeves.
"Hold on!" Kofi yelled, grabbing Marcus’s collar and hauling him up onto the pallet.
They secured the cargo crate, tying it to a rope, and lowered it to the ground floor. They scrambled down the steel frame, their boots sliding on the metal braces, and reached the safety of the main door just as the sluggish dead on the floor began to gather at the base of Aisle 4.
Elena threw the manual override switch, shutting down the solar inverter for Aisle 4.
The shuttle units on the tracks went dark, the blue lights turning off, leaving the high racks in absolute silence.
They escaped through the warehouse gates, carrying the medical supplies back to the camp, leaving the automated maze and its silent, sorted inhabitants behind in the dark. The concrete outside was cold, but they had the medicine, and Kofi was safe. They walked under the winter stars, their breathing heavy but synchronous, their cargo secure in the cart behind them. It was a long walk back to the compound, but they had won another night of life against the automated systems that refused to die. In the silence of the forest outside, the towering warehouse loomed like a dark tomb of consumerism, its internal robots finally sleeping. They secured the cart, making sure the rope wouldn't slip, and headed out into the night, their boots crunching on the frozen gravel of the driveway.

# 04. The Foundry Pour

## Chapter 1: Setup

The casting floor of the Algoma Steel Foundry was a landscape of raw heat and dark iron, dominated by the towering shape of Blast Furnace No. 5. The furnace was tap-ready, the liquid iron inside a white-hot lake of two thousand degrees. The air was dry and thick with the smell of sulfur, burned clay, and coke gas. It was a massive cavern of industry, where the walls were blackened by decades of soot and coal dust. The overhead cranes sat idle, their giant hooks hanging like frozen iron anchors under the high corrugated roof.
Devin stood near the primary runner—a three-foot-wide steel channel lined with refractory clay that ran across the concrete floor. He carried a heavy, ten-foot oxygen lance, a steel pipe connected to pressurized tanks of oxygen used to burn through the furnace’s clay tap-hole. Beside him stood Wei, on the lower deck of the control pulpit, holding a long steel scraper. Her silver heat-shield coat reflected the orange light of the furnace shell.
Wei looked at the floor, where a pile of old financial ledgers and corporate checkbooks had been dumped. They were now used by the foundry workers as fuel for the warming barrels or to wipe oil off the tools. Once, the numbers in those books represented millions of dollars in capital; now, they were just kindling. The wealthy executives who had owned the mill had fled to the south months ago, but the mill itself remained, a monument to the labor of the working class. Their paper riches were useless, a joke in a world where warmth and steel were the only true currency. Devin kicked a charred binder into the coal dust, his thoughts on the task ahead.
"The gate house is surrounded, Devin," Wei said, her voice dry in the hot air. "The sluggish ones from the rail yard are clawing at the fences. If we can tap this furnace and run the molten iron down the main trench, we’ll create a channel of liquid fire that they can’t cross. It’ll cut the yard in half and protect the generator house."
"The channel is blocked, Wei," Devin said, pointing the lance toward the runner. "Old slag and scrap metal have frozen in the gate section. We have to clear it manually before we tap the furnace. If the iron hits a blockage, it’ll overflow onto the casting floor and trap us."
"We tap in five minutes," Wei said, checking the pressure valves on the oxygen tanks. "The heat is drawing them, Devin. The furnace is radiating, and the dead—they walk toward the heat. They think it's life."
"Let's clear the gate," Devin said, stepping onto the hot concrete. He gripped his scraper, his muscles tensing against the heat. They had to clear the hardened metal chunks before the furnace was breached, a dangerous task under the best of conditions, and now made deadly by the silent threat wandering the casting bays. The furnace walls groaned under the immense thermal stress, sending small tremors through the brick floor. Wei looked at the steam venting from the main line, her eyes tracking the pressure gauge. The Hamilton mill was a beast of iron, and they were the only ones left to tame it. They had to keep their focus, despite the heat. The air shimmered with the intense radiation of the furnace shell, threatening to melt their safety gear if they stood too close. The ground plates under their feet were already starting to buckle from the heat.

## Chapter 2: Escalation
