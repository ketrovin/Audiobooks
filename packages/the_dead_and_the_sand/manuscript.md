# 01. The Glass Oven

## Chapter 1: Setup

The heat in Palm Springs was not merely a temperature; it was a physical weight, a suffocating hammer of fifty-degree air that pressed down on the cracked, bubbling asphalt of Vista Chino Road. The municipal power grid had collapsed three months ago during the final chaotic week of the evacuation, and without the constant, mechanical hum of ten thousand air conditioning units, the desert resort city had rapidly emptied. The emerald swimming pools of the luxury mid-century modern estates had dried into cracked, concrete basins filled with blowing sand and the skeletal remains of dead palm fronds. The fan palms along the boulevard stood like scorched matches against a copper sky that seemed to hold no moisture, only dust. The heat shimmered off the flat stucco walls of the valley, distorting the horizon into a wavering, liquid mirage.

Ramon, a thirty-two-year-old solar technician, adjusted the damp, salt-crusted bandanna around his neck. He wore thick, double-kneed canvas work pants, a wide-brimmed straw hat, and yellow safety goggles to protect his eyes from the blinding, white-hot glare reflecting off the gravel yards. Hanging from his heavy leather tool belt was a Fluke digital multimeter, a set of chrome-vanadium socket wrenches, and insulated screwdrivers. Beside him was Asha, twenty-eight, a community garden organizer who had helped establish the camp’s water-well security in the rocky foothills of the San Jacinto Mountains. She carried a heavy steel crowbar and a canvas backpack, her fingers checking the cinch straps to ensure the weight would be distributed evenly if they had to run.

They stood at the rusted steel gates of a modern, multi-million-dollar estate at the end of a private cul-de-sac. The driveway was littered with wind-blown debris—dried tumbleweeds, shattered roof tiles, and piles of paper that had spilled from a discarded designer briefcase. Asha knelt, picking up a handful of the papers. They were stock options for a Silicon Valley tech conglomerate and municipal utility bonds printed on heavy, embossed paper. 

"Look at this," Asha said, a dry, bitter laugh catching in her throat as she flicked the corner of a certificate. "Millions of dollars in municipal utility bonds. Once, this would have bought half the valley."

"Good for nothing but boot insulation now," Ramon replied, his voice raspy and dry. "Stuff them in your soles. The pavement is hot enough to melt the glue on your boots."

Asha nodded, sliding several sheets of the thick, useless bonds into the bottom of her work boots, creating a barrier against the heat of the ground. She tucked the remaining stock options into her pocket to use as shims for the battery racks they would have to build back at the camp. Wealth in this new world was measured in gallons of fresh water and working lithium-ion cells, not paper bonds.

They looked up at the villa. It was a monument to wealth and architectural hubris—a sprawling structure of steel frames, polished concrete, and massive, floor-to-ceiling glass walls designed to offer panoramic views of the desert floor. But now, in the post-apocalyptic desert, the glass was a deadly greenhouse trap. The sun beat down on the flat roofs, and without ventilation, the heat accumulated inside.

"The HVAC system failed weeks ago, Asha," Ramon said, pointing his screwdriver toward the roof where several rows of solar panels sat dusty and inactive. "With these glass walls and no ventilation, the sun has turned this entire villa into a solar kiln. The air inside will be sixty, maybe seventy degrees. It’s an oven, literally."

"We need those lithium-ion battery blocks from the garage, Ramon," Asha said, wiping sweat from her forehead with a gloved hand. "The well pump at the shelter is failing. The old lead-acid cells we salvaged from the golf carts can't hold a charge in this heat. If the battery bank dies before the sun sets, the water flow stops, and the community garden dries up by tomorrow morning. Those crops are the only food we have for the winter."

"The front door is locked, but the glass next to the patio is cracked," Ramon said, pointing his crowbar toward the rear courtyard. "But watch the shadows. The owner didn't make it out when the grid went down. He’s still inside, and in this heat, he’ll be dried out like leather."

They stepped onto the gravel courtyard, their boots crunching softly. In the desert silence, the sound of their steps felt dangerously loud, a beacon for anything wandering the dry arroyos nearby.

## Chapter 2: Escalation

They entered through the shattered patio window, stepping carefully over the glittering glass shards into the open-concept living room. The heat inside was immediate and suffocating, a thick, motionless wave of seventy-degree air that made Ramon’s lungs seize. The smell was a dry, musty tang of scorched leather, old dust, and decomposed paper. 

"Don't touch the metal frames," Ramon warned in a low whisper, pointing to the steel support beams. "They'll burn your skin right through your gloves. Treat everything like it's fresh out of a forge."

Asha pointed to the hallway. "Garage access is through the kitchen. Let’s move before the heat makes us dizzy."

As they crossed the polished concrete floor, Ramon looked toward the master bedroom. Through the open door, he saw a shape slumped on the leather sofa. 

It was a man, his skin baked by the greenhouse heat into a tough, shriveled gray-brown hide. The dry desert air had mummified him, drawing out all the moisture until his flesh was tight against his skull, his teeth exposed in a dry, permanent gape. He was wearing an expensive silk robe, now dusty and faded. On the floor beside him lay an open digital safe, its contents spilled across the concrete: gold Rolex watches and stacks of hundred-dollar bills. Asha noticed that several stacks of the worthless cash had been shoved under the corner of a nearby designer coffee table to keep it from wobbling. Class privilege had shrunk to the level of paper furniture shims.

The corpse did not move immediately, but as their heavy boots vibrated through the concrete floor, the dry, sun-mummified owner began to twitch. His neck crackled like dry paper as his head turned slowly, the empty, dry sockets of his eyes locking onto their direction. He let out a low, dry rasp as he tried to open his jaw, his joints creaking under the strain of vestigial muscle memory. There was no speed in him; the intense heat and lack of moisture had stiffened his tendons, locking him in a sluggish, slow-motion struggle.

"He's waking up," Asha said, her hand tightening on the crowbar. 

"He's slow in the day," Ramon said, guiding her toward the kitchen. "The heat makes them sluggish. But don't let him corner you. If he grabs you, his dried skin is tough as hide, and you won't break his grip easily."

They burst into the garage, where the cool concrete floor offered a temporary relief from the oven-like heat of the main house. Mounted on the wall was the Tesla Powerwall inverter system—three heavy, white battery blocks. 

Ramon set to work, using his socket wrench to undo the mounting bolts, his fingers sweating inside his gloves. "Asha, hold the frame. If it drops, the casing will crack, and the lithium cells inside will catch fire. We can't afford a chemical fire in this heat."

The bolts were stiff, locked by the expansion of the metal in the high temperatures. Ramon strained against the wrench, his muscles burning as he worked to free the heavy units.

Outside, the wind began to rise, sand scratching against the metal garage doors. 

"Ramon, we have to hurry," Asha said, looking at the door they had just come through. The scratching sound from the hallway was getting closer, and it wasn't the wind.

## Chapter 3: Climax & Escape

The metal bolts were hot, burning through Ramon's leather gloves as he worked. He wiped a bead of sweat from his eye, his vision blurring in the stifling garage.

Suddenly, a dry *crunch* echoed from the kitchen door. 

The mummified owner had dragged himself into the garage. He moved with a stiff, mechanical shuffle, his bare, leathery feet scraping on the concrete floor. Behind him, through the glass walls of the living room, they saw more shapes. Two sluggish, dry dead—former landscapers in dusty work clothes—were crawling over the broken patio glass, drawn by the sound of Ramon’s wrench. They were equally dried by the desert sun, their movements slow, halting, and silent. They did not run; they simply shuffled forward, driven by the mindless, relentless urge to pursue.

"They're coming through the house, Ramon," Asha said, raising her crowbar. 

"I've got the first block loose!" Ramon panted, hoisting the forty-pound battery into Asha’s pack. "Get the second one!"

The mummified owner lunged, his dry, leathery arms reaching for Ramon's neck. Ramon sidestepped, swinging his heavy wrench. The metal struck the zombie’s shoulder, but the dried, leathery skin absorbed the blow, the bone crackling but the figure remaining upright. The mummy’s skin was like cured rawhide, resisting the blunt impact.

Asha swung her crowbar, striking the zombie’s knee. The dried joint snapped with a sharp sound like dry wood breaking, and the mummy fell to the floor, still crawling and snapping its teeth. 

"The front gates!" Asha shouted. 

The two dead landscapers had reached the garage door. They were dry and stiff, but their numbers blocked the main exit. 

Ramon grabbed the second battery block, carrying it under his arm. "Through the side utility door! The lock is manual!"

Asha lunged at the first landscaper, driving the flat end of her crowbar into its face. The dried skull cracked, and the corpse fell back into the driveway. 

They burst through the utility door into the blinding Palm Springs heat. They ran across the gravel courtyard, the two mummified dead shuffling behind them, their dry feet scraping uselessly on the stones as they tried to match the speed of the living. 

They reached their solar-powered truck, throwing the batteries into the back and jumping into the cab. Ramon started the motor, the tires throwing up gravel as they sped down Vista Chino Road. 

Through the rear window, Asha looked back at the glass villa. In the shimmering heat waves, the glass-and-steel house stood silent under the white-hot sky, a gilded oven where the dry, mummified dead remained, trapped in the ruins of their own luxury. 

"We got the power, Ramon," Asha said, her chest rising and falling as she caught her breath. 

"Yes," Ramon said, steering the truck toward the shelter. "The pump stays running tonight."

They drove back to the commune, the battery blocks secure. The crops would drink tonight, and the community would live to see another sun rise over the dunes.

# 02. The Adobe Shelter

## Chapter 1: Setup

The red sandstone walls of the canyon rose three hundred feet into the copper-colored sky, radiating heat like the bricks of a kiln. The midday sun was blinding, and the air was so dry that every breath felt like sand in the throat. The desert floor below was a barren flat of cracked clay and dry brush, where the heat waves shimmered like water, distorting the horizon. 

Yazzie, a thirty-year-old Diné (Navajo) park ranger, led the way along the narrow clay trail. He wore his faded ranger uniform, a wide-brimmed hat, and carried a canteen of alkaline canyon water. Behind him came Nadia, a twenty-five-year-old teacher from Gallup, who was holding the hand of a seven-year-old girl named Clara. Two other children followed, their faces red and dusty, their lips chapped. 

At the back of their group was Harrison, a wealthy collector of Native American antiquities who had fled Santa Fe when the city fell. Unlike the others who carried only water and blankets, Harrison was struggling under the weight of a heavy leather backpack. He kept his hands protective over the straps, refusing to let anyone help him.

"Just a little further," Yazzie said, pointing to a high ledge on the canyon wall where the sandstone had eroded into a deep, shaded alcove. Nestled inside the alcove were the ruins of an ancient Ancestral Puebloan cliff-dwelling—a cluster of multi-story rooms built of flat stones and adobe mortar. 

"The old ones knew how to build, Nadia," Yazzie said. "The stone walls and the deep alcove block the direct sun. In the day, it stays twenty degrees cooler inside those rooms, and at night, the stones release the heat to keep us warm. We can shelter there until the heat wave passes."

"Is there water?" Nadia asked, her voice dry. 

"There's a natural seep in the back of the alcove," Yazzie said. "A slow drip, but enough to fill our canteens. But we have to climb the hand-holds. The access trail is narrow, only two feet wide, with a fifty-foot drop. We go one by one."

"What about the ones in the wash?" Nadia asked, looking down at the dry, sandy riverbed below. 

"They're down there," Yazzie said. "The heat has dried them out, turned them into leather. In the day, they don't move. They stand in the shadow of the boulders, waiting. But if they hear us climb, they’ll follow the trail."

## Chapter 2: Escalation

They reached the base of the cliff trail. The access path was a series of hand-and-toe holds carved directly into the red sandstone, leading up the vertical wall to the ledge of the ruins. 

Yazzie climbed first, his boots finding familiar purchase in the stone pockets. He reached the ledge, securing a nylon rope to a heavy juniper post, and helped Nadia and the children climb up one by one. 

When Harrison reached the bottom of the ladderway, he groaned under the weight of his pack. "I can't climb with this," he panted, his face red. "I'll lose my footing."

"Drop the pack, Harrison," Yazzie called down. "We only carry water and tools. We don't have room for luxury."

"No!" Harrison snapped, clutching the leather bag. "These are priceless. Ancestral pottery from the lower canyon ruins. I salvaged them before the collectors vandalized the site. I'm not leaving them in the sand."

"They're rocks, Harrison," Nadia said, her voice tight. "They won't keep the children alive. Drop them or stay below."

Harrison grumbled but strapped the pack tight, crawling up the hand-holds with slow, clumsy movements. He reached the ledge, immediately sitting in the back of a stone room, shielding his pack.

The ruins were cool and silent. The air inside the stone rooms was fresh, smelling of dry dust and old clay. At the back of the alcove, a slow drip of water fell from the sandstone ceiling into a small, stone-carved basin. Clara immediately ran to the basin, cupping the cool water in her hands. 

"Limit them to a cup each, Nadia," Yazzie said, setting down his pack. "We have to let the basin refill."

As Nadia managed the children, Yazzie walked to the edge of the stone ledge, looking down at the canyon wash. 

The heat waves were shimmering off the sand. Below, three figures stood motionless in the shadow of a massive sandstone boulder. They were former tourists, their skin shriveled and dark, their clothes faded by the sun. Because they were dehydrated, they didn't produce sweat or movement in the midday heat, standing like dry statues. 

Suddenly, Clara dropped her metal water cup. 

*CLANG.*

The sharp, metallic ring echoed off the canyon walls, a loud note that carried for miles in the dry air. 

In the wash below, the three dry statues twitched. Their heads snapped up toward the ledge. Their joints crackled as they began to shuffle forward, their leathery hands reaching out.

## Chapter 3: Climax & Escape

"They're coming," Yazzie said, grabbing his ranger axe. "Nadia, get the kids into the back room—the kiva. The entrance is a hatch in the floor. It’s easily blocked."

Nadia guided the children down the wooden ladder into the circular underground kiva, sliding the heavy stone cover over the hatch. 

Yazzie stood at the top of the cliff trail, where the narrow hand-holds met the ledge. 

The first of the mummified dead—a man in a torn hiking shirt—had reached the trail. His dried, leathery fingers found grip in the stone holds, his stiff limbs climbing the wall with a slow, mechanical momentum. Because he was light and dry, he climbed with surprising agility, his bones clicking against the sandstone. 

"Go back," Yazzie muttered, raising the axe. 

As the zombie's head cleared the ledge, Yazzie drove the butt of the axe into its forehead. The skull cracked like a dry gourd, and the corpse fell back, tumbling fifty feet into the sandy wash below. 

But the second zombie—a woman with shriveled, gray skin—reached the ledge from the side, her claw-like hand grabbing the leg of Yazzie's trousers. Her grip was like frozen leather, unyielding. 

Yazzie fell to one knee, his boot sliding toward the edge of the cliff. "Harrison! Help me with the line!"

Harrison sat in the corner, clutching his bag of pottery. "I can't... my hands are full! If I let go, the jars will break!"

"Harrison, he's going over!" Nadia screamed, running out of the kiva entrance. 

She looked for a weapon, her eyes landing on a heavy stone metate—a grinding stone used by the ancient inhabitants to grind corn. She grabbed the heavy block, hoisting it with both hands, and slammed the stone down onto the zombie's wrist. 

The dried bone snapped, and the grip released. The mummified corpse slid off the ledge, falling into the wash. 

Yazzie scrambled back, gasping for air. The third zombie was already at the top of the hand-holds, its dry hand grabbing the ledge. 

Yazzie grabbed Harrison's heavy leather pack, tearing it from the collector's shoulders. 

"No! My pottery!" Harrison screamed, lunging for the bag. 

Yazzie ignored him, throwing the heavy leather pack directly over the edge onto the climbing zombie's head. The pack, loaded with fifty pounds of stone-hard clay pots, struck the zombie's chest. The impact broke the mummy's grip, and both the zombie and the bag of priceless artifacts tumbled down the cliff, the pots smashing into a thousand pieces on the rocks below. 

Yazzie quickly pushed a heavy stone block over the edge, blocking the top of the hand-holds. 

The third zombie was trapped on the wall, unable to climb over the stone block. It stood on the narrow trail, its jaw clicking slowly. 

Yazzie sat on the stone floor of the ruins, looking out at the red canyon. The sun was setting, painting the cliffs in gold and crimson, and the air was beginning to cool. 

Inside the kiva, the children were safe and quiet, drinking the cool spring water. 

Harrison sat in the corner, staring at the empty cliff edge, his hands trembling. "My collection... gone."

"You're alive, Harrison," Yazzie said, his voice quiet. "That's the only collection that matters today."

"The old ones still protect us, Yazzie," Nadia said, looking at the stone walls that had sheltered their ancestors. 

They sat in the quiet ruins, the stone releasing its trapped warmth, while the mummified dead remained on the cliff face under the desert stars, unable to reach the living.

# 03. The Highway Siding

## Chapter 1: Setup

Interstate 40 was a metal graveyard stretching through the red-sand flats of the Mojave Desert, a long, shimmering line of abandoned cars, jackknifed semi-trucks, and melted RVs that had sat under the sun for three months. The heat waves rose from the cracked black asphalt in thick, warping sheets, making the distant blue mountains look like pools of liquid mercury. The air was dry and still, carrying only the faint scent of scorched rubber, hot iron, and blowing alkali dust. In this wilderness of abandoned transit, the sun was the absolute ruler, melting tires, cracking dashboards, and baking the interiors of stranded vehicles to lethal temperatures. Every metal surface was a branding iron, and the glare off the glass windshields was enough to cause temporary blindness without dark goggles.

Ramon, Asha, and a local driver named Rita stood in the narrow, flickering shadow of a massive, jackknifed semi-truck that blocked the eastern lanes. Rita was a thirty-five-year-old truck driver of mixed Diné heritage who knew how to hotwire diesel engines, bypass electronic trailer locks, and navigate the abandoned highway lanes. She carried a heavy canvas bag of tools, a plastic siphon hose, and wore grease-stained leather work gloves. Hanging from her shoulder was an old, well-oiled heavy-duty mechanic's wrench. Beside her stood Ramon, checking his tools, and Asha, holding her crowbar.

They were searching for two critical supplies: diesel fuel from the truck’s dual side tanks, and the cargo listed on the shipping manifest pasted to the driver's side door—a pallet of commercial rock-salt blocks used for cattle ranches. In the desert heat, without refrigeration, salt was their only means of preserving whatever wild meat they hunted or harvested in the hills. Without it, their winter stores would rot within days, leaving the mountainside commune to starve when the snows finally blocked the passes.

Rita climbed into the sweltering cab of the truck, where the air was easily sixty degrees. She opened the glove box and pulled out a heavy leather binder. It was filled with venture capital contracts, bank bonds, and stacks of high-denomination paper bills. She snorted, tearing out a handful of the thick bond papers.

"Look at this," Rita said, using a stack of worthless stock certificates to wipe a thick layer of grease and grit off her siphon hose. "Ten thousand dollars in treasury bonds. Now it's just a grease rag. The ink doesn't even smear properly."

Asha took a few of the dry, crisp paper bills and folded them, stuffing them into the pocket of her work trousers. "We can use them as shims to keep the salt blocks from shifting and cracking on the handcart. Better than letting them rattle to pieces on the gravel trails back to the canyon."

Ramon nodded, focusing on the task. "The truck is a refrigerated rig, Rita. The reefer motor is dead, of course, but the fuel tanks are half-full. That’s fifty gallons of clean diesel we can use for the camp generator. If we get this back, the shelter will have power for another week, and we can keep the water filters running."

"The trailer doors are sealed with a high-security shipping bolt," Rita said, climbing onto the ICC bumper at the rear of the rig. She adjusted her grip on a pair of heavy bolt cutters, her back muscles straining as she squeezed the handles. The metal bolt sheared with a loud, metallic *snap* that echoed off the empty highway. "Let's see if the cargo is intact, or if the heat got to it."

"The refrigeration failed months ago, Rita," Asha said, her hand resting on her crowbar as she scanned the surrounding dunes. "It’s been a steel box sitting in the fifty-degree sun. Anything inside is going to be baked."

Rita grabbed the rusted locking bar, throwing the heavy double doors open. A wave of hot, dry, mineral-laden air rushed out of the dark interior, smelling of dry salt, old iron, and the unmistakable, dusty scent of mummified decay.

## Chapter 2: Escalation

They shined their flashlights into the dark, cavernous trailer. The interior was lined with pallets of pink, rectangular salt blocks, stacked five feet high. But the cargo was not the only thing inside. 

Crowded into the far end of the trailer, huddled on the wooden pallets, were fifteen bodies. They were wealthy urban refugees who had boarded the sealed trailer in a desperate attempt to escape the cities during the initial panic, paying the driver with currency that had already lost its value, only to be abandoned when the truck jackknifed and the driver fled into the desert. 

The heat inside the steel container had risen to seventy degrees under the direct sun, and the massive load of rock-salt blocks had acted as a giant desiccant, drawing all the moisture from the air and their tissues. The bodies were completely mummified. Their skin was a dry, leathery gray-brown, stretched tight over their ribs and skulls, their designer clothes stiff with salt crust and dried sweat. They looked like statues carved from clay, their features preserved by the dry, mineral-rich environment. 

Asha shined her light near the floor. Discarded wallets, designer purses, and piles of hundred-dollar bills were scattered like dry leaves among the salt dust. Several stacks of bills had been wedged under the wooden pallets to level them out against the corrugated steel floor. The ultimate symbol of pre-collapse wealth had been reduced to literal shims for wooden cargo crates, while gold rings and high-end watches hung loosely on shriveled, leathery wrists.

"They're salt-cured," Ramon whispered, his face pale in the flashlight beam. "The salt dried them out before they could rot. In this environment, they don't decompose. The heat and the salt did the work of an embalmer."

"We need those salt blocks," Rita said, her voice tight but practical. She stepped into the trailer, the dry salt crunching under her boots. "We don't have a choice. We pull the blocks from the front pallets. We don't touch the back, and we don't look at them."

They began hauling the heavy, forty-pound salt blocks out of the trailer, loading them onto their handcart. The blocks were dry and rough, scratching their leather gloves and leaving white salt dust on their clothes. The physical exertion in the hot trailer made their breathing heavy, their sweat dripping onto the dry wood and immediately evaporating in the desiccated air. Their throats felt dry, like sand, and their muscles began to cramp from the sudden loss of hydration.

But as the fresh, hot air and light entered the trailer, the mummified dead began to twitch. The sudden shift in temperature and the vibration of the blocks being moved triggered their vestigial reflexes. 

The joints of a dry corpse in the front row—a man in a faded work shirt—cracked like breaking twigs. His head turned slowly, his empty, salt-crusted eye sockets locking onto Rita. He let out a dry, rattling gurgle, his leather-like fingers clawing at the salt block beside him. He moved with a slow, mechanical stiffness, his dried-out muscle fibers straining against the calcified joints. There was no speed in him, only a mindless, clumsy persistence. Slowly, the other figures on the pallets began to stir, their joints creaking in a dry chorus of decay.

## Chapter 3: Climax & Escape

"They're moving!" Asha warned, backing out of the trailer and raising her crowbar. 

The mummified dead began to shuffle forward, their dry feet scraping against the corrugated steel floor of the trailer. Because they were dehydrated and light, they moved with a stiff, jerky speed, their limbs swinging like dry wood. The salt had hardened their joints, but their grip remained an iron vise. They never ran, nor did they speak; they simply advanced in a sluggish, relentless line, drawn by the vibrations of the living.

One of the mummies—a former highway patrol officer in a shriveled uniform—lunged toward Rita, his blue, salt-crusted fingers grabbing the collar of her shirt. His grip was a dry clamp, locked by the salt, pulling her toward the dark end of the trailer.

"Rita!" Ramon shouted. 

He lunged forward, driving his shovel blade down onto the zombie's wrist. The dry bone snapped with a loud crack, and the grip released, the severed hand remaining pinned to Rita's collar. 

Asha used her crowbar to strike the second mummy, a woman in a shriveled dress, pushing her back into the salt pallets. The mummy fell, her leather-like skin absorbing the blow, but she immediately began to crawl forward on her hands and knees, her joints crackling with every movement.

"We have six blocks! That's enough!" Rita yelled, scrambling off the trailer bumper onto the asphalt. "Close the doors!"

Together, Ramon and Rita grabbed the heavy steel doors of the trailer, slamming them shut against the frame. The latches clicked, and they threw the iron lock bar down, securing the mummified dead back inside the dark steel box. 

Outside, they heard the dry, muffled *thump-thump* of the mummies slamming against the metal doors from the inside, their fingers scraping uselessly against the steel.

*Scritch. Scrape.*

"Let's get the diesel," Rita said, her hands shaking as she inserted the plastic siphon hose into the truck's fuel tank. She sucked on the tube, her mouth turning bitter as the green fuel rushed down the line. She spat the chemical taste onto the dirt and guided the flow into their jerrycans.

They filled their plastic jerrycans with the green fuel, the smell of the diesel mixing with the dry salt air. They loaded the cans and the pink salt blocks onto the truck bed, securing them with straps, using the crumpled stock certificates to pad the contact points between the heavy straps and the salt. 

They drove away down the empty, sun-baked highway, the utility truck’s motor humming as the heat waves shimmered off the asphalt behind them. 

Through the rear mirror, Ramon watched the jackknifed semi-truck fade into the distance, a steel tomb sitting under the white-hot Mojave sky, its silent, salt-cured cargo locked in the dark.

"We got the fuel, Rita," Asha said, her breathing settling. 

"Yes," Rita said, looking at the road ahead. "And the salt. The camp keeps its meat."

They drove back to the foothills, leaving the highway and the mummified dead behind in the shimmering heat.

# 04. The Ghost Town Saloon

## Chapter 1: Setup

The ghost town of Goldfield sat under a copper sky like a bleached skeleton, its wooden boardwalks and false-front buildings drying to the point of splintering. Nestled at the rugged base of the Superstition Mountains, the red dirt was dry as ash, swirling in lazy columns whenever the desert wind breathed across the flats. The midday air was a heavy, shimmering wall of heat, registering a brutal one hundred and fifteen degrees. It made the jagged peaks of the mountains look like liquid copper through the glare. Inside the old wooden saloon, the air was hot, stagnant, and thick with the scents of dry pine, stale beer, old whiskey, and fine desert dust. The double swing doors had been boarded shut with heavy pine planks to keep the sun out, and the high-backed bar offered a fragile shield against the heat.

Diego, a forty-year-old mechanic with grease permanently embedded in the creases of his knuckles, adjusted the heavy straps of his leather welding apron. His tools of survival were simple, practical, and worn from use: a heavy iron pry bar and a portable propane torch with a brass valve and a steel nozzle. Beside him was Aria, a twenty-five-year-old local historian who had spent years mapping the historical mines of the region. She was currently wrapping a dry linen cloth around her mouth and nose, protecting her lungs from the fine red dust that filtered through the building's joints. 

Aria pointed to the heavy iron grate in the floor behind the bar, which had been used for ventilation in the late nineteenth century. "The saloon is built directly over the main shaft of the old Mammoth Mine, Diego," she whispered, her voice dry and hushed. "The mine shafts run like a labyrinth under this entire street. When the heat hit and the dead began to rise, the tourists and the wealthy resort owners ran down into the tunnels to escape the sun. It’s cool down there, but there was no exit when the automated gates locked and the generators failed."

Diego looked down at the brass cash register behind the bar. It had been pried open long ago, its green paper money spilling out onto the floorboards, ignored and worthless. The wealthy executives from Phoenix had arrived in their luxury SUVs, carrying suitcases of cash and gold credit cards, trying to buy their way into the deep, air-conditioned shafts. Diego had watched them offer thousands of dollars for a single gallon of water, only to be turned away by the working-class locals who knew that paper could not quench thirst. Now, the SUV batteries were dead, the owners were gone, and the paper money lay in the dirt like autumn leaves.

"And they turned," Diego said, his hand resting on the heavy iron pry bar. 

"Yes," Aria said, taking a tiny, measured sip from her canvas-wrapped canteen, counting the drops to conserve every ounce of water. "The air in the shafts is dry as bone. It’s kept them from rotting. They’re like paper now, mummified by the draft. If they find a way up, they won't be like the wet, soft dead from the coast. They’ll be tough."

"We just need to wait out the midday heat," Diego said, wiping a thick line of sweat from his forehead. "Once the sun drops, we can make a run for the truck. But we have to stay quiet. The wood of this building is dry and brittle. It acts like a drum, echoing every step down into the shafts below."

## Chapter 2: Escalation

They sat in the deep shadows behind the bar, trying to preserve their energy and their precious water. The afternoon heat was a heavy, physical weight pressing down on the roof of the saloon, making the dry pine timbers groan and creak as they expanded under the blistering sun. Every sound seemed magnified in the silence of the ghost town. They could hear the distant, dry rattle of cicadas in the mesquite bushes outside, and the occasional hiss of sand blowing against the wooden exterior walls.

Suddenly, a strange, rhythmic sound rose from the floorboards beneath their feet.
