# The Dead and the Signal
## Cold War Analog Terror
### By Gabriel Simon

---




# Story 1: The Traffic Chopper

## Chapter 1: Pre-Flight Check
The pre-dawn air hanging over Metro Executive Airport was thick and stagnant, carrying a greasy, chemical tang from the shipping channels five miles south. Aaron Vance adjusted his shoulder harness, the rough nylon strap biting through his pilot's shirt and rubbing against the damp skin of his collarbone. The Robinson R44 sat motionless on the tarmac, its long rotor blades drooping in the humid dark like the wings of a exhausted, predatory bird. He clicked his flashlight on, the beam cutting through the oily mist to illuminate the pre-flight checklist. The yellow light reflected off the gauges, showing the fuel tank was topped. He patted the fuselage, the cold aluminum slick with condensation. 

"Sky-Eye One, fuel is topped, pre-flight check complete," Aaron spoke into his throat mic, his voice dry and flat in his headset. He tested the cyclic and collective controls, feeling the smooth, hydraulic resistance of the linkages. Below him, the police scanner hummed with a low, continuous static, though the usual morning rhythm was off. There were no reports of minor fender-benders or speeding motorists. Instead, the dispatcher's voice sounded tight, brittle, routing multiple emergency units to a "residential disturbance" on the East Side, her voice catching on words like *bite* and *unresponsive*. 

Aaron ignored the undercurrent of unease. In this job, you kept your eyes on the altimeter and left the ground trouble to the patrolmen. He turned the key. The starter let out a high-pitched, metallic whine, then the Lycoming engine caught with a deafening, throaty roar that rattled the cabin's plexiglass bubble. The rotor blades began their slow, sweeping arc, slicing the humid air with a rhythmic *whup-whup-whup* that vibrated in the soles of his boots. As the engine RPM needle climbed into the green arc, Aaron pulled the collective, and Sky-Eye One lifted off the ground, its nose dipping slightly as he swept toward the rising sun and the sleeping city skyline.

## Chapter 2: The Gridlock
By 07:15, the sun was a copper coin hanging in the grey industrial smog, casting long, dirty shadows across the concrete sprawl below. Aaron hovered the R44 at 1,200 feet directly above the intersection of Interstate 95 and the Downtown Bypass. Normally, this corridor was a slow, pulsing artery of red brake lights and white headlights. Today, it looked like a metal graveyard. The traffic wasn't slow; it was dead. Thousands of vehicles sat bumper-to-bumper, their exhaust pipes spewing thick plumes of gray carbon monoxide that blended into a shimmering, toxic lake of heat and gas.

"Sky-Eye One to News Nine Desk," Aaron said, adjusting the headset cup against his ear. "We’ve got a total standstill on I-95 Northbound, stretching all the way back to Exit 14. It’s an absolute lock. I'm not seeing any emergency response vehicles, which is highly unusual for a backup of this scale. The secondary lanes are also beginning to freeze as drivers try to exit. If you're heading toward the city, turn back."

Through the zoom lens of the chopper's nose-mounted camera, Aaron watched the scene on the asphalt below. The doors of several vehicles were opening. Drivers were stepping out onto the hot concrete, shielding their eyes as they looked toward the head of the backup. There was no movement, no creeping forward. Just a quiet, simmering panic. The metallic heat rising from the lanes was visible, warping the air above the roofs of the cars. It looked like a trap—a long concrete cage designed to keep these people pinned.

## Chapter 3: The First Signal
The police band scanner on the console suddenly flared, overriding the quiet drone of the Lycoming engine with a blast of raw, unedited static. It wasn't the usual dry codes or coordinate listings. It was the sound of a patrolman screaming, his breath rattling in his throat, the background noise filled with the wail of a siren and the flat, rhythmic crack of gunfire.

"All units, all units, we have... we have multiple pedestrians on the roadway at Mile Marker 12!" the patrolman shrieked, his voice cracking with pure terror. "They're... they're attacking the motorists! People are abandoning their vehicles, running into the lanes. Requesting immediate tactical support and EMTs! We are being overrun!"

"Unit 4-Delta, copy," the dispatcher's voice replied, though her professional calm was gone, replaced by a thin, trembling panic. "We have no units to dispatch to your location. All precinct personnel are currently deployed to the downtown medical centers. Secure your vehicle and retreat if possible."

Aaron leaned forward, his harness straining as he stared through the chin bubble of the helicopter. Mile Marker 12 was directly below him, a concrete cloverleaf where the highway ramp curved toward the local avenues. Through the camera feed on his monitor, he saw a woman in a blue sedan open her door, screaming. A man in a dark, dirt-smeared business suit walked toward her, his body stiff, his head tilted at an unnatural, broken angle. The woman reached out to him, but the man lunged, throwing her onto the concrete divider with a sickening, heavy thud.

## Chapter 4: The Outbreak from Above
From his vantage point at 1,000 feet, Aaron watched the infection spread like ink soaking into a dry paper towel. The man in the suit did not stop at the woman. He rose, his mouth smeared with a dark, glistening red, and stumbled toward the next vehicle, his fingers scratching against the passenger window. And the woman he had just thrown down—she was already moving again. She pulled herself up, her movements jerky and mechanical, and joined the suit, her head hanging limp against her shoulder as she walked.

"News Nine, are you receiving this feed?" Aaron barked into his mic, his heart hammering against his ribs, the taste of copper rising in his throat. "This isn't an accident! I repeat, this is not a traffic accident! People are attacking each other in the lanes. They are... they are tearing motorists out of their cars. The gridlock—it's a trap! If you're on the highway, you're boxed in. Abandon your vehicles! Run for the tree line!"

He didn't know if the station was still broadcasting his words, or if they had cut the signal to avoid showing the horror. The scanner was a solid wall of noise now: sirens, screams, and the high-pitched shriek of tires. The Interstate below had turned into a slaughterhouse, a grey strip of concrete covered in running figures, abandoned cars, and swarming packs of the dead.

## Chapter 5: Engine Trouble
Without warning, a sharp, metallic *clink-clink* echoed through the cabin, followed by a violent, shuddering vibration that made the instrument panel blur. The rotor RPM gauge flared yellow, then dipped deep into the red. The Lycoming engine, which had been a steady, comforting roar, coughed once, spat a cloud of black smoke from the exhaust, and died, leaving only the wind screaming through the vents.

"What the..." Aaron muttered, his hands moving instantly, his mind locking into emergency training. He slammed the collective stick down to preserve the rotor's rotational energy, his heart dropping into his stomach. The cabin was suddenly filled with the high-pitched, warbling wail of the low-RPM audio warning, a scream of electronics that signaled imminent disaster.

He glanced out the side window. Down on the highway ramp, a man was standing on the roof of a pickup truck, firing a hunting rifle wildly into the air to keep a shambling crowd away from his family. One of those stray, panicked rounds had just passed through the R44’s thin engine cowling, severing the fuel line. The silence inside the cabin was immediate and terrifying, broken only by the rush of the wind past the plexiglass and the slow, heavy *thump-thump-thump* of the unpowered rotor blades.

## Chapter 6: Mayday
"Mayday, Mayday, Mayday. Sky-Eye One is going down," Aaron shouted into the emergency aviation frequency, his voice tight, his throat dry. "Engine failure over the I-95 interchange. We have lost power. Entering auto-rotation. Descent rate is nine hundred feet per minute."

"Sky-Eye One, this is Metro Tower, copy Mayday," the controller's voice crackled through his headset, tense but controlled. "We have lost radar contact. Can you glide toward the runway?"

"Negative, Metro," Aaron gritted out, his eyes locked on the altimeter. The wind was rushing past the cabin, spinning the unpowered rotors, keeping them moving just fast enough to slow his fall. But he was descending at a steep, unforgiving angle. "I’m directly over the highway. The gridlock is total. Thousands of people are trapped in their vehicles below me. If I drop this thing on the asphalt, I'm going to plow straight into them. There's no room."

The windshield framed a terrifying view of the highway below—a solid mass of metal, running children, and the dark, swarming shapes of the dead. If he crashed on the concrete, the R44's fuel tank would rupture, turning the trapped lanes into a firestorm.

## Chapter 7: The Choice
The ground was rising at a terrifying speed. Aaron had less than twenty seconds before impact. His hands flew over the controls, fighting the R44's natural instinct to roll, keeping the nose aligned with the wind. 

"Sky-Eye One, you have a clearance to the west," the tower controller's voice squealed, the signal beginning to break. "Try to glide toward the industrial park. There's an empty lot near the warehouse."

"Too far," Aaron said, his jaw aching from how hard he was clenching his teeth. He looked down through the chin bubble. The highway ramp was crowded with people running on foot, their faces pale circles in the morning light. Directly ahead of him was the massive concrete overpass of the Bypass. If he hit the overpass, he’d kill himself and dozens of people hiding beneath it. To his left was a yellow school bus, surrounded by a crowd of the dead. To his right was the gridlock.

Every path ended in blood. Except one. Just past the highway ramp, nestled in the arm of the cloverleaf interchange, was a small, green patch of grass—Memorial Park. It was empty of people, though a few shambling figures were wandering near the benches.

## Chapter 8: The Park
"I'm steering for the park," Aaron announced, his voice dropping into a quiet, calm register as the decision took hold. "I'm going to drop it in the clearing near the pond. Metro... tell my family..." He choked on the words, then cleared his throat. "Tell them I made the landing."

He pulled the cyclic stick back slightly, trading his altitude for forward speed, aiming the nose of the descending helicopter toward the green canopy. The trees of Memorial Park rushed up to meet him like green fists, their branches clawing at the sky. 

"Copy, Sky-Eye One," the controller's voice whispered, the professionalism cracking. "Godspeed, Aaron."

Aaron ignored the radio. His world had shrunk to the instrument panel, the cyclic in his right hand, and the small patch of green grass between the trees. The wind screamed through the cabin vents, a high-pitched whistle that mixed with the slow, heavy beat of the dying blades.

## Chapter 9: The Descent
Ten seconds. The R44 cleared the metal guardrail of the highway ramp by mere feet, the skids almost brushing the roof of an abandoned sedan. Aaron could see the individual faces of the people below—some looking up in terror at the silent, descending shadow of the helicopter, others too busy fighting for their lives to notice the machine.

He adjusted the tail rotor pedals, keeping the nose straight, fighting the crosswind that threatened to spin the light aircraft. The trees of Memorial Park loomed, their branches scraping the bottom of the cabin. The pond was a flat, grey mirror directly ahead. He needed to flare—to pull the nose up at the last second, converting his forward speed into lift to cushion the impact before touching down.

"Keep it together," he muttered. The low-RPM warning in the cockpit was a continuous, deafening shriek. The altitude indicator spun toward zero. He gripped the collective, waiting for the perfect moment. Now.

## Chapter 10: Impact
Aaron pulled the collective up hard, using the final, stored energy in the spinning blades. The helicopter's nose flared upward, the tail dipping. The descent halted for a fraction of a second, the R44 hovering suspended in the air just ten feet above the grass.

Then, the blades stalled. 

The helicopter dropped. The metal skids hit the soft earth of the park with a bone-jarring crash. The tail boom snapped, whipping forward and slicing through the cockpit glass. Aaron was thrown forward into the instrument panel, the world exploding in a shower of sparks, breaking plastic, and tearing metal. 

The radio console shattered, the wires sparking against the wet grass. The live microphone dangled from its cord, swinging over the crumpled seat. For a moment, there was only the sound of hissing steam, the dripping of fuel onto the grass, and the distant, echoing screams from the highway. 

Then, the scanner speaker hissed with a long, unbroken wave of static.

*Static.*


# Story 2: The Street Reporter

## Chapter 1: The Smoke Column
Marcus Reed stood on the cracked, oil-stained asphalt of Westview Avenue, the heavy, biting weight of the remote broadcast deck pulling hard at the frayed leather strap across his shoulder, cutting deep into his collarbone. Beside him, his veteran cameraman Toby was muttering under his breath, adjusting the focus ring on their heavy Sony shoulder rig, aiming the long, glass lens toward the green canopy of Memorial Park. Above the trees, a thick, twisting column of black, oily smoke was rising into the morning air, staining the bright blue sky with a greasy, dark smudge. The breeze carried the suffocating stench of burning synthetic fuel, unburned aviation gasoline, and melting rubber, mixed with a faint, disturbing hint of sweet copper that Marcus couldn't quite place.

"We're live in three, Marcus," Toby muttered, his eyes glued to the rubber-ringed viewfinder, his face slick with sweat. "The signal is bouncing clean to the station transmitter, but the audio board is showing some nasty spikes. Static's creeping in from the edges. Make it quick, the police scanner in the van is going absolutely nuts. It's a wall of sirens out there."

Marcus checked the foam windscreen on his wireless microphone, tapping it twice. The sound registered in his earpiece as a low, muffled thump, followed by a soft, persistent hiss of static. "This is Marcus Reed, reporting live for News Nine from the eastern edge of Memorial Park. Just moments ago, we witnessed the tragic, terrifying descent of Sky-Eye One. The Robinson R44 traffic helicopter, piloted by veteran Aaron Vance, appeared to suffer a catastrophic engine failure over the I-95 interchange. It auto-rotated into the park clearing, narrowly avoiding the gridlock lanes below."

## Chapter 2: The Approach
Marcus began walking toward the park entrance, gesturing with his free hand for Toby to keep the camera rolling and follow close. The wet morning grass brushed against their boots, leaving dark, oily streaks on the leather. The distant sound of the highway's sirens was a fading, echoing wail, but here in the park, the silence was heavy, thick, and suffocating. The air smelled of damp earth, hot machine oil, and ozone, a sharp contrast to the peaceful green surroundings.

"We are moving toward the crash site now," Marcus said, keeping his voice in the professional, measured cadence he had practiced for years, though his chest felt tight and his throat was dry. "As you can see behind me, the smoke is rising from the center of the park's main lawn. There are no emergency vehicles on the scene yet, which is highly unusual. We’re going to see if we can provide any assistance or get eyes on the wreckage to confirm the pilot's status."

They pushed through a dense line of low, wet pine trees, the sharp needles scratching at their faces and hands. Ahead, the clearing opened up, the grass blackened and scorched, small fires licking at the dirt. The R44 lay on its side, a crumpled, steaming heap of blue and white metal, the tail boom snapped like a twig, the rotor blades bent into grotesque angles. The smell of hot metal and raw fuel was overwhelming, making their eyes water. No sirens had reached the park yet, only the rhythmic hiss of escaping hydraulic fluid and the soft, crackling pop of burning paint. Marcus took a cautious step forward, his boot sinking into the soft, soot-covered earth, feeling the heat radiating from the broken fuselage. The silence of the park felt unnatural, almost predatory.

## Chapter 3: The Gathering
"Oh, Jesus," Toby whispered, lowering the camera for a split second, his face pale under his baseball cap, before raising the rig back to his shoulder. 

"The fuselage is heavily damaged," Marcus reported, his voice dropping an octave as he moved closer, the heat of the fire hitting his face in waves. "The cockpit is collapsed. We are calling out for Aaron, but there's no response from inside the cabin. But wait... there are people already in the park. We have civilians approaching the wreckage from the opposite side."

From the deep shadows of the park's picnic pavilion, three figures emerged. They walked slowly, their steps uneven and dragging, their bodies swaying. Marcus felt a chill run down his spine. They weren't running to help. Their arms hung limp at their sides, their heads tilted at odd, broken angles. One of them, a man in a torn business suit, had cold grey skin and cloudy, milky eyes that stared blankly ahead.

"Hey! We need medical help over here!" Marcus shouted toward them, lowering the microphone slightly. "The pilot is trapped! Do you have a phone? Call 911!"

The figures didn't answer. They didn't quicken their pace. They just kept shambling forward, their heavy boots dragging through the dirt, their fingers clawing at the air. The sound of scraping fingernails on the metal of the picnic tables was a dry, scratching noise. As they drew closer, Marcus could see split, dry flesh on their faces and hands, showing no blood, only a thick, dark fluid. A strange, low rattle began to rise from their chests, a collective groan that sounded like air escaping from old bellows. Toby took a step back, the camera shaking slightly on his shoulder, but Marcus stood his ground, driven by the reporter's instinct, though a cold dread was pooling in his stomach.

## Chapter 4: The Horror in the Clearing
More figures emerged from the tree line—five, ten, then a dozen, their clothes torn and stained with dark, greasy patches. They surrounded the crumpled cockpit like ants on a fallen piece of fruit. 

"We are... we are witnessing something highly unusual here," Marcus stammered into the mic, his professional veneer cracking as he backed up a step. "The crowd... they aren't helping. They are tearing at the broken plexiglass. They've... they've broken through the windshield. They are reaching inside the cabin."

Through the lens, Marcus saw a pale, blood-slicked hand reach out from the cockpit—Aaron Vance, still alive, weakly trying to push his attackers away. But the crowd swarmed over him, their bodies pressing against the metal. A woman in a torn yellow sundress leaned into the cabin, her head dipping, and when she pulled back, her mouth and chin were slick with a dark, glistening red. She began to chew, her eyes wide and blank.

"They're... oh my god, they're attacking the pilot!" Marcus screamed into the mic, his voice cracking. "They're eating him! They are literally tearing him apart! Toby, we have to get out of here! Toby!"

The sounds from the cockpit were horrific—wet, chewing noises and the metallic snaps of jaws closing on bone. The attackers showed no emotion, their faces blank, their jaws moving in a relentless, rhythmic grind. Marcus felt a wave of nausea hit him. The sweet copper smell of fresh blood filled the air, mixing with the stink of the burning fuel. The victim's screams were cut short, replaced by a wet, gurgling sound that died away into the crackle of the flames. The crowd didn't stop. They tore at the flesh with their bare hands, pulling wet, red strips from the wreckage, their faces smeared with dark fluid.

## Chapter 5: The Turn
Toby didn't respond. He had frozen, the heavy camera still resting on his shoulder, capturing the gruesome scene in high definition. 

Suddenly, the young man in the suit—the one from the highway—turned his head toward them. His eyes were milky, devoid of light, but his jaw worked in a wet, chewing motion. He let out a low, rattling hiss that carried across the clearing. 

"Toby, run!" Marcus yelled, grabbing the cameraman's sleeve and pulling him hard. 

The movement triggered them. The crowd at the helicopter turned in unison, their clumsy footsteps shifting direction, heading straight toward Marcus and Toby. From the highway ramp behind them, more shambling figures began to spill over the metal guardrail, their fingers clawing at the air. They were boxed in. Toby panicked, ripping the heavy camera off his shoulder and throwing it to the ground, before bolting toward the northern fence. Marcus, weighted down by the broadcast deck, could only run toward the street.

The camera lay on the grass, still transmitting, its lens focused on the grass and the approaching boots. Marcus could hear the heavy, scraping sound of feet behind him. The air seemed to grow colder, filled with the scent of rotting organic matter and wet concrete. The wailing sirens on the highway seemed to grow more frantic, but they were distant, offering no hope of rescue. Marcus struggled to keep his footing on the wet grass, the broadcast deck bouncing violently against his side. He could hear the sharp, metallic snap of teeth just yards behind him. The dead were moving with a clumsy but relentless momentum, their limbs stiff but driving forward. Every instinct screamed at him to drop the equipment, but the heavy strap was tangled around his jacket, trapping him in his own gear.

## Chapter 6: The Chase Begins
Marcus sprinted, the heavy leather strap of the broadcast deck slamming against his ribs, making his lungs burn. Behind him, the sound of dragging feet, sliding gravel, and low, guttural groans seemed close, too close. The air was thick with the scent of hot rubber and decay.

He held the wireless mic to his lips, his voice shaking with terror. "Nine Desk! Nine Desk! If you can hear me, do not send rescue units to the park! The crowd... they aren't protesters! They're... they're dead! They are killing anyone they catch! They're eating people!"

He burst through the park gates onto Westview Avenue. The street was chaos. Cars were abandoned in the middle of the road, doors wide open. A station wagon had plowed into a light pole, the radiator hissing steam, its horn blaring a continuous, mocking note that drowned out the sirens.

"Stay away from the Interstate!" Marcus screamed into the mic, his boots pounding the hard asphalt. "If you are in your cars, you are trapped! They're coming out of the park!"

The pavement was slick with dark fluid and oil. He saw a man lying near a stalled sedan, his throat torn out, his chest rising and falling in a final, weak spasm. Even as Marcus watched, the man's eyes flicked open—cloudy and white. The dead man began to push himself up from the concrete, his jaw clicking open and shut. Marcus scrambled past, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird. The blaring horn of the station wagon was deafening, a wall of sound that seemed to draw more of the creatures from the side streets. He could see their shadows stretching across the asphalt, closing in.

## Chapter 7: The Streets of Panic
Marcus looked back over his shoulder. The horde had spilled out of the park gates, a slow, grey tide that flowed around the abandoned cars, their fingers reaching. 

"Is anyone receiving this?" Marcus gasped, his heart hammering in his ears, the sweat stingy in his eyes. "The signal... I hope the signal is still live. The city is... it's falling apart. I see people being dragged down on the sidewalk. A police cruiser just drove past, it didn't stop. They're overrun."

He tried the door of a brick diner, but it was locked from the inside. A terrified face looked out at him from behind the glass before pulling the metal blinds shut. 

"Open the door! Please!" Marcus screamed, banging his fist on the glass, leaving a greasy handprint. 

A groan echoed from the alley next to the diner. Two more figures emerged, their fingers outstretched, nails black with dried blood. Marcus turned and ran down the side street, his breath coming in ragged gasps. The air was heavy with the smell of sweet copper and rotting garbage. The street was a graveyard of stalled vehicles. He saw a woman trapped in a minivan, screaming as three of the creatures shattered the side window, their scraping fingernails clawing at the glass until it gave way. He couldn't help her; he could only run, his feet heavy, his lungs burning with every breath. The static in his earpiece was growing louder, a rushing white noise that threatened to drown out his own voice. He felt a deep, visceral dread that there was no one left at the station to hear him, that the signal was falling into an empty void.

## Chapter 8: The Blind Alley
The side street was a maze of brick facades and fire escapes. Marcus, disoriented by the smoke and panic, turned right, hoping to loop back to the main boulevard. 

Instead, he found himself in a narrow, high-walled alley behind a department store. The walls were smooth, windowless brick, offering no escape. At the end of the alley was a ten-foot chain-link fence topped with spirals of rusted razor wire. It was a dead end.

"No, no, no," Marcus whispered, running to the fence. He grabbed the metal mesh, shaking it, but the gate was secured with a heavy iron padlock and chain that rattled mockingly. 

He turned around. The mouth of the alley was already narrowing. The first of his pursuers had reached the corner, their silhouettes blocking the bright morning light. More followed, filling the narrow passage with a wall of shambling flesh and clutching hands.

The shadows in the alley were long and cold. The creatures moved with a mechanical, unstoppable force. Marcus could hear the dragging boots and the scraping fingernails against the brick walls. He looked up at the fire escape, but the ladder was retracted, far out of reach. He was trapped in a brick tomb, the only exit blocked by a growing mass of the dead. The air in the narrow space was stagnant, smelling of old grease, wet concrete, and the unmistakable, sweet decay of the creatures. Their jaws clicked rhythmically, a dry, metallic sound that echoed off the brick walls. Marcus clutched the microphone, his knuckles turning white as he pressed his back against the cold fence, the razor wire scraping his jacket.

## Chapter 9: Trapped
Marcus backed up until his spine hit the cold, rough brick wall. The smell of the alley—decaying garbage, damp masonry, and old grease—seemed to intensify, mixing with the metallic copper scent of blood that drifted from the approaching crowd.

He looked at the wireless mic in his hand. The green LED light was still glowing. The transmitter on his hip was still warm, humming with power. 

"I'm in the alley behind the Montgomery Store on 4th Street," Marcus said, his voice dropping to a desperate whisper, his eyes locked on the front rank of the dead. "If anyone is listening... if the station is still broadcasting... I'm trapped. They've blocked the exit."

He watched them close the distance. A middle-aged woman in a nurse's uniform, her throat torn open, the skin hanging in grey flaps; a young teenager with a shattered arm that dangled uselessly. They moved slowly, but they didn't stop. Their skin was cold grey, and their eyes were cloudy and milky, showing no sign of thought. Marcus realized with a sickening dread that they didn't feel pain. He saw one with a massive hole in its chest, a dry puncture with slow black ooze, yet it kept walking. The stories on the scanner were true: destroying the brain was the only way to stop them. But he had no weapon, nothing but a plastic microphone and a heavy broadcast deck. The creatures were only yards away now, their shadows stretching out to touch his boots. The wail of the city's sirens had faded into a distant, background hum, leaving only the wet, chewing sounds of their working jaws and the scraping of their feet on the concrete.

## Chapter 10: The Final Transmission
The horde was ten feet away, their breath a wet, rattling hiss in the narrow alley. Marcus could see the yellowed teeth, the vacant, staring eyes, the dried blood caked around their mouths. 

"They don't feel pain," Marcus whispered into the microphone, his knuckles white around the casing. "I saw one get hit by a car, and it just got back up. You have to... you have to run. Don't look back. Don't try to help anyone who has been bitten. They turn. They all turn."

A hand reached out, grabbing the sleeve of his jacket. Marcus screamed, pulling back, but another hand clamped onto his shoulder, the fingers digging into his flesh with surprising, cold strength. The grip was like an iron vise, the skin of the hand split and dry, yet unyielding.

"Aim for the..." Marcus shrieked, his voice cutting off as he was pulled down into the shadows. 

The wireless microphone slipped from his fingers, falling onto the damp concrete. It bounced once, the foam windscreen soaking in a puddle of dirty water, before the audio signal dissolved into a harsh, roaring wave of static. The screen of the transmitter flickered and died as boots trampled it into the mud. The last thing Marcus registered was the sweet, copper taste of his own blood and the sound of teeth grinding against bone. The creatures swarmed over him, a mass of grey flesh and clicking jaws. The microphone lay in the dark water, capturing the wet chewing sounds for a brief second before the battery failed, leaving only the empty, crackling static of a dead channel.

*Static.*


# Story 3: The Military Embed

## Chapter 1: The Secure Order
Corporal Jack Reyes wiped the grease-mixed sweat from his stinging eyes, the heavy, stiff edge of his Kevlar helmet pressing uncomfortably against his brow, leaving a deep red welt. The back of the LMTV transport truck was suffocatingly hot, smelling of diesel exhaust, hot metal, and the sharp, sour scent of twenty nervous men packed shoulder-to-shoulder in the dark. He and the rest of the 116th Infantry had been mobilized six hours ago under Title 32 orders, ripped from their armory and thrown into an unfolding nightmare.

"Rule of engagement is simple, boys," Sergeant Hall had barked over the rattle of the tailgates as they loaded their magazines with live rounds. "Establish a perimeter around the downtown district. Use non-lethal force where possible. If fired upon, return fire. We're dealing with extreme civil unrest, riots, and mass hysteria. Keep your heads on straight."

But as the heavy truck rumbled down 4th Street, Jack looked out the canvas flap at the back and saw a landscape that looked nothing like a riot. There were no political signs, no angry slogans, no organized groups protesting. Just a chaotic, screaming mass of terrified people fleeing in one direction, and a silent, shambling horde tracking them, their movements slow, heavy, and mechanical. The air was thick with the black smoke of burning rubber, the sharp smell of unburned fuel, and the crackle of distant, sporadic gunfire that didn't sound like police weapons. Jack gripped his M4 carbine tighter, his knuckles turning white against the plastic handguard, feeling the cold vibration of the truck's engine shaking his teeth. He looked at the faces of the men beside him; they were young, pale, and terrified. The sergeant's voice was loud, but it lacked the confidence of a man who understood the enemy. The city was dying around them, and they were driving straight into its open maw.

## Chapter 2: Overrun
The LMTV stopped abruptly, the heavy tires screeching on the asphalt, throwing the men forward in a chaotic tangle of gear, helmets, and rifles. 

"Dismount! Dismount! Establish a line!" Hall yelled, dropping the heavy tailgate with a loud metallic clang that echoed off the brick buildings. 

Jack jumped down, his heavy boots hitting the hard pavement, his rifle raised to his shoulder. Immediately, the noise hit him—a deafening wall of sound. A crowd of fifty or sixty people was stumbling toward their line. They were dirty, covered in dark, sticky stains, and they did not scream or shout. 

"Hold the line! Warning shots! Fire in the air!" Hall barked, his voice tense. 

Jack fired three rounds into the grey, smoke-filled sky. The crack of the M4 carbine was loud, but the approaching crowd didn't even flinch. They kept walking, their eyes blank and milky, their arms outstretched. They hit the military barricade like a slow-motion wave of grey flesh. A soldier to Jack's left, Private Miller, was pulled over the hood of a humvee, his screams instantly cut short as the crowd swarmed him, their fingers tearing at his uniform and digging into his neck. Jack watched in horror as a woman in a torn dress sank her teeth into Miller's cheek, tearing away a chunk of flesh with a wet chewing sound. The air filled with the sweet copper smell of fresh blood and the metallic snaps of teeth. The warning shots had done nothing to deter them; they didn't even seem to register the noise. Jack fired into the chest of a man in front of him, but the man didn't fall. He just kept coming, his dead eyes fixed on Jack's chest, his fingers scraping against the metal of the humvee.

## Chapter 3: Retreat to the Alley
"Fall back! Fall back!" Sergeant Hall screamed, his voice breaking as he fired a three-round burst into the chest of an attacker. The man didn't fall. He just kept stumbling forward, his hands locking onto Hall's collar with a desperate, cold strength.

Jack saw the sergeant go down under a mass of writhing, grey-skinned bodies, the sound of tearing fabric and wet, chewing noises filling the air. The perimeter was gone. Soldiers were running in every direction, their training evaporating in the face of the impossible. The radio in Jack's ear was a chaotic mess of screaming and static.

Jack turned and ran, the heavy plates of his body armor slapping against his chest, making every breath a struggle. He didn't look back. The street was a gauntlet of abandoned vehicles, hissing radiators, and clutching hands. He saw an opening—a narrow alleyway between two brick buildings. He ducked into it, hoping to find a path to the next block, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird. He ran past a stalled police cruiser, its siren wailing a broken, stuttering note, its windshield shattered. A creature with split, dry skin and a missing arm lunged at him from behind the car, its teeth snapping shut inches from his sleeve. Jack shoved it away, the flesh of its shoulder feeling cold and stiff, like raw meat left in a freezer. He threw himself into the alley, his boots splashing through puddles of greasy water. The sound of the horde behind him was a chorus of scraping boots and low, rattling groans that seemed to multiply in the narrow space.

## Chapter 4: The Discovery
The alley was narrow and dark, shaded by the high, windowless brick walls of the Montgomery Store. Jack ran twenty paces before realizing it was a dead end. A ten-foot chain-link fence topped with spirals of rusted razor wire blocked the exit.

He stopped, chest heaving, his rifle shaking in his hands. He looked around for another way out, the brick walls closing in on him like the walls of a tomb. The air was stagnant, smelling of old garbage, wet concrete, and decay.

On the ground near the base of the fence, half-submerged in a puddle of muddy water, was a pile of black gear. It was a professional video camera, shattered and broken, its lens cracked. Beside it lay a heavy leather bag with a glowing green LED. Next to the bag lay a wireless microphone, its foam windscreen soaked but intact.

Jack picked it up, his tactical gloves slick with mud. He recognized the logo on the microphone: News Nine. The transmitter unit in the bag was still humming, its signal light blinking. The microphone was still live. Marcus Reed's final transmission had ended here, and the gear was still sending its signal to whatever receiver was left in the city. Jack felt a chill as he looked at the bloodstains on the concrete nearby, a dark, greasy pool that was already beginning to dry. He realized that whoever had been using this gear was gone, dragged away or worse. But the equipment was a lifeline. If the station was still broadcasting, if the signal was still reaching the outside world, this was his only chance to speak to someone, to warn them of what was happening. He slung his rifle, picked up the transmitter bag, and strapped it over his arm.

## Chapter 5: The Experiment
A shadow fell over the entrance of the alley.

Jack raised his rifle, aiming it at the figure that had just turned the corner, blocking the morning light. It was a man in a torn business suit, his face pale, smeared with dark fluid, his jaw working in a wet, chewing motion. He moved with a slow, mechanical stride, his eyes fixed on Jack.

"Stop! Military! Stand down!" Jack shouted, his voice echoing in the narrow brick space.

The man didn't stop. He didn't even slow down. His feet dragged on the asphalt, his fingers clawing at the air.

Jack squeezed the trigger. *Pop. Pop. Pop.* Three rounds of 5.56mm ammunition hit the man directly in the center of his chest. Jack saw the fabric of the suit rip, saw the dark entry wounds open up. The impact knocked the man back a step, but there was no cry of pain, no hesitation. The man simply regained his balance, his arms rising again. Jack stared in disbelief. In training, they were taught that three rounds of center-mass would stop any human target, causing massive systemic shock and blood loss. But this thing was different. There was no blood spurting from the punctures—only a slow, thick, dark ooze like grease. The man's eyes remained fixed on him, cloudy and vacant, showing no sign of pain or awareness. He stepped forward again, his boots dragging through the dirt, his jaw clicking shut with a dry, metallic snap that made Jack's skin crawl.

## Chapter 6: The Unstoppable
Jack lowered the rifle slightly, expecting the man to collapse, to bleed out, to cry out. 

Instead, the man simply regained his balance. He didn't look down at his chest. He didn't check the wounds. There was no blood spurting from the punctures—only a slow, thick ooze like grease. He raised his hands again, his fingers curving into claws, and resumed his slow walk toward Jack.

"What are you?" Jack whispered, his throat dry, his heart hammering against his ribs.

He backed up until his pack hit the cold chain-link fence. The creature was ten feet away now. He could hear the wet, rattling breath in its throat, a low, gurgling sound that smelled of rot. He fired again, two rounds to the abdomen. The bullets passed right through, sparking against the brick wall behind him. The creature didn't even blink. It was like shooting a mannequin, a piece of meat that had no life but still moved. The creature's boots scraped on the concrete, a rhythmic, dragging sound that was getting closer. Jack felt a wave of sheer panic. He had plenty of ammunition, but if bullets did nothing to the body, he was as good as dead. He saw the entry wounds in the creature's chest, dry and black, showing no signs of life. The creature was a walking corpse, driven by some instinct that didn't rely on a heartbeat or blood flow. It was unstoppable, a force of nature dressed in a businessman's suit.

## Chapter 7: The Discovery of the Headshot
The creature was five feet away. The smell of it—like old meat left in the sun, mixed with wet concrete and ozone—filled Jack's nostrils, making him gag. He could see the yellowed teeth, the dry, cracked lips, and the cold grey skin of its neck.

In a final, desperate panic, Jack raised the muzzle of his rifle and aimed not for the chest, but for the center of the creature's forehead.

He squeezed the trigger once.

The crack of the rifle was deafening in the enclosed alley, the muzzle flash lighting up the brick walls. The bullet struck the creature directly between the eyes.

Instantly, the tension left the creature's body. The arms dropped. The legs buckled. The man in the suit fell forward onto the concrete like a sack of wet sand, his head hitting the ground with a dull thud. He did not move again. Jack stood frozen, his rifle still raised, his breath coming in ragged gasps. The silence returned to the alley, broken only by the hum of the News Nine transmitter in his bag. He looked at the fallen figure. No groans, no movement. A dark, thick pool of fluid began to seep from the forehead wound, but the body was completely still. The realization hit him with the force of a physical blow: the body was just a shell. The brain was the control center, the only part that mattered. Destroy the brain, and the machine stops.

## Chapter 8: The Broadcast Setup
Jack stared at the motionless body. He looked at his rifle, then back to the dead man. 

"The head," Jack whispered. "You have to hit the head."

He looked down at the wireless microphone in his left hand. The green light on the transmitter deck was still solid, humming with power. The news station's frequency was still open, the signal bouncing clean to the main transmitter. If they were still broadcasting, if anyone was still listening to the radio or TV, they needed to know. 

Jack safety-offed his rifle and pulled the microphone to his mouth. He cleared his throat, trying to stop his hands and voice from shaking. 

"This is Corporal Jack Reyes, 116th Infantry," he said, his voice echoing off the brick walls, sounding hollow and small. "If anyone can hear this... if the News Nine station is still online... listen to me. I have an emergency broadcast. This is not a drill. I repeat, this is not a drill." He adjusted the strap of the transmitter bag, making sure it didn't slip into the mud. He could hear the crackle of static in the background of the channel, a soft, rushing sound that felt like the breathing of the dying city. He knew he didn't have much time. The sirens were fading, replaced by the sounds of the dead. He had to deliver the message before the alley was overrun, before his own voice was cut off like the others.

## Chapter 9: The Warning
"We were ordered to shoot center-mass," Jack spoke into the foam windscreen, his voice growing louder, more urgent, filling the narrow space. "That is a mistake. I repeat, shooting them in the body does nothing. They do not bleed out. They do not feel pain. You can pump ten rounds into their chest and they will keep coming. They don't have a pulse, they don't need blood."

He glanced toward the alley entrance. Three more shadows had appeared, their shambling figures silhouetted against the streetlights, their arms outstretched. 

"You have to destroy the brain," Jack yelled into the microphone. "Aim for the head! Headshots are the only way to stop them! If you have a gun, don't waste your ammo on body shots. Aim for the forehead, the temple, the base of the skull. Destroy the brain, or they won't stop! They will just keep walking, even with their chests blown open!" He could hear the creatures approaching, their boots scraping on the gravel, their jaws clicking in anticipation. The smell of decay was getting stronger, a thick, sweet stench that seemed to cling to the walls. Jack pressed the microphone closer to his lips, his voice cracking with emotion. He wanted his family to hear this. He wanted his buddies to hear this. The old rules of engagement were dead. The new rule was survival, and it had only one bullet.

## Chapter 10: The Last Stand
The three new attackers were halfway down the alley now. Behind them, more were turning the corner, drawn by the sound of Jack's voice and the scent of living flesh. 

Jack raised his rifle with his right hand, keeping the microphone pressed to his lips with his left. 

"Spread the word," Jack pleaded into the mic. "Tell the police, tell the Guard, tell your families. Aim for the head. Destroy the brain. That is the only rule that works. Aim for the..."

A cold hand grabbed his boot through the chain-link fence. Jack kicked it away with a yell, firing a single round into the head of the closest shambler. It dropped. He fired again, dropping the second. 

His rifle clicked dry. 

Jack looked at the advancing wall of dead faces, their milky eyes staring, their mouths open. He pulled the microphone close one last time. "Aim for the head," he whispered, his voice trembling. 

The microphone was knocked from his grip as the wave hit him. The transmitter unit was crushed under a heavy, dragging boot, and the signal cut to *static*. The creatures swarmed over him, their cold hands tearing at his tactical vest, his skin, his neck. The pain was immediate and blinding, but it was quickly replaced by a numb coldness. The microphone lay on the ground, its foam screen soaked in fresh, sweet copper blood. The transmitter sparkled once, a tiny blue flame of shorting electronics, before it was stomped into the dirt, leaving only the empty, roaring hiss of static on the airwaves.

*Static.*


# Story 4: The News Anchor

## Chapter 1: The Last Man in the Studio
Arthur Pendelton adjusted his silk tie, his fingers steady despite the muffled screams filtering through the building's concrete walls. The main broadcast studio of News Nine, usually a humming hive of producers, teleprompter operators, and floor managers, was dead silent. The automated robotic cameras stood like silent, three-legged iron beasts, their green tally lights glowing in the dim, high-ceilinged room. The air was thick with the scent of floor wax, dust, and the warm, slightly metallic odor of electronic equipment left running. It was a space he had occupied for thirty-two years, a sanctuary of information now turned into a tomb of static. The rest of the staff had fled twenty minutes ago when the emergency sirens started their low, rhythmic wail. Arthur had stayed. He was sixty-two years old, and he had been the face of evening news in this city for three decades. He wasn't going to spend his final broadcast running in a parking lot. His desk, a massive arc of polished mahogany, was empty except for a single bottle of water, a stack of notes, and the digital clock counting down the seconds. Through the double-paned glass of the control room window, he could see the banks of monitors displaying empty corridors and static-filled channels. Arthur sat down in his heavy leather chair, the leather creaking under his weight. He knew the risks, but the drive to report, to stand as the last voice of order in a collapsing world, was stronger than the urge to survive. He reached forward and turned on his desk microphone, the red indicator light casting a warm glow over his hands.

## Chapter 2: The Raw Audio
A sharp burst of static hissed from the control room speakers, followed by a voice that made Arthur sit up straight. It was the raw feed from the remote deck Marcus Reed had taken to the park. The audio was muddy, filled with the roar of wind, the crack of gunfire, and the terrifying sound of screaming. But it was the voice of Corporal Jack Reyes that cut through the noise, his gasps sharp and desperate. "This is Corporal Jack Reyes, 116th Infantry," the voice gasped, competing with the crackle of gunfire and screaming. "If anyone can hear this... aim for the head! Center-mass shots do nothing! They do not bleed... You have to destroy the brain!" The transmission was cut short by a wet, tearing sound, then a final, definitive wave of static that filled the studio with a low, white noise. Arthur stared at the speaker, his heart hammer-beating against his ribs. He had known Marcus. He had mentored the kid. Now Marcus was gone, and this soldier had sacrificed his life to send a warning. The smell of ozone in the control room seemed to grow stronger, a physical reminder of the electronic signal that had just carried a man's death rattle. Arthur pulled a pad of paper toward him, his hand shaking slightly as he wrote down the words: *Aim for the head. Destroy the brain.* The white noise of the static continued to hiss from the speakers, a relentless, dry sound that filled the empty studio with a sense of impending doom.

## Chapter 3: The Anchor's Duty
Arthur pulled a stack of paper maps from his desk drawer—the official Civil Defense evacuation routes that had been filed away since the Cold War. He crossed out I-95 with a thick black marker, the ink smelling of raw alcohol. He wrote: *BLOCKED - DO NOT USE.* He looked at the master control monitor. The station's automated backup transmitter was still broadcasting a loop of pre-recorded emergency slides. He could override it. He could go live. "The public needs to know," Arthur said to the empty room, the sound of his voice hollow. "They're running straight into the teeth of it." He reached down and pulled a large, heavy roll of gray gaffer tape from the shelf beneath the desk. The adhesive had a strong, familiar smell of rubber and solvent, a scent that associated with late-night production fixes. But today, the tape would serve a different purpose. He looked at the heavy, steel-framed chair he sat in, which was bolted directly into the concrete floor beneath the carpet to keep the camera angles consistent. It was a sturdy anchor, a fixed point in a world that was rapidly turning to chaos. Arthur took the end of the tape, his eyes fixed on the door, his mind resolved. He would not run. He would stay here, at his post, and deliver the final message to the city that had trusted him for so many years.

## Chapter 4: The Roll of Tape
Arthur ripped the first strip of tape with a loud, tearing screech that echoed in the high-ceilinged studio. He pressed the end to the heavy chrome frame of his broadcast chair, then wrapped the tape tightly around his waist, binding himself to the seat. He tore another strip, wrapping it around his chest. The chair was a heavy, custom-built steel model, bolted directly into the concrete floor beneath the carpet to keep the camera angles consistent. Once taped in, he couldn't get up. He couldn't run even if his instincts begged him to. The tape pressed hard against his ribs, making it difficult to breathe, but it gave him a strange, comforting sense of stability. He smoothed down the edges of the gray adhesive, the smell of rubber strong in his nose. "If I'm going to do this," he whispered, tightening the tape until it compressed his chest, "I'm going to finish it." His hands were free, resting on the mahogany desk, ready to operate the switcher and hold his notes. He looked at the red digital clock on the wall; it was 8:15 AM. The sirens outside had grown louder, a continuous wail that was joined by the distant pop of small arms fire. Arthur checked his earpiece one last time, adjusting the volume. The line was silent, but the transmitter was active. He was ready.

## Chapter 5: The Glass Shivers
A distant, muffled crash echoed from the lobby downstairs. It was the sound of the station's heavy glass security doors shattering under a massive weight. Arthur heard the alarms begin to wail—a low, rhythmic pulsing that vibrated through the floorboards. On the security monitor at the corner of his desk, he saw the lobby filling with moving shapes. They weren't looting; they were simply walking forward, pushing past the broken metal frames of the turnstiles. The camera captured their gray, lifeless faces, their clothes stained with dark, sluggish grease. They moved slowly, their limbs stiff, but they didn't stop. "They're inside," Arthur noted, his voice flat, his fingers resting on the mouse. The sound of their feet on the stairs—a rhythmic, dragging scrape—began to echo down the hallway. Arthur reached for the master console switcher on the desk. He flipped the red toggle marked *AIR-OVERRIDE*. The automated cameras hummed, their lenses zooming in on his face, adjusting their focus. The red *ON AIR* light atop Camera One flared to life, casting a bright red glow on his mahogany desk. He cleared his throat, looked directly into the lens, and began to speak, his voice steady and calm.

## Chapter 6: Going Live
"Good morning," Arthur said, his voice dropping into the deep, authoritative cadence that had comforted the city through blizzards and blackouts. "This is Arthur Pendelton, broadcasting live from the News Nine studios. If you are watching this, please listen carefully. The civil unrest currently affecting our city is not a riot. We have confirmed reports that the attackers are deceased individuals who have reanimated. I repeat: the dead are walking." He paused, letting the words sink in. He didn't want to cause panic, but he needed them to believe him. "We have just received raw audio from the field. Standard defensive measures are ineffective. If you are forced to defend yourself, you must destroy the brain. Center-mass shots do not stop them. Aim for the head." Through the glass window of the control room, he saw the security monitors flicker, then go black one by one as the dead moved through the building, tearing at the wiring. The sound of their approach was growing louder—a wet, rattling hiss that seemed to fill the corridor just outside the studio doors. Arthur kept his eyes locked on the camera lens, refusing to look away, his voice remains steady and clear.

## Chapter 7: Explaining the Rules
Arthur clicked his mouse, bringing up the civil defense map on the broadcast screen behind him. "Do not attempt to use the interstate highways. The gridlock on I-95 has become a bottleneck, and we have reports of active outbreaks along the entire corridor. If you are in your vehicle on the highway, abandon it immediately and seek high ground or shelter." He heard a heavy thud from the hallway just outside the studio. It was the sound of something hitting the thick, soundproof double doors. The heavy brass handles began to shake, the metal mechanism clicking as something pushed against it from the other side. "The primary evacuation route is Route 7 West," Arthur continued, pointing to the screen. "It is currently clear, though we advise caution. Head toward the county fairgrounds. The National Guard is attempting to establish a secure perimeter there." The gaffer tape around his chest felt tighter now, a constricting band that reminded him of his choice. He could feel the vibration of the thumping through the mahogany desk, a rhythmic rattle that matched the pounding of his heart. He did not look at the door. He kept his focus on the lens, his hands flat on the desk.

## Chapter 8: Reading the Routes
*Thump. Thump.* The double doors rattled. Arthur didn't look at them. He kept his eyes locked on the camera lens. "If you cannot leave the city, seek shelter in buildings with reinforced steel doors or upper-level access. Pull up ladders. Block stairwells. Gather water and non-perishable food. Do not open your doors for anyone who has been bitten, regardless of who they are. The infection is fatal, and the deceased reanimate within minutes." The thumping grew louder, the sound of wood splintering under a relentless, unyielding pressure. The heavy oak panels of the door frame groaned, the brass latch clicking as the dead threw their weight against it. The smell of sweet copper and rotting flesh began to seep through the gap under the door, a heavy, nauseating scent that filled the air. Arthur's throat was dry, but he continued to read the emergency information, his voice rising to compete with the noise. "I repeat, the waterfront is unsafe. Do not attempt to reach the docks. Stay in your homes, secure your doors, and aim for the head if they break in."

## Chapter 9: The Buckling Doors
A loud *crack* echoed through the studio. The wood around the left door's lock had split. A pale, grey hand pushed through the gap, the fingers clawing uselessly at the air. The skin on the fingers was torn, showing dry, grey flesh underneath, with no blood dripping from the wounds. Arthur looked at the hand, then back to the lens. The gaffer tape around his chest felt like a metal band, keeping him pinned to the chair. His heart was racing, but his face remained composed. "If you have family members who are unaccounted for, do not go out to look for them. Stay where you are. Keep your radios tuned to this frequency. We will continue to broadcast as long as we have power." The door frame groaned again, a long, tearing sound of metal separating from wood. The hand in the gap was joined by another, the fingers gripping the splintered wood, pulling it apart. Arthur could see the face of a security guard through the opening—his eyes milky, his jaw clicking in a wet, chewing motion.

## Chapter 10: The Last Word
The double doors burst open with a shower of splintered wood. A dozen figures spilled into the brightly lit studio. They blinked, momentarily confused by the glare of the 10,000-watt studio lights. Then they heard Arthur's voice. They turned toward the anchor desk, their dragging steps accelerating as they smelled living flesh. Arthur didn't flinch. He looked straight into Camera One. "This is Arthur Pendelton, News Nine," he said, his voice steady to the very end. "We are going to static now. To those of you still listening... good luck, and may God help us all." A grey hand reached across the desk, knocking over the microphone. The automated camera caught a brief glimpse of a shadow falling over the desk before the video feed cut to a wall of gray static. The microphone bounced on the mahogany surface, the sound of wet biting and tearing fabric carrying over the airwaves for a final, horrific second before the transmitter unit was crushed under a heavy, blood-soaked boot.

*Static.*


# Story 5: The Happy Clown

Thomas Vance looked into the dressing room mirror, checking the red paint on his nose. He adjusted the oversized yellow collar of his jumpsuit, the fabric smelling of cheap laundry detergent and old sweat. For twelve years, he had been "Buster the Clown," the host of News Nine's morning kids' hour, a job he had taken to pay the bills but had grown to love. "Looking good, Buster," he told his reflection, forcing his lips into a wide, painted-on grin that felt increasingly heavy as the years went by. Outside the dressing room, the air in Studio B was filled with the excited chatter of twenty children. The sound system was isolated to his own studio, where the bright primary colors of the Funhouse set reflected in the children's eyes. Thomas felt a cold knot of dread tighten in his stomach. He knew what was happening. He had seen the early morning news reports before going into makeup. The parents of these children were down in the main lobby or in the parking lot, and if the crew was running, the lobby was likely gone. He looked at the twenty small, frightened faces looking up at him from the bleachers, their eyes wide with confusion. They were alone in this room, helpless. Thomas reached into his yellow pocket and pulled out three bright orange juggling balls. He tossed them into the air, his hands moving in a smooth, practiced arc. "Who wants to see Buster juggle with his eyes closed?" he asked, making a silly face. The kids cheered, their attention pulled back to the stage. Thomas kept the balls in the air, but his eyes wandered to the double doors at the back of the studio. Through the small glass windows of the doors, he saw shadows moving in the hallway—jerkily, slowly, pressed against the glass. The smell of sweet copper and old blood seemed to drift through the vents, a heavy, metallic scent that made his throat dry. He juggled faster, his heart racing, his hands slick with sweat. ## Chapter 6: The Glass Shivers
Above the bleachers was the VIP viewing gallery, a room separated from the studio by a large pane of plate glass. *Clack.* Thomas looked up. A spiderweb crack had appeared in the center of the glass, reflecting the bright studio lights. On the other side, in the dark gallery, a figure was leaning against the pane. The children continued to count, their eyes shut tight. "Four... five... six..." Thomas draped the scarf over his arm, trying to focus on the trick, trying to ignore the guard in the window. ## Chapter 8: The Shatter

Thomas yelled, his voice cracking as he stepped between the bleachers and the stage. He began to sing, his voice loud and theatrical, trying to drown out the sound of the glass. "Oh, Buster the Clown is in the town, he spins around and jumps up and down!" # Story 6: The Broadcast Tech

Kira stepped away from the gate, her heart heavy, and looked at Ben. They couldn't stay here; the hum of the intercom was still broadcasting their presence to the tunnels. They turned their backs on the locked grate and the blood trail, heading back toward the main subway track, leaving the bolted gate and the silent station behind them in the dark.

The fatal fever had taken them, and now they were reanimated, trapped in their restraints or wandering the ICU. In this new reality, the heart did not need to beat for the muscles to move. The corpse would rise, its skin cold and grey, its chest showing only dry punctures from useless medical needles. The open phone line carried the sounds of this living death across the hospital's empty circuits, a chilling reminder that the floors above were occupied by things that no longer slept, waiting in the dark for any sign of life.

## Chapter 5: The Welded Door
Julian hurried away from the triage desk, following the faded yellow signs marked *HELIPAD ACCESS*. He pushed open the heavy door to the concrete service stairs and began his ascent. The stairwell was a vertical tomb of poured concrete, channeling the chill from the upper levels. With every flight he climbed, the air grew colder and thinner, smelling of wet concrete, dust, and the faint tang of winter wind. His flashlight beam bounced off the gray walls, revealing old water stains and peeling paint. His boots made soft, rhythmic thuds on the steps, a sound he tried to muffle but which seemed to echo unnaturally in the narrow shaft. At the top of the stairs, the landing was plunged into near-total darkness, save for a single, low-wattage red exit light that glowed over the doors. The crimson light cast a bloody hue across the landing, making the shadows look like crouching figures. These were the heavy, fire-rated double steel doors that led directly to the roof helipad, the hospital’s primary evacuation point. Julian's heart hammered in his chest as he stood on the landing, his lungs burning from the rapid climb. The silence here was thick, broken only by the low whistle of the wind leaking through the door frames. He knew this was his only chance to escape the building if the lower levels were overrun, but the silence from the other side of the steel barrier felt ominous, a heavy weight pressing against the door. He stepped forward, his hand trembling as he reached toward the cold metal handle.

## Chapter 6: The Steel Barrier
The double doors were firmly closed, blocking the path to the outside air. Julian reached out and gripped the cold metal push bar, his fingers slick with sweat. Taking a deep breath, he threw his shoulder against the bar, expecting the heavy latch to click and swing open. Instead, the doors did not move. They felt entirely solid, as if they had been cast from concrete or bolted to the frame. Panic flared in his chest. He pushed again, harder this time, using all of his body weight, but the steel barrier remained absolutely motionless. He checked the latch mechanism; the panic bar depressed fully under his hands, and he could hear the internal mechanism clicking, indicating the latch itself was disengaged. Yet, the doors refused to budge even a millimeter. Julian stepped back, his breathing ragged, and shone his flashlight beam along the edges of the door frame. The light cut through the red glow of the exit sign, revealing the industrial gray paint of the doors. He traced the beam up the center seam where the two doors met, then along the hinges, looking for some kind of security chain or padlock. The air was dead, and the only sound was his own rapid inhalation. He noticed a strange silver discoloration along the outer edge of the frame, and a faint, chemical smell that didn't belong in a hospital—the sharp scent of burnt metal and industrial flux. He stepped closer, his flashlight focusing on the center of the double doors.

## Chapter 7: Welded from the Outside
Julian froze, his flashlight beam trembling as it illuminated the top section of the doors. A thick, rusted steel rebar pole had been laid horizontally across the double doors, welded directly to the reinforced steel frames. The weld beads were rough and bubbly, a silver scar across the dark metal. The smell of burnt flux, hot iron, and electrical slag was still strong in the air, indicating the work had been done recently. The doors had been welded shut, sealed with industrial permanence. But as Julian stared at the silver welds, a horrifying realization dawned on him. The rebar and the welds were not on his side of the door. They were on the outside—the roof side of the barrier. The weld had been executed from the helipad itself, using a portable welder. The implications hit him like a physical blow. The evacuation team, the doctors, or the guards who had made it to the roof had deliberately sealed the stairwell behind them. They had trapped everyone still in the building, ensuring that no one could climb up to join them or compromise the helipad. It was a cold, calculated decision to sacrifice the living to save themselves. Julian pressed his forehead against the cold steel of the door, his heart sinking. He was locked inside a giant concrete tomb with a rising tide of the dead, while the creators of this barrier had flown away into the sky. He looked up at the small, wire-reinforced glass pane in the center of the left door, hoping to see some sign of life.

## Chapter 8: The Message on the Glass
Julian stared at the small, square, wire-reinforced glass pane in the center of the left door. The glass was layered with a thin coat of gray dust and condensation from the cold air outside. Written in thick, black permanent marker on the inner side of the glass was a message, the letters hurried and dripping: 'Helipad full. We took off. We had to seal the stairs. Don't open.' The words were a death sentence written in marker. Julian pressed his face against the cold glass, his breath fogging the surface. He wiped it clear with his sleeve and peered through the wire grid embedded in the pane. The view outside was bleak and gray. The rooftop of St. Jude was exposed to the biting wind, which whipped across the concrete pad. He could see abandoned gurneys, empty supply crates, and pieces of medical equipment scattered across the helipad, left behind in the mad scramble to board the final flight. The windsock hung limp against its metal pole, a orange rag in the gray atmosphere. The message on the glass was clear: there was no room for anyone else, and to prevent the infected or the dead from swarming the helipad, they had sealed the only exit. Julian's chest tightened. The people who wrote this note had likely watched from the safety of the sky as the hospital fell. He was completely alone, staring at an empty concrete pad that had been his last hope, while the wind howled outside the thick glass, mocking his isolation.

## Chapter 9: The Helipad Sound
Through the wire-reinforced glass, the rooftop helipad lay empty, a barren plateau of concrete. The large yellow 'H' painted in the center was peeling and stained with oil. The windsock hung motionless against the gray sky, a solitary sentinel. But as Julian stood there, his forehead pressed against the cold pane, a low-frequency vibration rattled the glass. It wasn't a sound from the rooftop, but a deep, rhythmic thrumming echoing from the thick clouds above. Whump-whump-whump. The unmistakable sound of a helicopter rotor beat against the air, growing fainter by the second. Julian strained his eyes, looking up into the overcast sky, but the aircraft was already gone, swallowed by the dense, gray fog. 'They made it out,' Julian whispered, his voice cracking. The fading thrum of the rotor was the last thread connecting him to the civilized world, to the hope of rescue and organized survival. The faint, sweet smell of unburned jet fuel drifted through the door seals, a ghostly reminder of the departure. As the sound of the rotor died away completely, the silence returned, heavier and more absolute than before. The sky was an empty sheet of gray canvas, offering no answers and no hope. The people in that helicopter were heading to some distant sanctuary, leaving the city to rot. Julian was left behind, standing on the threshold of a locked door, with nothing but the cold wind and the dark stairs behind him.

## Chapter 10: The Locked Gate
Julian looked back at the welded bar. The steel rebar and the silver weld beads were an impenetrable seal; he had no tools to cut through them, and the fire doors were built to withstand immense force. Suddenly, a sound echoed from the concrete stairwell below, rising through the dark shaft. It was the heavy, uneven thumping of boots climbing the steps. Thud-drag... thud-drag. Along with the footsteps came the metallic scrape of a loose belt buckle against the concrete wall and a low, wet rattle from a dead throat. The dead were climbing. They had heard the clicking of the door latch or smelled his warm blood. Julian’s heart seized. He could picture them—their cold grey skin, their cloudy, milky eyes staring blankly ahead, their dry jaws snapping in anticipation. With the path to the roof welded shut, the landing was a dead end. Julian scrambled toward the opposite service stairs, a narrow fire exit that descended into the eastern wing of the hospital. He pushed through the door just as the first grey fingers rounded the stairwell landing below. He retreated down the dark steps, his flashlight beam shaking violently as he ran. He left the welded doors behind, the fading echo of the helicopter's rotor and the mystery of its destination lingering in the dark. As he descended into the blackness of the eastern wing, he could only hope that the path below was clear, though the whispers in the dark suggested otherwise.


# Story 22: The Prison Intercom

## Chapter 1: The Penitentiary
Sergeant Ross raised his gloved hand, halting his squad at the perimeter gate of the Blackwood Penitentiary. The gravel parking lot was empty, save for a few abandoned guard vehicles with doors left open and headlights long burned out. Above them, the double razor-wire fences stood intact, though scraps of bloodied orange fabric and dead, rotting birds hung caught in the silver barbs. The high concrete watchtowers loomed against the heavy, leaden sky like ancient, silent sentinels. "Watch your angles," Ross commanded, his voice a low grunt through his tactical headset. His hands were clamped tight on his M4 carbine, his index finger resting just above the trigger guard. The main vehicle gate, a massive structure of iron bars, was slightly ajar, swinging slowly in the damp wind with a high-pitched, rusted screech. The prison looked completely deserted, a sprawling fortress of brick, concrete, and cold steel that had gone dark. The air smelled of wet asphalt, rusted metal, and decaying pine needles from the surrounding forest. There was no signs of life, nor of the dead. Ross scanned the yard, his eyes darting to the dark glass of the guard towers. He knew the horror that could lurk inside a sealed facility like this; in the early days of the outbreak, the fatal fever had swept through crowded spaces like wildfire, and since reanimation claimed everyone who died, a single death behind bars could turn an entire block into a feeding frenzy in hours. The heavy silence of the yard was unnatural, warning him that the fortress had not survived, but had merely run out of screams.

## Chapter 2: The Voice from the Wall
From the concrete gallery of watchtower One, a massive, rusted metal horn speaker was broadcasting a loud, distorted message that cut through the silence. The audio was warped, filled with crackles of static and the low-frequency hum of a deteriorating amplifier. "...this is Warden Croft... facility is under emergency protocol... all guards report to the motor pool... secure the yard..." The recording paused with a sharp click, hissed for a second, and then began the loop again. It had been playing for days, powered by the watchtower’s solar cells, a mechanical ghost shouting to an empty courtyard. The warden’s voice was remarkably calm, carrying the practiced authority of a man used to managing chaos, but the endless, robotic repetition gave it a chilling, unnatural quality. The words bounced off the tall concrete walls, echoing over the gravel yard and the empty guard booths. Ross and his squad walked beneath the speaker, their boots crunching softly on the loose stones. The sound made them tense, their eyes constantly scanning the empty gun ports of the towers. The horn speaker clicked again, repeating the directive to secure a yard that was already lost. It was a warning from the past, a transmission sent out in the final hours when the living still believed they could contain the threat. But looking around the desolate space, Ross knew that 'emergency protocols' had failed here just as they had everywhere else. The voice was a tombstone, marking the exact moment the prison’s chain of command had snapped under the weight of the dead.

## Chapter 3: Entering the Guardhouse
Ross led his squad through the open gate, their boots clicking in a tight, cautious rhythm on the cracked asphalt. They approached the main guardhouse, a squat concrete structure that served as the prison’s entry point. The heavy steel security gates, which should have required electronic authorization, hung wide open. The magnetic lock housings were shattered, their internal wiring pulled loose and dangling like copper veins. Inside, the air was freezing, holding a stagnant chill that smelled of spilled coffee, wet masonry, and a faint, sweet trace of old blood. "Clear!" a private called out, his voice tense as his flashlight beam swept the dark lobby. The beam illuminated a scene of hasty departure. Riot shields were piled in a corner, their plastic visors scratched and cracked; a helmet lay near a shattered coffee cup, its chin strap severed. The linoleum floor was covered in muddy footprints that led deeper into the building. The squad moved in a tight wedge formation, weapons raised to cover every dark doorway. The walls, painted a sickly light green, were peeling from the dampness, and the emergency lights flickered with a quiet, irregular buzz. This guardhouse was built to keep the outside world out and the inmates in, but now the barrier was gone, the doors swung wide to the elements. The cold air blew through the broken glass of the entrance, rustling the papers on the intake desk, a reminder that the fortress's walls were now nothing more than cold monuments.

## Chapter 4: The Empty Desk
The main security desk was a cluttered mess of discarded logbooks and empty coffee mugs, but the primary phone console was still active. Its small digital screen glowed, showing a flashing red indicator for an open external line. The phone speaker was active, emitting a flat, continuous dial tone that buzzed like a hornet trapped in a jar. The sound was deafening in the silence of the room, a monotonous flatline of communication. Ross ignored the phone and focused on the wall of security monitors above the desk. Most of the CRT screens were dead, their glass shattered or screens dark, but three monitors still flickered with low-resolution, black-and-white feeds. The cameras showed the interior of Cellblock A and B. The heavy iron cell doors were thrown open, their lock bars retracted. The long, narrow corridors of concrete and steel grates were completely vacant. No inmates, no guards, no bodies. It was an eerie sight; the prison’s cells, designed to hold the state's most dangerous men, were empty tombs. The cameras panned slowly, showing only discarded blankets and overturned plastic tables. The silence captured by the lenses was absolute. Ross felt a drop of sweat run down his neck. An open prison was a terrifying prospect. If the inmates had been infected and died, they should still be here, wandering the corridors. If they were gone, it meant they had left before the virus could claim them—or they had found a way to survive the slaughter.

## Chapter 5: The Armory
The squad moved down the main corridor toward the armory, their weapons raised, flashlight beams cutting through the heavy shadows of the hallway. The walls were thick concrete, painted a sterile white that had begun to yellow. At the end of the hall, the armory door, a heavy reinforced steel structure designed to withstand explosives and riots, stood wide open. The electronic keypad mounted beside the frame was completely shattered, its plastic housing cracked and the internal copper wires pulled out and cut, dangling like dead vines. Someone had used brute force to override the electronic lock, slamming a heavy tool into the delicate circuitry. Ross stepped over the broken plastic debris, his boots crunching on the glass. "They broke the vault," he said, his voice echoing in the small room. The air inside the armory was dry and smelled of gun oil, solvent, and cold steel. The vault was a secure room within the secure facility, built to house the prison’s lethal response gear. The steel plating on the walls was thick, designed to keep weapons out of the hands of inmates. But the door’s locking bolts had been forced back, the metal around the frame bent and scarred. Ross shone his light around the room, the beam reflecting off empty metal racks and bare wooden shelves. The realization of what had occurred here began to set in. This wasn't a case of the dead breaching the room; it was a human breach, a deliberate and successful effort to raid the facility's most dangerous assets.

## Chapter 6: Cleaned Out
The armory was completely empty. The vertical gun racks, which should have held dozens of Remington shotguns, Ruger rifles, and heavy tear gas launchers, were bare, their padded metal holders empty. The ammunition lockers, massive steel cabinets with dual-padlock assemblies, stood wide open, their heavy doors bent on their hinges. The shelves inside had been stripped clean, not a single crate or box left behind. Only a few empty cardboard ammunition boxes and plastic shotgun shell trays lay discarded on the concrete floor, kicked aside in the rush. "Every weapon is gone," Ross muttered, running a gloved hand along a bare gun rack. "And the ammo. Every single round." The squad members exchanged tense glances. A cleared armory meant that whoever had abandoned this prison was now heavily armed. The fresh smell of gun oil and the lack of dust on the shelves suggested the raid had occurred recently, perhaps only a couple of days ago. The team’s corporal stepped to the lockers, shining his light into the dark recesses, but found nothing but empty paper manuals. The loss of the weapons was a severe blow; they had hoped to resupply their own dwindling ammunition reserves here. Instead, they were standing in an empty room, realizing that a massive arsenal had been released into the countryside. The dread grew heavier. An armed force, desperate and unchecked, was operating in the area, and they had no way of knowing if they were friendly or hostile.

## Chapter 7: Smashed Outward
They exited the rear of the guardhouse and walked out into the motor pool yard, a fenced enclosure at the back of the prison. The area was designed for vehicle maintenance and transport staging. At the far end, the massive steel gates that opened onto the state highway had been violently smashed *outward*. The heavy iron bars, designed to keep vehicles from ramming their way into the prison, were bent toward the road in a chaotic V-shape. He examined the bent metal, noting how the force had been applied from the inside out. This wasn't a breach from a hostile force trying to get in; this was a breakout. Something heavy had plowed through the barrier at high speed, using sheer mass to crush the gate. The smell of unburned diesel fuel and leaked transmission fluid hung in the damp air, fresh enough to sting their nostrils. The squad stood in the gap, looking out at the empty highway that stretched into the gray distance, the path of the escapees clear.

## Chapter 8: The Sledge and Tire Tracks
On the wet asphalt near the ruined gate lay a heavy sledgehammer, its wooden handle splintered and broken as if it had been struck against steel with frantic force. Next to it, deep, wide tire tracks ran through the muddy shoulder of the road, carving dark ruts into the soft earth. The tracks were dual-wheel impressions, characteristic of heavy vehicles. The inmates and guards had apparently loaded the buses with the armory's contents and used the vehicles as battering rams to breach the perimeter. The broken sledgehammer suggested they had tried to break the locks first before resorting to ramming the gates. The cooperation required for such an escape was staggering. In a normal outbreak, the guards would have locked down the blocks, but here, the shared threat of the dead had forced a truce. They had opened the cells, shared the weapons, and fled together. Ross walked into the small motor pool office, a cluttered room with windows overlooking the repair bays. On the desk, next to a rusted toolbox, lay a wooden clipboard. Clipped to it was a sheet of official prison stationery, covered in a hastily scribbled note written in blue ballpoint pen. Ross picked it up, his flashlight beam illuminating the shaky handwriting. "Croft here. 09:30. Armory secured. Inmates and guards are armed. We took the transport buses. Heading north. God help the city." They had realized that the walls of the penitentiary were no longer a defense, but a trap. They had emptied the armory, loaded the buses, and breached the gates to escape. It was a desperate alliance born of absolute terror. In the face of an enemy that did not tire, did not bleed, and stood back up after death, the old boundaries of law and crime had dissolved. The guards had armed the very men they were paid to cage, choosing the risk of an armed breakout over the certainty of being eaten alive in the dark. Ross folded the paper and slipped it into his vest pocket. The note was a testament to the collapse of the old world. The caravan was gone, heading north into the wilderness, leaving the empty prison behind as a monument to their desperate truce.

## Chapter 10: Empty Yard
Ross stepped back out into the open yard, looking around the empty space as a cold drizzle began to fall. The rain hissed against the hot engine of their vehicle, mixing with the damp smell of concrete. There were no bodies lying in the dirt—no guards in uniform, no inmates in orange, and no reanimated dead wandering the fences. The prison was completely empty, its entire population having departed in the night as an armed caravan. They had cleaned out the weapons, the food, and the transport, leaving behind only the hollow shell of the facility. Ross called his squad back to their transport vehicle, their mission here complete and fruitless. They climbed inside, the metal doors shutting out the damp chill of the yard. Through the rear window, Ross watched the watchtower fade into the gray fog. The massive metal horn speaker was still looping its warning into the empty yard, its voice growing fainter as the engine roared to life. "...facility is under emergency protocol... secure the yard..." The recording would play until the solar batteries finally failed, a useless command to an empty fortress. As the squad drove out through the ruined gates, Ross looked north along the highway. The road was empty, the tire tracks of the buses already washing away in the rain. The mystery of the caravan's fate remained, a ghost trail leading into the dark hills, leaving the squad to wonder if they would ever cross paths with the warden’s armed convoy.


# Story 23: The Logging Camp

Tristan pushed a heavy, pine-scented branch aside, his mud-caked boots sinking deep into the wet moss of the forest floor. Here, the air was freezing and clean, smelling of damp earth, crushed needles, and the sharp scent of ozone. Tristan stepped through the door of the office cabin, the hinges letting out a dry, rusted groan. Tristan approached the desk, his boots making no sound on the dirt-caked floorboards. He stared at the glowing console, the repeating message filling the empty room. The voice belonged to a living man, but the repetition suggested it was a recorded loop, a automated beacon left behind to guide any other loggers who had been separated from the main convoy in the initial panic.

Tristan reached out and picked up the heavy plastic microphone, the coiled black cord stretching stiffly in the cold air. He pressed the talk button, the static on the speaker instantly cutting out, replaced by the silent transmission of his own channel. "Who is this?" Tristan turned from the radio and began to search the rest of the cabin interior, looking for anything useful. Instead of fleeing toward the crowded towns, they had opted to head into the trackless forest. Tristan touched the paper, the dry wood pulp smelling of old ink. The logbook gave them a direction, a clue to where the loggers had gone, but it also confirmed their worst fears: the cities were dead, and the wilderness was the only place left to hide.

The massive yellow skidders, designed to drag multi-ton logs through the mud, were gone. With these massive machines, the loggers had no need for paved roads or cleared paths. They could literally carve their way through the dense forest, knocking down trees and crushing boulders in their path. The empty yard was a silent testament to the loggers' resourcefulness. ## Chapter 7: The Tire Tracks

Tristan walked to the edge of the clearing, his boots sinking into the ruts. The trail was a scar on the forest floor, a path carved by raw horsepower and steel. ## Chapter 8: The Logbook Clue

Torres took the headset back and set it on the console. The green light of the sonar screen flickered, casting a sickly hue over the empty station. The sea-water temperature gauge was bottomed out. The sub was a giant steel coffin, floating on the surface but dead inside, its electrical systems slowly draining away while the mysterious scraping sound continued to echo faintly through the structural frames of the hull.

They had chosen the cold, indifferent sea over the cramped corridors of the *Appalachian*. Their lungs would fill with salt water, their hearts would stop, but the infection or the simple act of dying would reanimate their cold grey flesh. They climbed back up the vertical shaft, exiting the hatch into the freezing sea wind. The Atlantic was a vast, grey desert, the waves cresting with white foam. As the zodiac pulled away, the massive black sail of the submarine slowly vanished into the creeping fog, a silent monument to a crew that had fled into the dark water, leaving only the static to mourn them.

The structure, over a hundred feet wide, rose like a giant, metal bowl on its colossal concrete pedestal. It looked like an alien monument in the snow. Despite the storm, the hydraulic motors inside the pedestal were humming, a deep, low-frequency vibration that shook the snow from the metal framework and resonated through the soles of Harris's boots.
The air around the pedestal smelled of hot machine oil, unburned fuel from the auxiliary generators, and the sharp, metallic scent of ozone. The dish was moving, its massive gears turning with a slow, grinding shriek that sounded like a groaning door hinge. It was tracking a path across the cloudy sky, following an invisible orbit with mechanical precision.

Harris walked to the base of the concrete pedestal. Near the maintenance ladder, he noticed a dark stain on the concrete. He wiped the snow away to reveal a splatter of sweet-smelling, copper-colored blood, now frozen into a dark crust. Next to the bloodstain lay a dropped wrench, its handle coated in a dry, dark fluid. The machinery was running on autopilot, maintained by the station's geothermal power grid, but the people who operated it had left their marks behind. The giant dish continued its slow, mechanical sweep, turning its face toward the stars while the storm raged around it. The contrast between the cold, unfeeling machinery and the visceral remains of human violence on the concrete made Harris's skin crawl. He gestured for the squad to move toward the main entrance of the observatory dome.

Harris pushed open the heavy security door of the main building, the magnetic lock clicking open automatically as the sensors detected their approach. The interior of the observatory was a stark contrast to the freezing mountain peak. The air was warm, smelling of fresh paint, recycled air, and the dry, electrical scent of ozone. The geothermal grid was fully active, supplying heat and power to the entire facility.

The chairs were overturned, and the desk drawer was pulled open, its contents scattered. On the wall behind the desk, a smear of dark fluid ran down the plaster, ending in a dry pool on the floor. Harris knelt to inspect it. The blood had dried to a dark, tarry consistency, indicating it had been there for days. There were no bodies, but the signs of a sudden, violent evacuation were clear. The squad moved in a tight formation, their eyes scanning the dark corners and the open doorways of the offices, waiting for a sound, a groan, or the drag of boots that would signal they were not alone.

Harris walked into the central control room, a spacious chamber dominated by wall-mounted monitors and rows of computer servers. The air pressure, water reserves, and electrical output of the ISS were all within normal parameters. The space station was a metal island orbiting a dead world, its automated systems keeping it alive while the planet below burned. Harris stared at the green tracking line, imagining the astronauts looking down at the dark, silent cities, watching the fires burn out one by one, knowing they could never return to a living world. The realization was cold, a deep dread that settled in his stomach as the computer continued to track the silent orbit.

It was a partial fingerprint, left in a smear of sweet-smelling, copper-colored blood. Below the desk, a discarded first-aid kit was open, its sterile bandages scattered and stained with a dark fluid. 
The technician's words were a final testament. The crew had watched the world die from their mountain peak, tracking the ISS until the silence on the airwaves became too much to bear. They had retreated to the geothermal bunker beneath the station, hoping the steel doors would keep the horror out. But Harris knew that a bunker was only a sanctuary if everyone inside was healthy. In this new world, a single fever, a heart attack, or a hidden bite would turn a safe haven into a sealed tomb. He closed the logbook, the paper making a dry, whispering sound, and signaled his men toward the basement stairs.

Harris led the squad down the narrow concrete stairs to the basement level, their flashlights cutting through the thick darkness. Harris shined his light on the seal, seeing the dark stain that had seeped through. The barrier was intact, but the seals were not enough to contain the smell of what lay beyond. The steel was unmarred, the concrete frame intact. The crew had made their choice, sealing themselves in the dark beneath the mountain, away from the sky and the cold wind. But the locking bars were a double-edged sword. If something had gone wrong inside the bunker, the doors would prevent any escape. The crew would be trapped in the dark, with no way to open the hatch, forced to face whatever horror was growing in the geothermal warmth. The solid red light of the panel seemed to stare back at Harris like a bloody, unblinking eye, confirming the finality of their decision.

## Chapter 8: The Indicator Lights
The geothermal system was humming, a deep, rhythmic vibration that resonated through the steel plate of the door and into their bones, providing unlimited power and heat to the bunker below. Harris checked the secondary indicators on the door console. The air filtration system was green: *NORMAL*. The water pressure was steady, and the internal temperature of the bunker was listed at seventy degrees.

A single infected person, or a single death from natural causes, would start a chain reaction in the closed space. Without weapons or room to run, the crew would have been cornered in the dark. The geothermal heat had kept their bodies from freezing, and the air filtration system had kept them from rotting away entirely. They left the radio dish to track the stars and the crew to pace their underground cell. The snow would bury their tracks, leaving the Deep Space Observatory alone on the peak, a silent monument to a world that had ended, its last signals fading into the cold, empty sky while the dead below paced in the warmth of the mountain.


# Story 28: The ISS Radio

## Chapter 1: The Signal
Kyle sat in the corner of his wooden shack, the warm hum of the vacuum-tube receiver the only comfort in the quiet, frozen room. It was years after the fall of the cities, and the airwaves had been silent for as long as he could remember, yielding nothing but a flat, hissing sea of static. Outside, the northern wilderness was a dark sprawl of skeletal trees, their branches scraping against the cabin's roof. 
Suddenly, a clear, high-frequency signal broke through the static. 
*Beep... Beep... Beep...*
It was a tracking transponder, its tone sharp, clean, and carrying the cold resonance of the upper atmosphere. Kyle adjusted the tuner dial, his fingers trembling on the warm brass knob. The signal was remarkably strong, carrying no atmospheric distortion. 
But as he leaned closer to the speaker, his heart hammered against his ribs. Outside the window, a sound cut through the pine trees. It was the dry drag of boots through the snow, accompanied by the scratching of fingernails against the cabin's log wall. Kyle froze, holding his breath, his eyes darting to the frost-rimed glass. In the moonlight, a shape moved. It was a wanderer, its skin cold and grey, its clothes hanging in tatters. The thing's eyes were cloudy and milky, reflecting the pale light of the sky. Its jaw worked in a silent, mechanical snap, split dry flesh tearing at the corners of its mouth as it sensed the warmth inside. Kyle remained motionless, waiting for the thing to pass, his ears filled with the dual rhythm of the transponder's beep and the wet, rattling breaths of the dead just inches away.

## Chapter 2: The Tracking
The wanderer outside eventually drifted away, its boots dragging slowly into the dark pines. Kyle exhaled, the hot air clouding in the freezing cabin. He turned back to the receiver, the blue dial light casting long shadows across the wooden floor. He needed to track this signal's path across the sky before the battery died.
Using his hand-wound directional antenna—a makeshift array of copper wire and aluminum tubes mounted on a rusted pipe—he began to plot the signal's vector. He stepped to the manual crank, turning the mast with a quiet, grinding squeak. The signal peaked when the antenna was pointed high to the southwest, then began to fade as he rotated it.
By measuring the signal's strength and the angle of his antenna over several minutes, Kyle calculated its trajectory. It was moving rapidly, crossing from the western horizon to the east in a matter of minutes. The speed was immense, far too fast for a conventional aircraft. The trajectory was precise, matching the exact orbit of the International Space Station, a metal island that had been silent for years. 
Kyle's mind raced. He remembered watching the station pass overhead as a boy, a bright star crossing the heavens. Had someone survived up there all this time, looking down at the burning cities and the creeping grey tide of the dead? The signal beeped again, a clean, mechanical heartbeat in the silence of his cabin, a voice from a world that had ceased to exist. He connected the audio cable to his old cassette recorder, his fingers slick with sweat.

## Chapter 3: The Broadcast
The transponder tone cut out, replaced by a sudden click, and then a voice emerged from the speaker. It was a man's voice, calm, clear, and unhurried—a voice from the past, untouched by the panic of the surface.
"This is Expedition 72. The station is secure. We have set the transmitter on a timer... if anyone is listening..."
The recording clicked, paused, and then started again. It was a looped broadcast, its frequency stable.
"We survived," the voice continued, though there was a tremor in the astronaut's tone that he couldn't quite hide. "But the containment failed. We had a casualty in the Zvezda module. Dr. Volkov suffered a sudden cardiac arrest. We didn't understand. We didn't know what was happening on the ground."
Kyle leaned closer, the vacuum tubes of his receiver smelling of hot metal and ozone. 
"He died," the recording said, the voice dropping to a whisper. "Within minutes, he... he got back up. His eyes were cloudy. He didn't breathe, but he was moving. He tore through the internal seals. We had to isolate the module. We locked him in, but we can hear him. He's been scraping his fingernails against the hatch for three weeks. We can hear the metallic snap of his jaws through the ventilation ducts. The air scrubbers are beginning to fail, and we can't access the filters without going through Zvezda. We are evacuating."
Kyle felt a chill that had nothing to do with the drafts in the cabin. The infection had reached the stars. Even in the vacuum of space, the dead did not rest.

## Chapter 4: The Timer
"The station's air scrubbers are operational," the recording continued, the voice echoing in the small, dark cabin. "But the backup systems are failing. ## Chapter 7: The Console Note
They returned to the central control room, their pack straps heavy with supplies they had gathered from the pantry. They had loaded their bags with canned meat, dried fruit, medicine, and boxes of rifle ammunition from the armory. 
On the desk, next to the spinning tape deck, was a single sheet of paper. It sat under a brass paperweight, a clean white page with a message written in bold black marker. 
Gavin shined his light on the paper. The page was uncreased, the ink dry and black. It looked like a final, deliberate message left by the last operator before they walked out the door. 
"There's a note," Gavin said, reaching out to lift the paperweight. The brass was cold, shaped like an anchor, its surface polished and reflecting the green console lights. 
The paper under the weight was a standard piece of printer paper, its edges clean. The writing was large and clear, a message meant to be seen by anyone who entered the room. Gavin picked it up, his gloved fingers holding the paper as he read the words in the beam of his light. The writing was a hasty but steady scribble, the strokes of the marker bold. It was the final testament of the bunker's creators, a guide for those who came after, left on the desk next to the machine that was still broadcasting its warning to the empty airwaves, a bridge between the living and those who had gone.

## Chapter 8: The Message
Gavin held the paper in his hands, the paper rustling in the draft from the ventilation system. He cleared his throat, his voice sounding dry and hoarse in the quiet room. 
"We figured it out. We went North. Follow the river."
Gavin read the words aloud, his voice echoing in the quiet control room. 
Elena stepped closer, looking at the paper over his shoulder. "That's it? Just 'We went North'?"
"It's enough," Owen said, his eyes fixed on the black marker strokes. 
The message was brief, a hasty but steady scribble. The creators of the bunker had figured out the secret of survival in this dead world. They hadn't been overrun by a horde; they had simply realized that staying in the bunker was a slow death, a gilded cage that would eventually run out of power or air. The only way to survive was to head into the northern wilderness, where the winter was long and cold enough to freeze the infected solid, halting their decay and their movement. 
Gavin folded the paper, slipping it into the pocket of his heavy coat. The note was a map, a guide to a new life. They had been searching for a sanctuary, and the note had given them a direction. The river was the Saskatchewan, a frozen ribbon of ice that cut through the mountains to the north. The creators had followed the river, and they would do the same. They had the supplies, the weapons, and the direction. Gavin looked at the tape deck, its reels still spinning, sending the message to the sky, a beacon for anyone else who might find the open door.

## Chapter 9: The Realization
"The cold," Owen said, looking toward the open portal and the snow-covered peaks outside. "They went where the dead freeze." 
They realized the creators of the bunker hadn't been overrun. They had simply realized that a bunker was a tomb, a steel vault where they would eventually starve or turn. In this new world, survival lay in the northern wilderness where the winter keeps the infected frozen and immobile. 
In the deep cold, the flesh of the dead would freeze, their joints stiffening until they were unable to move. The infection would be halted by the ice, the bodies preserved but harmless until the spring thaw. If they went far enough north, they would find a land where the winter lasted for months, a natural barrier that would protect them from the hordes that had destroyed the cities. 
The realization was a revelation. They had been hiding in the ruins, fighting for every scrap of food and living in constant fear, when the key to survival was the weather itself. The creators of the bunker had left the doors open for anyone who followed, leaving the supplies and the warning. Gavin looked at his companions. They were dirty, tired, and scared, but for the first time in years, they had a plan. They would head north, into the cold, where the dead could not follow, using the winter as their shield against the monsters that had taken the world.

## Chapter 10: Heading North
They didn't stay in the bunker. 
They packed their bags with the fresh food, the medicine, and the ammunition from the armory. They checked their weapons one last time, the steel bolts clicking home in the quiet room. 
Gavin left the tape deck spinning on the desk, its reels turning slowly as it sent the emergency warning to the empty airwaves. The signal would continue to broadcast, a beacon for any other survivors who might find their way to the gorge. 
They stepped out through the blast door, the warm air of the interior hitting the freezing wind of the mountain. They shut the heavy door behind them, the hydraulic seals engaging with a soft, final hiss. They turned north, following the river bank into the snowy mountains, the note tucked safely into Gavin's pocket. 
The storm was still raging, the snow falling in heavy sheets that were slowly burying their tracks in a white shroud. The river was a rushing torrent below them, its white water cutting a path through the frozen gorge, a cold ribbon guiding them. They walked in silence, their boots sinking deep into the snow, their eyes fixed on the northern horizon where the mountains rose. The wilderness was vast and cold, but it was their only chance at a future. They were heading into the frost, leaving the dead world behind, following the river toward a new beginning in the snow, where the dead could not walk.
