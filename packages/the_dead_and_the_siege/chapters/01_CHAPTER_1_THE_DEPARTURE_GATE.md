CHAPTER 1: THE DEPARTURE GATE

The air inside Terminal 3 of John F. Kennedy International Airport had the dry, chemical chill of an industrial refrigeration unit that had run non-stop for twenty years without a filter swap. It was four in the afternoon on a Tuesday in late October, but beneath the low acoustic-tile ceilings and the humming amber sodium fixtures, time did not move by the sun. It moved by the staggered flicker of the split-flap flight departure boards, the mechanical clatter of thousands of plastic letters spinning in unison, updating delays with a dry, insectoid rattle that echoed across the polished terrazzo floors.

Arthur Pendelton stood near the security checkpoint at Gate 14, his thick fingers hooked into the heavy leather utility belt cinched around his waist. He was fifty-six years old, thirty-one of those years spent wearing the navy-blue wool uniform of the Port Authority terminal operations division. His knees ached whenever the Atlantic barometer dropped, and today the barometer was plunging like an anvil tossed into Jamaica Bay. Outside the massive panes of laminated plate glass, the sky over Long Island was the color of dirty lead, low-bellied clouds scudding fast off the grey Atlantic swells.

"Arthur, you got a second?"

Arthur turned with the slow, measured economy of a man who had seen four decades of lost baggage, delayed charters, drunken brawls, and blizzard shutdowns. It was Deputy Supervisor Bobby Harris, a young man of twenty-eight whose uniform still had crisp factory creases and whose brass badge lacked the fine spiderweb scratches of thirty years on the night shift. Harris was breathing shallowly through his nose, his eyes darting toward the passenger queue at the security screening lanes.

"What is it, Bobby?" Arthur asked. His voice was low, a raspy baritone cured by three decades of filterless cigarettes and damp Queens air.

"We got a regional arrival at Gate 42," Harris said, stepping closer so his words wouldn't carry over the drone of the floor scrubbers. "Beechcraft 1900 twin turboprop out of Bangor. Pilot touched down eight minutes ago on Runway 22 Left. Taxiing in now. Ground ops says the pilot declared an emergency halfway down the taxiway."

Arthur sighed, taking off his heavy peaked cap to smooth down the sparse grey hair at his temples before putting it back on with a tug at the bill. "Declared what kind of emergency? Landing gear hydraulic leak? Fuel sensor failure?"

"Neither," Harris said, glancing down at a yellow legal pad covered in jagged shorthand. "He called ground control directly on the tactical band. Said he had a combative passenger in seat 4B. The pilot wanted emergency medical services and Port Authority police standing by at the gate apron before he popped the cabin door."

"Combative how?" Arthur asked, already stepping toward the moving walkway that connected Concourse B to the outer domestic finger of Concourse C. "Drunk? Some passenger having an anxiety fit because the turbulence over the Sound kicked his stomach into his throat?"

"Pilot used the word 'unstable,' Arthur," Harris said, walking fast to match Pendelton's heavy stride. "Said the passenger had some kind of violent seizure ten minutes out of Bangor, bit another traveler through a denim jacket, and then tried to kick out the forward cockpit bulkhead partition. The co-pilot had to leave the right seat and pin the guy against the aisle floor with the emergency crash axe handle."

Arthur frowned, the skin around his eyes tightening into deep creases. "Did dispatch alert the CDC quarantine station over in Terminal 4?"

"Line went dead when I tried," Harris muttered, checking the screen of his handheld motorola radio. "The whole internal telephone exchange has been crawling since noon. Cell service is dropping bars all over Queens. The radio channels are buzzing with static. Dispatch says there's some kind of widespread power anomaly rippling out of Westchester and northern New Jersey."

Arthur didn't answer. He led the way down Concourse C, his heavy work boots striking the commercial carpet with a steady, rhythmic thud. The terminal around them was abnormally crowded. Flights to Boston, Philadelphia, and Washington National had been grounded since mid-morning without official explanation. Stranded travelers were slumped across banks of vinyl chairs, surrounding luggage carts piled high with nylon suitcases, their faces illuminated by the pale blue glare of mobile phones that displayed spinning connection wheels. The smell was thick: stale pretzel butter, overheated jet exhaust drifting through air intake louvers, and the sour tang of hundreds of anxious bodies packed into a closed space.

They passed Gate 28, where a Delta Boeing 767 sat motionless at the jet bridge, its cabin windows dark and its boarding door sealed shut. Two mechanics in greasy high-visibility vests stood beneath the wing, staring up at the engine cowling with their hands deep in their pockets, speaking in low, urgent voices.

"Look at that tarmac," Harris whispered, pointing through the plate glass.

Arthur glanced out. Out on the taxiways, five regional jets were lined up nose-to-tail on the concrete apron, their navigation strobes flashing against the gathering dusk, their engines idling in place. None of them were moving. Ground marshallers in fluorescent orange coats were nowhere to be seen. The baggage tugs sat abandoned in their painted staging boxes, their yellow caution lights left revolving silently in the cold mist.

When they reached Gate 42 at the far terminus of the low-ceilinged commuter concourse, two Port Authority officersCollins and Higginswere already stationed by the heavy steel service door that opened onto the tarmac ramp. Both officers had their hands resting casually near the holsters of their service weapons, but their shoulders were rigid, their jaws clenched tight.

"Supervisor," Collins said, giving Arthur a curt nod. "The Beechcraft is parked on the apron twenty yards off the line. Engines are spooled down, props feathered. But the pilot hasn't lowered the airstair door."

"Why not?" Arthur asked.

"He cracked the pilot side storm window," Collins said, gesturing through the reinforced wire glass of the gate door. "Shouted down to the ramp supervisor that the passenger broke through the duct-tape restraints on his wrists. He wants armed officers to board through the rear baggage compartment door and secure the cabin before anyone else steps out."

Arthur looked through the narrow rectangular glass. Below them on the oil-stained concrete sat the small white turboprop, its twin propellers motionless. Through the oval passenger windows, Arthur could see shadows moving with violent, unnatural speedjerking silhouettes slamming against the plexiglass panes. A palm struck the inside of the third window back, smearing a dark, wet trail of red across the glass.

"Get your batons out," Arthur said calmly, unclipping the heavy steel flashlight from his utility belt. "Higgins, you stay here at the top of the ramp. If that door opens and anything comes up these stairs that doesn't follow orders, you shut this fire door and throw the deadbolt. You understand me?"

Higgins swallowed hard, his Adam's apple bobbing. "Understood, Supervisor."

Arthur pushed open the heavy steel exterior door. The October wind hit them like a wall of wet iron, carrying the raw, sulfurous stench of burned kerosene and the salty tang of Jamaica Bay mud. The cold tarmac vibrated under their bootsnot from an aircraft engine, but from a low, rhythmic thrumming sound rolling in from the north, from the direction of Manhattan, like the distant rumble of an approaching freight train that never arrived.

They descended the metal exterior stairs and walked across the asphalt apron toward the rear of the Beechcraft. As they approached, the small plane rocked violently on its landing gear struts, the rubber tires groaning against the damp concrete.

"Port Authority!" Collins called out, stepping up to the rear baggage hatch. "Pilot, we are at the hatch. Release the latch from the inside!"

A heavy metallic click echoed from within the fuselage. The latch turned.

Arthur gripped the steel flashlight in his left hand, his right hand resting on the heavy wooden baton at his side. "Bobby, stay behind Collins. Collins, on three."

Collins took a deep breath, seized the flush-mounted exterior handle, and yanked the hatch downward.

The interior of the commuter plane did not look like a pressurized passenger compartment. It looked like an abattoir. The narrow aisle carpet was soaked in dark, viscous fluid. The overhead plastic luggage bins were cracked and hanging by their wiring harnesses. In the fourth row, a man in a torn grey business suit was pinned against the floor under two twisted aluminum seat frames, his spine arched at an impossible, broken angle. His face was entirely devoid of human expression: skin grey as river slate, lips pulled back over broken, bloody teeth, eyes milky white and fixed in a wide, unblinking glare.

The moment the hatch swung open to the salt air, the figure didn't groan or scream. It let out a wet, rattling hiss, like steam venting from a split copper pipe, and lunged forward with frantic, terrifying momentum, its fingernails dragging through the carpet, pulling the wreckage of the seats with it.

"Back up!" Collins shouted, drawing his sidearm. "Stay down! Stay on the floor!"

The man in the suit didn't flinch. He didn't register the drawn weapon or the shout. He surged forward on his hands, his head snapping side to side with predatory speed, his jaw snapping shut with a hollow, wooden clack that echoed out across the damp tarmac.

Arthur Pendelton didn't hesitate. Thirty years of terminal operations had taught him the physics of panic, but this was not panic. This was an eradication event. He brought the heavy six-cell aluminum flashlight down in a hard, two-handed diagonal strike across the creature's collarbone and neck, the impact resounding with the solid crunch of wet pine.

The force drove the thing against the metal doorframe, but it did not collapse. Its milky eyes rolled toward Arthur, its fingers clawing into the heavy wool sleeve of his coat, seeking meat.

"Fire!" Arthur barked, driving his boot into the creature's chest to break its grip. "Collins, take the head! Fire now!"

The gunshot cracked across the open tarmac, flat and sharp, instantly swallowed by the cold Atlantic wind. The creature's head snapped back, black, congealed fluid spraying across the yellow aircraft skin, and the body slumped over the lower sill of the hatch, twitching once before going entirely limp.

Silence fell over the apron, save for the whistling wind through the plane's propeller blades.

Bobby Harris was leaning against the landing gear strut, vomiting onto the concrete, his face ash-white. Collins stood with his service weapon drawn, his hands trembling so violently that the barrel clicked against the metal of his holster.

Arthur stood breathing hard, his knuckles aching from the impact of the flashlight. He looked past the Beechcraft, out toward the main terminal complex.

Through the plate glass windows of Concourse B, three hundred yards away, the lights flickered twice and died, leaving the entire concourse in the eerie green glow of emergency battery lamps. And then, through the glass, Arthur saw the first wave of passengers runningnot toward the exits, but toward the gates, knocking down velvet stanchions, screaming soundlessly behind the thick glass, pursued by dozens of dark, frantic shapes moving with terrifying, unnatural speed across the polished floors.

"It isn't just Gate 42," Arthur whispered, wiping a fleck of black bile from the sleeve of his coat. "The terminals are breached. The whole airport is going down."
