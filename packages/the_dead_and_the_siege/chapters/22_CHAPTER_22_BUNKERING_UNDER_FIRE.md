CHAPTER 22: BUNKERING UNDER FIRE

Marcus sprinted down the center of the pier, his boots splashing through deep puddles of diesel fuel and rainwater, followed by Brenda and three Navy fuel handlers dragging the heavy four-inch hose.

Manifold Station 4 was a heavy steel cage bolted into the concrete, housing the ten-inch marine diesel supply lines that ran from the storage tanks on the bluff. The automated electronic control panel was a melted ruin of burned wires, having taken a direct lightning strike during the previous night's storm.

"The electronic actuators are fried!" Brenda shouted, shining her headlamp into the cage. "The gate valves are locked shut on mechanical dogs!"

"Out of the way!" Marcus bellowed.

He dropped his track wrench onto the concrete, grabbed the four-foot cast-iron handwheel of the primary supply valve, and threw his entire weight against the rim.

The valve was seized. Salt air and industrial soot had corroded the heavy bronze stem, locking the threads tight.

Marcus retrieved his twelve-pound carbon-steel track wrench, jammed the hexagonal jaw between two spokes of the handwheel, braced both boots against the concrete curb, and hauled back with every ounce of muscle in his broad back.

The iron groaned. A high-pitched, metallic shriek echoed above the roar of rifle fire as sixty years of industrial grease broke free. With a sharp *CLACK*, the stem turned half an inch.

"It's moving!" Brenda yelled. "Keep turning, Marcus!"

Marcus worked the wrench, turning the wheel one spoke at a time, his breath tearing in ragged, burning gasps from his lungs. "Handlers! Hook the cam-lock fittings! Now!"

The two Navy handlers hauled the four-inch hose forward, snapping the heavy bronze cam-lock couplers onto the manifold discharge horns, locking the brass arms down with sharp blows from their rubber mallets.

Twenty yards back, the defensive perimeter was collapsing.

A massive swarm of several hundred infected had broken through the shipping container alley, swarming over the hood of an overturned fuel truck. Two Marines went down beneath a wave of bodies, their screams instantly cut short by the wet tearing of flesh.

Arthur Pendelton stepped into the gap.

Standing beside a steel bollard, Arthur fired his carbine with cold, methodical precision. Every shot was deliberate. He didn't spray the crowd; he picked his targets, putting rounds through the foreheads and ocular cavities of the lead hosts, breaking their momentum, buying Marcus seconds.

"Arthur, fall back!" Drake yelled, reloading his shotgun with green magnum shells. "They're flanking the pier on the lower maintenance cat-walks!"

"Keep turning that wheel, Marcus!" Arthur barked, dropping an empty thirty-round magazine onto the concrete and slapping a fresh one home. "I'm holding the line!"

An infected dockworker, his chest torn open to the ribs, lunged at Arthur from behind the bollard. Arthur didn't have time to raise the barrel. He drove the steel flash hider of his carbine straight into the creature's teeth, shattering the jaw, then pivoted on his heel and brought the heavy buttstock down across the back of its neck, severing the cervical spine with a sickening crack.

"Valve is open!" Marcus roared from the manifold station.

A deep, violent thrumming shook the concrete pier as three million gallons of high-pressure marine diesel began surging through the pipes, swelling the heavy four-inch rubber transfer hoses into rigid, vibrating conduits that pumped three thousand gallons a minute into the tanks of the waiting landing craft.

"Pumps are running!" Brenda screamed, monitoring the pressure gauges. "Ten minutes to full bunker capacity!"

"We don't have ten minutes!" Drake yelled, stepping beside Arthur, his shotgun barking in rhythm with Arthur's carbine. "Hold this corner! Ten minutes, and then we blow the pier!"
