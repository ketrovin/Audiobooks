CHAPTER 17: THE FLEET AT SANDY HOOK

The six-hundred-foot steel hull of the USS *San Antonio* rode the black Atlantic swells five miles east of Sandy Hook light, rolling slowly through a long, rhythmic twelve-degree arc. Even in the dead of night, with her navigation lights extinguished and her superstructure blacked out under wartime illumination control, the amphibious transport dock was a terrifying monument of industrial naval power. Her high, slab-sided grey hull deflected radar waves, and from her stern well-deck, an exhaust plume of diesel vapors rose into the freezing drizzle.

The Bell 412 descended through the mist, its searchlight painting a circular patch of non-skid deck on the ship's aft flight deck. As the skids touched down, deck handlers in yellow cranials and heavy float coats sprinted across the wet steel, hooking heavy chain tie-downs into the deck cloverleafs before the rotor blades had even slowed.

Captain Jonah Drake pushed the cabin door open, stepping out into the raw Atlantic wind. The air smelled of salt spray, marine diesel, and burnt JP-5 fuela clean, violent contrast to the sickening, sweet stench of the burning metropolis they had left behind.

"Clear the deck!" a flight officer bellowed through a bullhorn. "Evacuees to the starboard hangar bay! Weapons to the red line!"

Drake helped Dr. Sarah Mercer down the metal ladder, followed by Arthur Pendelton, Marcus Vance, Brenda, Pete, and the two FDNY paramedics. Armed Navy security personnel in blue digital camouflage uniforms formed an armed corridor, their M4 rifles held at low-ready, their eyes fixed on the newcomers' hands and faces.

"Welcome aboard the *San Antonio*, Captain Drake," a tall naval officer said, stepping through the hangar blast curtain. He wore a sodden foul-weather parka over khaki service dress, his collar bearing the silver eagles of a Navy captain. It was Captain Thomas VanceMarcus's cousin.

Marcus stepped forward, his boots ringing on the steel deck, his twelve-pound track wrench still clutched in his right hand. The two men stared at each other for three silent seconds, thirty years of family memories passing between them without a word.

"You look exhausted, Marcus," Thomas Vance said quietly.

"I feel worse," Marcus rasped, shifting the wrench to his left hand and offering a calloused, grease-stained palm. "Midtown is gone, Tommy. Grand Central is an incinerator. The whole island is overrun."

Thomas gripped Marcus's hand firmly. "We saw the flash from the bridge. The thermobaric strike on 42nd Street lit up the cloud deck all the way to Asbury Park." He turned to Dr. Mercer. "Dr. Mercer, I assume that insulated case contains the isolates?"

"Twenty-four cryo-vials," Sarah said, holding the nylon pack close to her chest. "Cerebellar and spinal tissue from the index patient. If you have an ultralow freezer and a sequencing centrifuge aboard, I can begin synthesizing the baseline antigens immediately."

"The hospital ship *Comfort* was supposed to join us from Norfolk," Thomas said grimly, "but her main boiler rooms were breached before she cleared the pier. We have converted the *San Antonio*'s forward medical triage bay into an isolation lab. My medical officers are waiting for you below. But firstthe protocol."

"Quarantine," Arthur Pendelton said, nodding in understanding.

"Standard seventy-two-hour observation," Thomas confirmed. "Nobody bypasses the well deck. Not my cousin, not Port Authority supervisors, not the President of the United States if he stepped off that bird. If a single carrier slips into the lower berths of this ship, six hundred sailors and twelve hundred civilian evacuees become trapped in a floating steel coffin. Follow the security detail down to the staging tier."
