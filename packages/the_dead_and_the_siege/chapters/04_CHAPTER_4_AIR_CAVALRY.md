CHAPTER 4: AIR CAVALRY

The vertical service stairwell leading to the control tower roof was a five-hundred-foot concrete echo chamber. Every footstep, every metallic clink of an equipment carabiner against the iron handrails, reverberated up and down the shaft like a gunshot.

Arthur climbed with his teeth clenched, the cold air burning his lungs. His fifty-six-year-old knees screamed with every riser, but the sound below them kept the adrenaline surging through his blood. Down on Sub-level 1, the heavy fire doors had already been breached. The sound of hundreds of hands clawing at the metal risers, the rhythmic thud of bodies throwing themselves against the steel framing, was rising up the shaft like a surging tide.

"Keep climbing!" Drake barked from ten paces above, his flashlight beam cutting through the damp mist that hung in the stairwell. "Do not look down! Keep your eyes on the next tread!"

Bobby Harris was sobbing quietly as he scrambled up behind Miller, his fingers slick with sweat, slipping on the cold iron handrail. "They're right behind us, Arthur! I can hear them breathing!"

"That isn't breathing, kid!" Arthur snapped without slowing his pace. "They don't have breath! That's fluid in their trachea! Move your legs, Bobby, or I'll leave you on this landing!"

They reached the roof bulkhead door on the fortieth floor. Kowalski was there, a burly combat engineer with a soot-stained face and a heavy automatic rifle resting against his shoulder. He grabbed Miller by the tactical harness and yanked him through the doorway onto the wind-swept roof deck, followed by Harris and Arthur.

Drake was the last one through. The moment his boots cleared the threshold, Kowalski kicked the heavy steel fire door shut, threw the three-point deadbolt into the concrete jamb, and drove a six-foot steel pry-bar through the exterior door handles, wedging it tight.

A split second later, the other side of the heavy door slammed with the force of an automobile collision. The thick steel plate bulged outward half an inch, the concrete anchors around the frame groaning as dust sprayed from the masonry joints.

"Rotor blades untied!" Kowalski shouted over the roar of the gale. "Bird One is ready for flight!"

The roof of the JFK central control tower was a sixty-foot square of non-skid concrete, surrounded by a low four-foot perimeter parapet. Beyond the parapet, the world was an apocalyptic panorama of fire and darkness. The entire five-thousand-acre airport complex was burning. Terminal 3 was a hollow skeleton of twisted steel beams engulfed in blue and orange chemical fire. On the runways below, the wreckage of Flight 814 had burned down to its titanium spar, surrounded by thousands of tiny, scurrying figures that looked like ants crawling over an overturned sugar bowl.

To the west, across the dark expanse of Jamaica Bay, the Manhattan skyline was dark. Not a single skyscraper showed interior illumination. The Empire State Building, the Chrysler Building, the massive glass towers of Hudson Yardsall were black monoliths against a bruised purple sky, punctuated only by plumes of oily black smoke rising from midtown and the flickering strobes of emergency vehicles trapped on the bridges.

"The whole city is blacked out," Harris said, staring in paralyzed shock at the darkened horizon. "There are no lights. None."

"The grid collapsed three hours ago," Drake said, pushing Harris toward the open side door of the lead Bell 412 helicopter. "The regional nuclear plants at Indian Point scrammed their reactors automatically when the transmission lines dropped. Manhattan is a concrete canyon full of two million trapped civilians."

The twin turbine engines of the Bell 412 spooled up with an ear-splitting scream, the rotor blades biting into the dense, cold Atlantic air, whipping rain and exhaust fumes across the roof deck.

Pilot VanceMarcus Vance's younger brother, Tommylooked back from the cockpit, giving Drake a sharp thumbs-up through his visor. "We're at maximum gross weight, Captain! Where are we setting down?!"

Drake climbed into the passenger cabin, hauling Arthur in behind him. Kowalski leaped into the second helicopter alongside Miller and the pelican cases containing the data drives.

"Bellevue Hospital northern helipad!" Drake shouted through his headset intercom. "First Avenue and 30th Street!"

Tommy Vance frowned, looking back over his shoulder. "Bellevue? Captain, the civilian reports coming over the maritime bands say Manhattan is totally compromised! Bellevue is ground zero for the East Side medical evacuations!"

"Dr. Sarah Mercer is in the subterranean pathology lab at Bellevue," Drake answered, his voice firm and cold. "She has the primary viral tissue isolates from the initial outbreak in New Rochelle. Without those cultures, no naval lab in the Atlantic can synthesize an antiviral baseline. We pull Mercer, or we have nowhere to go. Lift this bird, Vance! Lift it now!"

Tommy pulled collective. The Bell 412 shuddered, its skids clearing the non-skid concrete deck by six inches, then three feet, before banking hard to the northwest over the burning ruins of the terminal.

Arthur Pendelton looked out through the open cabin door as they gained altitude. Below them, JFK fell away into a sprawling landscape of smoke, flame, and the dark, undulating swarm of the fallen. They crossed the dark waters of Jamaica Bay, climbing through the cold rain toward the dark, silent canyons of New York City.
