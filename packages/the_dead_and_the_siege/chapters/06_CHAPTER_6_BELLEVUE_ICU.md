CHAPTER 6: BELLEVUE ICU

Inside the subterranean pathology laboratory on Sub-level 3 of Bellevue Hospital, Dr. Sarah Mercer was working under the cold, blue glare of an autonomous battery-powered surgical lamp. She was thirty-eight years old, her dark hair pulled back beneath a rubber surgical hood, her face protected by a clear polycarbonate full-face respirator that hissed with every intake of purified air.

On the stainless-steel dissection table before her lay the corpse of a forty-two-year-old male who had been brought in from the Port Authority Bus Terminal at three that morning. His chest cavity was open, pinned back by stainless steel rib retractors, but Sarah wasn't examining his lungs or his heart. Her attention was focused entirely on the cervical spine and the base of the skull.

Using a high-speed pneumatic bone saw, she cut a three-inch circular plug from the occipital plate, exposing the cerebellar cortex.

What she saw made her breath catch in her throat, fogging the inside of her face shield.

The brain tissue was not grey or white. It was shot through with thick, fibrous tendrils of black, calcified plaque that had colonized the brainstem like a fungal root system. The neural pathways were completely coated in a dense, proteinaceous sheath that had bypassed the blood-brain barrier entirely, traveling along the peripheral nerves from the site of the initial bite wound on the man's forearm.

"It isn't a retrovirus," Sarah muttered into the voice recorder pinned to her scrub collar. "It isn't viral meningitis, and it isn't rabies. It's a synthetic prionic pathogen. It reorganizes neural transmission into a closed, high-voltage motor loop. It shuts down higher cognitive processing, eliminates autonomic pain feedback, and drives the adrenal cortex into continuous, catastrophic secretion. The host is dead by medical definitions within two minutes of transmission, but the motor cortex continues to fire until cellular ATP reserves are completely exhausted."

Behind her, the heavy steel security door of the lab vibrated with a dull, heavy thud.

Sarah froze, the pneumatic saw still dripping saline in her gloved hand.

Through the reinforced wire glass of the door, a face was pressed against the pane. It was Dr. Aris Thorne, her department head, a man who had mentored her for twelve years at Columbia Medical. Thorne's neck was torn open, the carotid artery severed and clotted with black, jelly-like coagulum. His eyes were milky white, fixed on her with a blank, predatory hunger, his teeth grinding against the glass with a high-pitched, agonizing screech.

Beside Thorne, three more figures appeared in the hallway: two nurses in blood-soaked scrubs and a hospital security officer whose sidearm holster was empty and flap torn away. They began slamming their shoulders against the door, their collective weight rattling the heavy steel hinges.

Sarah stepped back from the table, setting the saw down on the tray. She walked to the cryogenic storage freezer at the rear of the laba massive, stainless steel upright unit powered by an independent lithium-ion battery backup. She unlocked the heavy vacuum latch, pulled out a cold, frosted aluminum transport cylinder marked with high-visibility biohazard symbols, and checked the temperature gauge.

Minus eighty degrees Celsius. The liquid nitrogen jacket was intact. Inside were twenty-four cryo-vials containing the raw spinal fluid and cerebellar tissue samples from the New Rochelle index patient.

"If this burns, everything burns," she whispered, slipping the heavy cylinder into a padded nylon tactical backpack and zipping it shut.

From above, through the concrete ceiling slabs, came the deep, rhythmic thrumming of helicopter rotors. The building shivered as a heavy aircraft touched down on the northern roof deck twelve stories above.

A second later, the laboratory door gave way.

The lower hinge sheared with a sharp crack like a rifle shot, the heavy steel door swinging inward on a single bolt. Dr. Thorne stumbled into the room, his hands outstretched, his jaw working with frantic, clicking speed as he lunged across the stainless-steel tables toward her.

Sarah reached into the chemical safety cabinet beside the freezer, grabbed a two-liter glass jug of concentrated sulfuric acid, and smashed it across the corner of the metal dissection table, hurling the contents directly into Thorne's face and chest.

The chemical reaction was instantaneous. Thick, white, acrid smoke billowed from Thorne's flesh as the acid dissolved tissue and bone on contact. The creature let out a dry, rattling hiss, stumbling sideways into the instrument trays, but it did not fall. Even as the skin on its face melted away to expose the white bone of the maxilla, its hands continued to claw forward blindly.

Before the other three could push through the shattered doorframe, the service door at the rear of the laboratorythe narrow iron hatch leading to the utility liftwas kicked open from the outside.

A man in a heavy tactical plate carrier and a kevlar helmet stepped through the smoke, a Remington 870 shotgun leveled at eye level.

"Down!" Captain Jonah Drake barked.

Sarah dropped flat onto the linoleum floor, covering her head with both hands.

The shotgun roared twice in the enclosed concrete room, the muzzle blasts concussing like thunderclaps. The heavy double-aught buckshot tore through the chest and skull of the two infected nurses, hurling them backward into the corridor in a spray of bone fragments and black fluid.

Drake grabbed Sarah by the shoulder strap of her backpack, hauling her to her feet with one arm. "Dr. Mercer?"

"Yes!" she gasped through her respirator. "I have the isolates!"

"Then we're leaving," Drake said, shoving her toward the service lift where Arthur Pendelton stood holding a smoking carbine. "The lower floors are crawling. We've got two minutes before the roof gets overrun."
