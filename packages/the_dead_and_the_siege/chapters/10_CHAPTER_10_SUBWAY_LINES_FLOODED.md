CHAPTER 10: SUBWAY LINES FLOODED

The Lexington Avenue express tunnel between 42nd Street and 33rd Street was a pitch-black tomb filled with three feet of brackish, foul-smelling runoff water. When the regional electrical grid collapsed, the automatic sump pumps that kept Manhattan's subterranean transit arteries dry had ceased operating, and water from broken municipal mains, sewer overflow, and East River seepage had begun pooling along the track beds.

Marcus led the column through the freezing water, his twelve-pound track wrench held ready at waist height. Behind him came Brenda, carrying a waterproof canvas sack containing the digital relay schematics, followed by young Pete and four transit laborers armed with heavy iron pry-bars and claw hammers.

The only illumination came from the narrow beams of their headlamps, which cut through the thick, yellow fog that rose from the standing water. Every step was an exercise in extreme caution. Beneath the opaque surface of the water lay third-rail insulator caps, shattered wooden ties, discarded brake shoes, and submerged signal cables that could trip a man in an instant.

"Watch the frogs on the track switches," Marcus warned in a low rasp. "You catch a boot in an open switch point and you'll tear your ankle off before you can yank it free."

"Marcus," Pete whispered from the middle of the line, his teeth chattering uncontrollably from the cold water soaking his thighs. "What's that floating up ahead?"

Marcus raised his wrench, signaling a halt.

Thirty yards ahead, where the express tracks crossed the local alignment at 38th Street, the water was choked with debris. A ten-car subway train sat motionless on Track 2, its stainless-steel cars dark and silent, its doors pried open from the outside. Floating in the stagnant water between the train and the tunnel wall were dozens of dark shapes.

At first, they looked like waterlogged winter coats. But as Marcus took two slow steps forward, the beam of his light revealed pale, water-bloated faces submerged just beneath the surface. They were civilianswho had fled into the tunnels when the panic started above, only to be trapped when the water rose.

And then, one of the floating shapes moved.

It did not splash or thrash. A figure in a soaked business suit lifted its head from the black water with a sickening, hydrodynamic slowness. Its hair was plastered to its grey skull, its lips rotted away to reveal blackened gums and jagged teeth. The milky white eyes fixed on the reflection of Marcus's headlamp.

From beneath the water came a low, gurgling hiss as the creature found purchase on the submerged railroad ties, rising onto its feet with unnatural, mechanical power.

Around it, five more bodies broke the surface, their limbs churning through the dark water, displacing thick ripples of oily scum.

"Form a line against the tunnel bench wall!" Marcus commanded, his voice dropping into the cold, authoritative register of a foreman facing a structural collapse. "Do not let them flank us! Brenda, stay behind me!"

The first creature lunged through the waist-deep water with shocking speed, its arms clawing outward, creating a bow-wave of black sludge. It didn't pause to size up the opposition. It threw its entire weight forward, aiming its open jaws directly at Marcus's throat.

Marcus didn't step back. He planted his heavy work boots against the ballast between the ties, dipped his left shoulder, and brought the twelve-pound track wrench around in a compact, devastating horizontal arc.

The solid carbon-steel jaw of the wrench caught the creature squarely on the left temple. The sound in the enclosed tunnel was like an iron sledge striking a wet paving stonea sharp, wet *CRACK* that echoed down the tube. The creature's skull collapsed inward, the kinetic energy spinning its torso entirely around before it splashed heavily into the water and did not rise.

"One down!" Marcus growled, already resetting his grip on the knurled handle. "Take the next one, Pete! Use the pry-bar!"

A second host, wearing the torn uniform of a transit conductor, surged out of the darkness toward Pete. The young man panicked, raising the flare gun and pulling the trigger at point-blank range.

The magnesium flare ignited with a blinding, magnesium-white flash and a deafening roar. In the narrow tunnel, the blast was incandescent, illuminating every damp rivet and weeping crack in the concrete tunnel lining. The burning phosphor projectile struck the conductor in the center of the chest, embedding itself in the sternum, throwing off sparks of blinding white fire and thick, choking sulfur smoke.

The creature shriekednot in pain, but in rage as the heat boiled the fluids in its chest cavity. It continued forward, its arms engulfed in white flame, reaching for Pete's throat.

Before it could touch the boy, Brenda stepped forward from the bench wall, driving the pointed end of a five-foot steel crowbar directly through the creature's eye socket into the cranial vault.

The creature stiffened instantly, its motor functions short-circuiting as the steel severed the brainstem. It collapsed into the water, the magnesium flare hissing furiously beneath the surface, turning the dark water around their knees into a bubbling, luminous cauldron of emerald green.

"Move!" Marcus yelled, waving the surviving transit workers forward through the smoke. "The flare is going to draw everything within five stations! Move toward the 33rd Street ventilation shaft!"
