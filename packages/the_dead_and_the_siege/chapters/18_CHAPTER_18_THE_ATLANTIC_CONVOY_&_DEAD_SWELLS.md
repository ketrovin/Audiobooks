CHAPTER 18: THE ATLANTIC CONVOY & DEAD SWELLS

Dawn over the Atlantic Ocean did not bring light. It brought a pale, sickly slate-grey hue that bled into the low ceiling of sea fog.

By ten in the morning, the task force was underway, steaming south at a steady fourteen knots along the twenty-fathom curve off the New Jersey coastline. Ahead of the *San Antonio* rode the guided-missile destroyer USS *Mason*, her grey phased-array radar faces scanning the empty horizon, while two hundred yards off the starboard quarter, the amphibious assault ship USS *Kearsarge* sliced through the grey combers, her vast eight-hundred-foot flight deck lined with tied-down transport helicopters and Harrier jump jets.

From the observation bridge on the *San Antonio*'s superstructure, Arthur Pendelton stood beside Marcus Vance, looking through heavy naval binoculars toward the distant shoreline.

The coast of New Jersey was a panorama of silent disaster. Plumes of black smoke rose from the refineries at Bayonne, the storage tanks at Perth Amboy, and the coastal towns along the barrier islands. Every few minutes, the ship's tactical VHF radio receiver hissed with automated distress loops recorded hours before:

"...this is Coast Guard Station Barnegat... we have been overrun... fuel dock is compromised... any vessel hearing this, maintain fifty miles offshore... repeat, stay offshore..."

"...pleasure craft *Sea Fox*, taking on water near Atlantic City... passengers are biting... Somebody help us, they're breaking through the cabin hatch..."

Arthur adjusted the focus ring on the optics. Two miles off their starboard beam, an eighty-foot commercial sea-clam dredge wallowed silently in the troughs of the waves, her diesel engine dead, her outriggers dragging in the salt water.

On the wooden foredeck of the fishing boat, three figures were pacing in tight, repetitive circles. Through the high-magnification lenses, Arthur could see their torn flannel shirts and grey, water-bleached skin. One of them stopped at the gunwale, staring with milky, cataract-covered eyes at the passing grey hull of the naval transport. It did not wave. It did not shout. It simply stood in the freezing spray, its jaw working with that dry, rhythmic clatter that Arthur could still hear in his nightmares.

"Ghost ships," Marcus said, his voice a low rumble beside Arthur. "Hundreds of them. People panicked, ran to the marinas, jumped into whatever boat had fuel in the tank, and sailed out into the open ocean without navigation charts or drinking water. Then, someone on board turned."

"A boat is just a floating room with no back door," Arthur said, lowering the glasses. "Once the virus takes root in a wheelhouse, there's nowhere to run except into forty-degree salt water."

The steel deck beneath their boots vibrated as the destroyer *Mason* altered course five degrees to starboard, her high-pressure turbine wake boiling into white foam.

On the bridge console, the tactical intercom squawked. "Bridge, Combat Information Center. Surface radar has a slow-moving contact bearing one-eight-two, range six miles. Transponder is squawking civilian emergency code 7700. Vessel appears dead in the water directly in the mouth of Delaware Bay."
