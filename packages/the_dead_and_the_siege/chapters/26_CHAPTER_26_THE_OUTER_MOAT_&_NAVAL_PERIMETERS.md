CHAPTER 26: THE OUTER MOAT & NAVAL PERIMETERS

By the second week of November, Fort Jefferson had ceased to be an ancient military relic and had become a fully modernized, self-sustaining coastal bastion.

Every morning at dawn, Arthur Pendelton walked the top tier of the curtain wall, his heavy wool uniform coat replaced by lightweight khaki utility trousers and a faded navy-blue Port Authority work shirt. Across his shoulder hung a standard-issue M4 carbine, its barrel kept spotless and lightly oiled against the corrosive Gulf salt spray.

Below him, the seventy-foot-wide tidal moat was circulating clean, turquoise ocean water through the massive granite sluice gates. Marcus Vance and a team of thirty civilian tradesmen had cleared forty years of silt and dead coral from the intake channels, restoring the natural tidal flushing mechanism designed by Army engineers in 1861. Twice a day, the high tide swept millions of gallons of oxygenated salt water through the moat, keeping the depth at an unvarying eight feet over a bed of crushed coral and sharp granite boulders.

"Look at that current, Arthur," Marcus said, walking up the brick ramp to join him on the terreplein. Marcus carried a grease gun in one hand and his trusty track wrench in the other. "Six knots through the sluice. Anything that tries to wade through that moat without a boat gets swept straight out through the southern discharge channel into eighty fathoms of open sea."

"And what if they don't wade?" Arthur asked, nodding toward the western horizon.

Two miles to the west, riding at anchor in the deep-water roadstead, the guided-missile destroyer *Mason* and the assault carrier *Kearsarge* maintained continuous surface radar surveillance. Every six hours, a pair of SH-60 Seahawk helicopters flew lazy, circular search patterns over the coral shoals, their dipping sonar and forward-looking infrared cameras scanning the waters between Key West and the Dry Tortugas.

"The destroyers are our outer ring," Marcus said, wiping grease from his knuckles with an oily rag. "Nothing bigger than a rowboat gets within five miles of Garden Key without Captain Thomas Vance knowing about it. But we aren't relying on fuel to keep us alive. Come see what Brenda finished in the central parade ground."

They descended the massive granite spiral staircase of Bastion 2 into the lush, open interior of the fort.

The sixteen-acre parade ground had been transformed into an agricultural marvel. Two hundred rectangular raised beds, constructed from salvaged ship timbers and filled with a mixture of native sand, composted seaweed, and nutrient-rich soil brought from the *San Antonio*'s holds, were already sprouting rows of sweet potatoes, bush beans, and dark green collard greens. Above the cisterns, an array of three hundred high-efficiency solar photovoltaic panels gleamed in the intense tropical sun, silently feeding twenty-four large commercial inverter banks that powered the reverse-osmosis desalination plant.

Inside the shaded brick casemates of the northern curtain, Dr. Sarah Mercer had established her permanent viral synthesis laboratory. The room was cool, chilled by an autonomous heat-pump system venting through an ancient smoke flue, its counters lined with stainless-steel centrifuges, liquid nitrogen storage dewars, and digital sequencing monitors.

"Arthur, Marcus," Sarah called out, stepping through the lab's canvas entry curtain, removing her protective goggles. "You need to see the latest marine degradation data."

Arthur walked over to the illuminated bench. "What did the lab in Norfolk confirm?"

Sarah held up a glass petri dish containing a pale, calcified fragment of neural tissue. "The hyper-saline environment of the Straits of Florida accelerates viral lysis by eight hundred percent compared to freshwater lakes or rivers. When an infected host is submerged in ocean water with a salinity above thirty-five parts per thousand, the lipid bilayer of the viral capsid begins saponifying within twelve hours. By forty-eight hours, the neuromuscular connections in the motor cortex are completely severed. In the deep sea, the virus literally drowns itself in salt."

"That's good news for the beaches," Marcus said.

"It is," Sarah said gravely. "Provided the hosts remain submerged in deep water. But if they are carried on floating wreckage, barges, or derelict hulls where they stay dry, the virus remains viable indefinitely. And there is a storm coming."

Arthur stepped to the casemate's gun embrasure, looking out at the sky to the south.

The deep azure blue of the morning had faded into a dull, yellowish haze. Far to the south, over the western tip of Cuba, a massive wall of dark, bruised clouds was slowly rising, its anvil tops reaching forty thousand feet into the stratosphere. The barometric pressure on the wall-mounted brass barometer had dropped six millibars in the last three hours.

"Hurricane season isn't over," Arthur muttered, his instincts honed by three decades of watching coastal weather radar at JFK. "That's a tropical cyclone, and it's tracking straight up the Yucatan Channel."
