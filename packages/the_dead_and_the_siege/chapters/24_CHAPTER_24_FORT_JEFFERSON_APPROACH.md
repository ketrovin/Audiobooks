CHAPTER 24: FORT JEFFERSON APPROACH

The first sighting of Fort Jefferson took Arthur Pendelton's breath away.

Rising directly out of the crystalline turquoise shallows of the Gulf of Mexico, seventy miles from the nearest mainland road, sat a colossal, hexagonal fortress of sixteen million solid red bricks. Built in the mid-nineteenth century to guard the maritime gateway between the Atlantic Ocean and the Gulf of Mexico, the fortress was a military marvel: half a mile in perimeter, its eight-foot-thick walls towering fifty feet above the sea, pierced by hundreds of arched brick gun embrasures across three vertical tiers.

Surrounding the entire fortress was an eighty-foot-wide tidal moat, separated from the open sea by a massive, granite-capped counterscarp seawall that kept the crashing Gulf combers from eroding the foundation. Inside its sixteen acres of open parade grounds sat green casuarina trees, brick storehouses, and a massive rainwater catchment system capable of storing one point five million gallons of fresh water in subterranean brick cisterns.

The USS *San Antonio* and the USS *Kearsarge* slowed to dead-slow, dropping heavy steel anchors into the deep-water channel two hundred yards off the fortress's wooden coaling wharf.

"Look at that masonry," Marcus Vance whispered, his hands gripping the bridge railing as he stared at the immense red walls. "Sixteen million bricks. Laid by master masons using hydraulic lime mortar that gets harder the more salt water it absorbs. No subway tunnels. No sewer mains. No electrical grids to short out. Just solid stone and deep ocean."

"And no way in except across that moat," Arthur added, watching the clear water swirl through the tidal sluice gates.

A flight of two Navy utility craft descended from the *Kearsarge*, carrying the advance engineering detachment, Dr. Mercer, Arthur, Marcus, Brenda, and Captain Jonah Drake toward the wooden wharf.

As the boat touched the pilings, the silence of the Dry Tortugas washed over them.

It was not the dead, suffocating silence of the frozen Cape Breton woods or the ruined streets of Manhattan. It was the ancient, living silence of the sea: the gentle lap of turquoise waves against the brick counterscarp, the cry of sooty terns wheeling against the bright tropical sky, and the warm, steady rush of the Gulf trade wind through the palm leaves.

On the wooden wharf stood three National Park Service rangers in faded green uniforms, their faces weathered and dark from the Florida sun, holding vintage bolt-action rifles across their chests.

The lead ranger, a grey-bearded man named Henderson, stepped forward, his eyes scanning the armed naval party.

"State your business," Henderson said, his voice raspy from disuse.

Captain Jonah Drake stepped onto the wooden planks, his boots ringing on the weathered pine. "United States Naval Task Force 8. We have twelve hundred civilian evacuees, five hundred naval personnel, and the primary viral research detachment from Bellevue Hospital. We are declaring Fort Jefferson the sovereign continuity redoubt of the Atlantic theater."

Henderson looked past Drake, out toward the two massive grey warships riding at anchor in the deep-water channel, their flight decks bristling with helicopters and their deck guns trained on the empty horizon.

A slow, tired smile broke through the ranger's grey beard.

"You're late, Captain," Henderson said, lowering his rifle. "We've been saving sixteen million bricks for you. Welcome to the end of the world."
