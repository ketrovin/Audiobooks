CHAPTER 12: DATA WIPE

They scrambled onto the flat gravel roof of the municipal annex, breathing the raw, cold salt air of the surface. Above them, the night sky was torn by sheets of sleet, but here on the roof, eighty feet above 33rd Street, they had a terrifying, panoramic view of Midtown Manhattan.

The city was an empire of ruins.

Below them on 33rd Street, the avenue was completely clogged with thousands of infected, a churning, dark ocean of bodies flowing toward the burning ruins of Pennsylvania Station. The air was filled with the deafening roar of burning diesel fuel and the distant, rolling thunder of structural collapses.

"Over here!" Brenda called out, running to the western edge of the roof.

Set into the brick parapet wall of the annex was a reinforced steel equipment shed marked: `MTA REGIONAL FIBER RELAY 4-A`.

Inside the shed were four tall equipment racks housing the fiber optic switches and telemetry data relays that connected the automated signal systems of the Long Island Rail Road, Metro-North, and the New York City subway system. The servers were still humming on an independent battery array, thousands of tiny green and amber LEDs blinking across the faceplates.

"Drake told me about this relay," Brenda said, pulling a heavy cordless impact driver from her canvas tool sack. "The military command in Norfolk needs these logs wiped before the regional grid completely collapses. If the host networks in foreign hands or automated protocols get hold of the passenger manifest data and routing logs, they can calculate every subterranean evacuation corridor between here and the harbor."

"How long will it take to wipe the drives?" Marcus asked, standing guard at the roof hatch, his wrench held across his chest.

"Too long if I do a software scrub," Brenda said, kicking the battery rack open. "We do it the physical way."

She grabbed a five-gallon plastic jug of industrial transformer dielectric fluid that was stored in the corner of the shed for cooling repairs. She unscrewed the cap and dumped the heavy, petroleum-based fluid directly over the circuit boards, the server motherboards, and the backup tape drives, soaking every rack in clear, flammable oil.

"Pete," Marcus called. "You still got a round in that flare gun?"

Pete looked down at the orange plastic launcher in his trembling hand. "One white signal star left, Marcus."

"Step back, Brenda," Marcus ordered.

Brenda stepped out of the equipment shed, hauling her tools clear. Pete stepped up to the open doorway, leveled the flare gun into the soaked server racks, and pulled the trigger.

The flare struck the center rack with an incandescent flash. The dielectric fluid ignited instantly with a deep, muffled *WHOOSH*, sending a pillar of white-hot chemical fire roaring through the shed. Within seconds, the aluminum server casings were warping and melting under three thousand degrees of heat, the circuit boards dissolving into slag, taking every passenger manifest, routing log, and regional transit secret with them.

From the street below, hundreds of heads snapped upward as the light of the fire reflected against the low clouds.

"They saw the flash," Marcus said, looking over the parapet. "They're moving into the annex stairwells. We have two minutes."

High above them, slicing through the sleet, came the thundering roar of helicopter rotors.

A Bell 412 utility helicopter, its navigation lights dark, dropped down through the cloud deck over 34th Street, its searchlight flashing twice in a tight, double-pulse pattern.

"That's Drake!" Pete yelled, waving his hardhat in the air. "That's Alpha-1!"
