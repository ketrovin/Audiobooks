CHAPTER 2: PRESSURIZED CABINS

The central operations room of JFK Command Center Delta smelled of ozone, burnt thermal printer paper, and cold coffee that had sat in ceramic mugs for nine hours. Located in the reinforced bunker beneath the central control tower, Delta had no exterior windows, no natural light, and no access to the coastal wind that was now howling across the runways. Its walls were lined with banks of CRT monitors, rack-mounted transponder processors, and heavy steel consoles painted military battleship grey.

Captain Jonah Drake sat at the edge of the tactical plotting desk, his sleeves rolled to his forearms, revealing thick bands of corded tendon and an old shrapnel scar running from his left wrist to his elbow. Drake was forty-four, a former marine combat engineer who had spent the last six years commanding the regional Rapid Emergency Action and Containment TeamR.E.A.C.T. His team was not a civilian police unit; they were trained for chemical train derailments, airport fuel depot explosions, and hostage barricades.

None of their training manuals had contained a single sentence about what was currently unfolding across the radar screens.

"Give me the northern approach corridor again," Drake ordered, his voice steady, flat, stripped of all inflection.

At the main terminal radar console, Technician Miller rubbed his red-rimmed eyes. His hands were shaking on the trackball. "Captain, Radar Sector 4 is dark. The beacon transponders on three commercial heavies inbound from Montreal and Boston stopped reporting altitude five minutes ago. They aren't answering radio calls on the regional emergency frequencies."

"Are they airborne or down?" Drake asked.

"Airborne, sir," Miller said, pointing a trembling finger at three green primary blips drifting slowly south across the radar sweep without identifying data blocks. "Primary radar still paints skin reflections. They're at eight thousand feet, descending through the cloud deck on standard runway 22 visual approach paths, but their transponder squawks are dead. And their flight paths are erratic. One of them is banking twenty degrees off centerline."

The steel blast door at the rear of Command Center Delta hissed open, and Arthur Pendelton stepped inside, accompanied by Bobby Harris. Both men were spattered with rain, jet fuel mist, and the dark, foul-smelling residue from the tarmac encounter at Gate 42.

Drake looked up, his grey eyes narrowing as he took in the dark stains on Arthur's uniform. "Arthur. Report. Did you secure the Beechcraft?"

Arthur walked over to the console, placed his heavy peaked cap on the metal desk, and leaned both hands against the edge. "The Beechcraft was dead before it touched the runway, Drake. Three passengers inside were ripped to shreds. The pilot locked himself in the avionics bay forward. When Collins put a round through the one in row four, it didn't bleed red. It bled black grease, like old differential fluid. And it didn't feel pain. It took an aluminum strike to the neck that would have shattered a bull's spine, and it just kept biting."

The room went dead silent. The only sound was the high-pitched whine of the cooling fans in the server racks and the clicking of the ceiling ventilation louvers.

Miller swallowed hard. "Captain... Terminal 1 just lost internal power. Port Authority security at Terminal 1 is reporting hundreds of civilians breaking through the customs barrier into the baggage processing tunnels. They're saying the passengers off the European arrivals aren't sickthey're hunting."

Drake walked over to the tactical weapons locker set into the reinforced concrete wall of the bunker. He entered a six-digit code into the electronic keypad, turned the heavy chrome wheel, and swung the two-inch-thick steel door open. Inside hung rows of short-barreled Colt carbines, heavy tactical shotguns, ceramic chest rigs, and olive-drab crates of standard twelve-gauge buckshot.

"Listen to me," Drake said, turning to face the room. His voice cut through the ambient machine hum like a cold steel blade. "Whatever this pathogen is, it isn't an airborne respiratory agent. If it were airborne, every operator in this bunker would already be convulsing on the floor. It is transmitted by direct fluid contactbites, deep tissue tears, arterial exposure. The latency period is under four minutes. That means containment is impossible once a pressurized cabin lands and discharges into a concourse."

"What about the three inbound planes?" Miller asked, his voice cracking. "The ones on primary radar?"

"They aren't landing," Drake said grimly. "If those cabins are compromised, each of those fuselages is a sealed container carrying two hundred infected hosts straight into our runway perimeter. Miller, can you override the runway approach lighting arrays?"

Miller tapped at his console. "I can cut the main transformer feed from Substation 3. It kills the runway centerlines, the visual glide slope indicators, and the high-intensity approach strobes."

"Do it," Drake ordered. "Douse the lights. Every runway, every taxiway. Turn JFK into a black hole from ten thousand feet. If those pilots still have manual control, they divert out over the Atlantic or belly-flop onto the beaches. If they don't have control, they won't hit our terminal roofs."

Miller's fingers hesitated for half a second over the protected toggle switches. Then, he flipped the three guarded switches down in rapid succession.

Through the subterranean floor plates, a deep, resonant *CLACK* echoed as the massive high-voltage circuit breakers in Substation 3 tripped open. Across five thousand acres of concrete runways, marshes, and taxiways, every incandescent lamp and LED strobe died simultaneously, plunging the airfield into total, impenetrable Atlantic darkness.

Almost immediately, the emergency tactical radio speaker mounted above the plotting desk crackled with a burst of static, followed by a voice filled with raw, unvarnished panic.

"Mayday, Mayday, Mayday! This is Flight 814, Boeing 777 heavy, inbound runway 22! We have lost visual reference! We have cabin breach forward, repeat, cabin breach! Flight deck door is giving way! We are losing hydraulic pressure on the primary controls! Someone shut the runway lights off! Where are the lights?!"

The audio dissolved into a horrifying crescendo of metal shrieking, glass shattering, and the distinct, guttural sound of dozens of jaws clicking frantically against the cabin microphone.

Then, the carrier wave went dead.

On the radar sweep, the green primary blip of Flight 814 dipped sharply downward, crossing the southern perimeter fence near Rockaway Boulevard, before vanishing entirely below the radar horizon.

A second later, the reinforced concrete floor of Command Center Delta lifted two inches and slammed back down with a dull, subterranean concussion that rattled the teeth in Arthur Pendelton's jaw. Deep within the bunker walls, the light fixtures swung on their steel conduit pipes, casting dancing shadows across the grey consoles.

Drake didn't flinch. He racked the bolt on a heavy Remington shotgun, chambering a green three-inch magnum shell with a sharp, oily *CLACK-CLACK*.

"Arthur," Drake said, looking directly into the old supervisor's eyes. "The perimeter is gone. Flight 814 just came down on the cargo apron. We have five minutes before whatever crawled out of that wreckage reaches this bunker door. Suit up, or you die on this concrete."
