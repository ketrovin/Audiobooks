CHAPTER 11: SCRAPING THE VENTS

The emergency ventilation shaft at 33rd Street was an eighty-foot vertical chimney of reinforced concrete, originally built in 1912 to vent coal fumes from the steam locomotives below. Inside the chimney, a rusted iron spiral staircase clung to the curved wall, climbing from the track level all the way to an exhaust housing situated on the flat gravel roof of a municipal utility annex on 33rd Street.

Marcus stood at the base of the stairs, shining his light up the shaft.

High above them, twenty feet from the top, the massive steel intake louvers were shaking. The sound of metal scraping against metal was deafeningan unrelenting, rhythmic screech as hundreds of fingernails, boot heels, and metal tools scraped against the heavy exterior grating on the street outside.

"They're on the surface directly above us," Brenda said, wiping black soot from her cheek as she reached the bottom landing. "That louver vents to the sidewalk right outside the post office. If they break those steel slats, they'll fall right down this chimney onto our heads."

"The louvers are two-inch angle iron, bolted into three-foot masonry," Marcus said, inspecting the base of the iron spiral staircase. "They won't break the steel. But if enough bodies pile onto the grate, their weight will shear the mounting anchors."

"Marcus," Pete said, looking down the dark tunnel they had just escaped. "Listen."

From the subterranean tube behind them came a sound that froze the blood in their veins. It was not the splashing of five or six hosts. It was the sound of a rushing subterranean rivera thundering, rhythmic churn of hundreds of bodies sprinting through the knee-deep water, their collective hissing echoing through the tunnel like the sound of escaping high-pressure steam.

"Up the stairs!" Marcus ordered, shoving Pete and the laborers onto the iron risers. "Do not stop until you hit the roof hatch! Brenda, go!"

They began the climb. The iron staircase was narrow, barely two feet wide, its steps coated in a slick film of wet soot and mineral deposits. With every step, the rusted bolts holding the staircase to the concrete wall groaned in protest, flakes of red rust raining down into the black water below.

Marcus took the rear. As he climbed past the thirty-foot mark, the first wave of infected reached the base of the shaft.

Looking down through the center of the spiral, Marcus saw a churning mass of pale, upturned faces illuminated by the green glow of the dying flare in the tunnel. They did not pause at the water's edge. They hit the bottom of the spiral staircase with the force of an avalanche, clawing at the risers, trampling those who lost their footing, climbing over one another to reach the stairs.

The iron staircase shuddered violently. A mounting bracket at the ten-foot level sheared with a loud, metallic *BANG*, the lower section of the stairs sagging outward into empty space.

"Keep climbing!" Marcus bellowed. "Double time!"

A hand caught the heel of Marcus's right boot.

Marcus looked down. An infected track worker, his jaw hanging by a shred of muscle, had pulled himself onto the riser directly below. His fingers were clamped around the thick rubber lug sole of Marcus's boot, pulling downward with terrifying leverage.

Marcus braced his back against the curved concrete wall of the chimney, raised his left leg, and drove the heavy steel head of his track wrench straight down like a pile driver.

The hexagonal jaw crushed the creature's fingers, severing two digits against the iron step. The host didn't scream; it simply opened its broken mouth wider and lunged forward with its other hand, trying to bury its teeth into Marcus's shin.

Marcus swung the wrench again, this time driving the flat face into the top of the skull with all the force of his broad shoulders. The bone gave way with a sickening, hollow pop. The creature lost all muscle tone instantly, slipping off the step and plummeting thirty feet down into the center of the swarm, knocking three more off the sagging staircase as it fell.

Marcus turned and hauled himself up the remaining steps, taking them two at a time, his chest burning as if he had swallowed broken glass.

At the top landing, Brenda and Pete were already throwing their weight against the heavy steel roof hatch.

"It's chained from the outside!" Pete screamed in panic, beating his fists against the iron plate. "Someone padlocked it!"

"Out of the way!" Marcus roared, reaching the top platform.

He wedged the twelve-pound carbon-steel jaw of his track wrench between the hatch rim and the iron padlock shackle. Bracing his legs against the masonry wall, he threw his entire two-hundred-pound frame into the lever arm.

The steel groans. The leverage of the thirty-six-inch wrench was immense. With a sharp, explosive *SNAP*, the brass padlock body sheared in half, and Marcus threw the heavy hatch upward into the freezing October night.
