CHAPTER 3: TELEMETRY LOSS

The explosion on the cargo apron sent a pillar of orange flame three hundred feet into the low October overcast, illuminating the wetlands of Jamaica Bay with a toxic, flickering glow. Black smoke, thick with the stench of burning composite plastics, rubber tires, and jet fuel, rolled across the concrete runways in rolling waves, carried by the thirty-knot coastal wind.

Inside Command Center Delta, the air filtration system began to wheeze, its pre-filters clogging with airborne soot.

"Primary power from the Queens grid is completely severed," Technician Miller announced, his face illuminated only by the dull amber glow of the backup battery indicators. "We are on emergency diesel generators. Fuel reserves in the underground bunker tanks will give us forty-eight hours of juice, provided the coolant lines don't get contaminated."

"Forget forty-eight hours," Drake said, tightening the nylon straps of his tactical plate carrier. "In forty-eight minutes, the perimeter fence won't exist. Arthur, where is the nearest ground transport that isn't parked in the public lots?"

Arthur hauled a heavy tactical vest over his wool uniform, buckling the side clips with practiced fingers. "Sub-level 2 beneath the central heating plant. Port Authority keeps four armored riot control vans and two diesel utility trucks on high-clearance chassis. They're fueled and locked behind heavy steel roll-up gates."

"Good," Drake said. "Miller, pack the hard drive backups for the regional air traffic telemetry and the terminal surveillance logs. If there's any operational authority left in the state after tonight, they'll need to know where the breach vectors started."

Miller nodded numbly, unscrewing the heavy thumbscrews on the rack-mounted storage arrays, yanking the solid-state drive cartridges and stuffing them into a reinforced waterproof pelican case.

"Captain!" Bobby Harris shouted from the door observation slit. "Look at the perimeter monitor for Concourse A!"

Drake stepped to the monitor. Concourse A was the international arrivals terminal. On the monochrome screen, the exterior glass walla sixty-foot-wide facade of tempered architectural glasswas bowing inward under enormous, collective pressure. Beyond the glass, the dark tarmac was swarming. Thousands of figures were moving through the smoke, emerging from the burning wreckage of the 777 and from the abandoned maintenance hangars. They were not wandering aimlessly. They were moving toward the illuminated emergency exit signs with singular, terrifying purpose, drawn by the sound of the emergency backup sirens.

With a silent, sickening shudder on the monitor, the entire sixty-foot glass wall shattered. A tidal wave of bodies poured over the aluminum frames, trampling one another without pausing, surging into the baggage claim halls like floodwater bursting through a rotted wooden levee.

"They don't stop for glass," Harris whispered, his voice trembling. "They were crawling over broken shards three inches thick, dragging their stomachs across the frames, and they didn't even slow down."

"Pain receptors are dead," Arthur said coldly, checking the slide on an issued Glock 19. "The nerve endings are firing on raw motor impulses. You can't intimidate them. You can't bargain with them. You destroy the brainstem, or they keep crawling until their arms fall off."

Drake slung his shotgun and picked up his field radio. "Alpha Team, this is Drake. Status on the rooftop pad?"

The radio sputtered with heavy static, but the voice of Sergeant Kowalski came through, clear and clipped. "Drake, we have two Bell 412 utility helicopters on the tower roof pad. Rotors are tied down, but tanks are at ninety percent. We're holding the roof access stairwell, but the lower floors of the tower are filling up fast with evacuees and infected. If you're coming up, you need to move now."

"Hold the roof, Kowalski," Drake said. "We are extracting via the service elevator to the roof deck. Have both birds spooled up on battery power. We're five minutes out."

"Copy that, Captain. Don't linger. The wind off the bay is picking up, and the smoke is dropping visibility to fifty feet."

Drake turned to Arthur, Harris, and Miller. "Miller, take point behind me. Arthur, you and Harris take the rear. Anything that moves in the stairwell that doesn't answer a verbal challenge gets a round through the skull. Move."

They breached the reinforced door of Command Center Delta, stepping out into the subterranean service corridor that ran beneath the airport's central terminal. The corridor was lit only by flickering red emergency strobes. Water from broken fire sprinkler pipes was already pooling on the concrete floor, six inches deep, carrying floating plastic cups, discarded baggage tags, and thin slicks of engine oil.

From the darkness ahead came a sound that chilled Arthur Pendelton to the marrow of his bones: the collective, wet dragging of hundreds of shoeless feet against the wet concrete, accompanied by that dry, wooden clicking of snapping jaws.

"Form up," Drake whispered, raising his carbine. "Keep your footing. Keep moving."
