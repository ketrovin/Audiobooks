CHAPTER 5: FIRST AVENUE GRIDLOCK

At street level on First Avenue, the collapse of civilization had not happened in silence. It had happened in an ear-splitting, grinding cacophony of blaring car horns, shattering safety glass, and the relentless, mechanical wail of abandoned car alarms whose batteries were slowly bleeding down to nothing.

Thomas Kincaid gripped the steering wheel of FDNY Ambulance 12 so hard his knuckles had turned white beneath his blue nitrile gloves. He was a veteran paramedic of twenty-two years, his face lined with the deep grooves of a man who had seen every conceivable manifestation of human physical trauma. But nothing in his career had prepared him for the three-mile-long graveyard of metal and glass that stretched from the Midtown Tunnel portal all the way south to the entrance of Bellevue Hospital.

"We can't push through, Tom," his partner, Elena Cruz, said from the passenger seat. She was twenty-four, her hair pulled back into a tight ponytail, her hands trembling as she held a heavy oxygen tank across her knees like a club. "The intersection at 34th is blocked by a city bus that jumped the median and crushed two cabs. The street is completely wedged."

Ambulance 12 sat idling in the center lane of First Avenue, its red and white emergency strobes casting frantic, rhythmic reflections against the soot-stained brick tenements and the dark storefront windows. Outside the reinforced windows of the cab, the sidewalk was a churning mass of human terror. Thousands of residents were fleeing south toward the Brooklyn Bridge, carrying garbage bags stuffed with clothes, dragging crying children, or pushing shopping carts loaded with canned food.

And behind them, weaving through the stalled vehicles with sickening agility, came the infected.

They were fast. In the cold night air, the virus did not make them shamble; it drove their nervous systems into a hyper-metabolic frenzy. A woman in a torn cashmere overcoat leaped onto the hood of a stalled BMW three car lengths ahead of the ambulance, her fingers bloody stumps where the nails had been torn away from clawing at windshields. She hurled herself against the glass of the BMW, smashing it with her forehead, ignoring the blood pouring down her face as she reached inside to drag the screaming driver through the steering wheel.

"Tom, put it in reverse!" Elena screamed, pointing to the sidewalk. "They see our lights!"

"The transmission is binding!" Kincaid grunted, hauling on the gearshift lever. The heavy diesel engine groaned, the rear dual wheels spinning on a patch of wet garbage and broken glass before catching traction. "Hold on!"

He backed the four-ton ambulance into a delivery alley between a dry cleaner and a bodega, the steel rear bumper crushing an iron trash bin against the brick wall with a deafening metallic screech. He killed the emergency strobes, leaving only the dim amber parking lights burning.

Through the rain-streaked windshield, they watched the swarm sweep down First Avenue. Hundreds of figures, their clothing shredded, their eyes reflecting the distant fires of Queens like the eyes of predatory wolves, surged down the asphalt. They did not attack each other. They moved with a terrifying, coordinated momentum, flowing around the stalled cars, tearing open doors that had been left unlocked, pulling survivors into the street with a relentless, mechanical efficiency.

"Where is the National Guard?" Elena whispered, tears tracking through the soot on her cheeks. "The radio said the 69th Infantry was deploying from the Lexington Avenue Armory at six o'clock."

"The 69th never made it out of the motor pool," Kincaid said quietly, pointing up into the dark sky over the East River.

Through the low rain clouds, two twin-turbine helicopters flew low beneath the cloud deck, their searchlights slicing through the mist as they crossed from the Brooklyn shoreline toward the massive, multi-tiered complex of Bellevue Hospital four blocks to the south.

"That's military," Kincaid said, watching the navigation strobes blink against the clouds. "They're landing on the hospital roof."

"Bellevue is walled off, Tom," Elena said. "They locked the perimeter gates on First Avenue three hours ago. The security guards were firing over the crowd with shotguns."

"Then we don't go through the gates," Kincaid said, reaching behind his seat to pull out a heavy iron halligan bar and a flat-head fire axe. "We go through the subterranean maintenance tunnel that connects the hospital heating plant to the East River water intake station. My brother worked stationary engineer at Bellevue for ten years. He showed me the flood gates during Hurricane Sandy."

He shoved the axe into Elena's hands. "Take this. If anything gets between you and the service door, don't aim for the chest. Aim for the bridge of the nose. Put everything you have into the swing."

They pushed open the ambulance doors and slipped out into the freezing October rain. The air smelled of wet ashes, copper, and the cold mud of the East River. Keeping their backs pressed against the brick walls of the alley, they moved south toward the hospital, their boots splashing silently through the dark puddles, while out on First Avenue, the screaming of the doomed city slowly drowned beneath the relentless drumming of the storm.
