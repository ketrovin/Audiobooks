CHAPTER 9: THE TRANSFORMER VAULT

The air seventy feet beneath the marble concourse of Grand Central Terminal did not smell like the city above. It smelled of ozone, ninety years of lubricating oil baked into damp Manhattan schist, and the sharp, mineral tang of electrical arcing. This was Sub-level M-42, the secret rotary converter substation that had powered the New York Central railroad tracks since the days of the steam locomotives. Even during the height of the Cold War, its exact location had been removed from city blueprints.

Marcus Vance stood in the narrow gap between two massive, twelve-foot-tall rotary converters, his left hand resting on the cold, oil-slicked casting of a Westinghouse cast-iron housing. Marcus was forty-nine years old, a master track inspector for MTA Metro-North whose family had worked the subterranean tracks beneath New York for three generations. He was broad-shouldered, with thick forearms covered in old grease burns and a jaw that looked like it had been carved from a block of granite. In his right hand he carried his trademark tool: a thirty-six-inch, twelve-pound carbon-steel track wrench, its heavy hexagonal jaws polished bright from decades of tightening rail bolts.

Beside him, kneeling over an open copper busbar trench, was Brenda Cole. Brenda was thirty-six, a senior signaling engineer whose jumpsuit was smeared with grey anti-corrosive grease. Her short-cropped dark hair was tucked beneath an orange hardhat, and a battery-powered headlamp cast a sharp white beam over the high-voltage knife switches.

"The third-rail feed from Long Island City dropped completely ten minutes ago, Marcus," Brenda said, her voice tight but remarkably steady. "The frequency meters are reading flat zero. The only juice left in these lines is coming from our emergency nickel-iron battery bank in Vault 4."

Marcus spat a speck of tobacco onto the concrete floor. "How much time does that bank buy us on the ventilation blowers?"

"Forty minutes if we run all six fans," Brenda said, consulting a cracked digital multimeter. "Maybe an hour and a half if we cut the intake blowers for the lower train shed and only circulate air through the machine room and the western egress tunnel."

"Cut the train shed," Marcus said without hesitation. "Nothing's coming down those tracks from the Bronx anymore anyway. The dispatch radio went silent at three o'clock after the commuter express out of Croton-Harmon derailed inside the Park Avenue tunnel."

"Did anyone get out?" Brenda asked, looking up from the trench.

Marcus looked at the heavy steel watertight bulkhead door that sealed Sub-level M-42 from the active subway tubes. The steel door was three inches thick, secured by eight dogging levers. For the last twenty minutes, something had been striking the other side of that doora rhythmic, wet impact, like heavy sacks of wet grain being dropped from a crane.

"They got out of the train," Marcus said, his voice flat. "The problem is what came down the tunnel behind them. They aren't passengers anymore, Brenda. I saw them through the inspection scope at Track 32. Their eyes are milk, their jaws don't close right, and they were tearing into the signal maintainer's crew with their bare hands. They aren't looking for a platform. They're following the vibration of our generator pumps."

A young transit worker named Pete, barely twenty-one years old, sat on an overturned wooden cable spool near the battery racks, clutching a twelve-gauge flare gun in his trembling hands. "Marcus, my brother lives on 46th Street. I tried calling his landline. The operators are gone. Everything's busy. Can we go up to the main concourse? Just to check the streets?"

Marcus walked over and put a heavy hand on the boy's shoulder. His grip was firm, grounding. "Listen to me, Pete. The main concourse is a death trap. That ceiling is sixty feet high with four sets of monumental glass doors. Once the street crowd pushed through those doors, the infected followed them right down the marble stairs. If you go up there, you won't make it twenty feet across the Vanderbilt hall. Our only way out is through the subterranean utility conduits south to the East River."

A heavy metallic scrape echoed through the concrete chamber.

Brenda stood up immediately, shining her headlamp toward the ceiling. High above the rotary converters, near the massive iron intake louvers that vented to the sidewalk grates on 42nd Street, the steel grating was flexing. Through the iron slats, black liquid was dripping down, sizzling as it struck the hot casing of the transformer coils below.

"Marcus," Brenda whispered, pointing the beam upward. "They found the intake vents."
