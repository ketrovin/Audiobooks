CHAPTER 27: THE DRIFTING FLOTILLA

By noon the following day, the sea had turned from crystalline turquoise to a dark, restless bottle-green. Long, oily groundswells, six feet high, were rolling in from the south with an ominous, hypnotic rhythm, smashing against the fort's granite counterscarp in rolling explosions of white foam.

On the bridge of the USS *San Antonio*, Captain Thomas Vance held a conference with Jonah Drake and Arthur Pendelton.

"The National Hurricane Center station in Miami went dark two days ago," Thomas said, tapping a weather satellite printout received via naval satellite downlink. "This system is Hurricane Vancea late-season Category Four storm with sustained winds of one hundred and thirty knots and a central pressure of nine hundred and twenty-eight millibars. The eye will pass thirty miles west of the Dry Tortugas within eighteen hours."

"What does that do to the ships, Captain?" Drake asked.

"We cannot remain at anchor in shallow water," Thomas answered without hesitation. "Garden Key channel is sixty feet deep, but the storm surge will be fifteen to eighteen feet. If the *San Antonio* or the *Kearsarge* drags anchor in fifty-foot waves, these six-hundred-foot steel hulls will be driven onto the coral shoals and broken in half like dry twigs. We have to weigh anchor and ride out the tempest in deep water fifty miles to the south, where we have sea room to maneuver into the wind."

"You're leaving the fort?" Arthur asked, his voice steady but demanding.

"The fort's sixteen million bricks survived the great hurricanes of 1873, 1910, and 1919 without losing a single arch," Thomas said. "The masonry is eight feet thick. It cannot sink, and it cannot drag anchor. You have three months of fresh water in the subterranean cisterns, six months of rations, and the entire Marine ground detachment is remaining inside the redoubt under Captain Drake's command. But you will be on your own for forty-eight hours until the sea calms."

"Understood," Drake said. "We'll secure the sally port and dog down the gun embrasures."

"There is one more thing," Thomas said, turning the tactical radar display toward them. "Look at the surface track on Loggerhead Key, four miles to the southwest."

Arthur leaned over the monitor.

Ground radar showed a massive, radar-reflective blip lodged firmly on the outer coral reef of Loggerhead Key. It was nearly a thousand feet long, rising twelve decks above the sea.

"The *Ocean Empress*," Thomas said grimly. "A three-thousand-passenger luxury cruise liner out of Miami. She ran aground on the Loggerhead reef three days ago during the initial outbreak. Her engines are dead, her lower hull is breached on the elkhorn coral, and her upper decks are completely swarming with thousands of infected passengers who fled the mainland. When this hurricane's storm surge hits that reef, the sixteen-foot tide will lift that derelict hulk off the coral, or tear the superstructure apart."

"And the wind will blow the flotsam straight toward Garden Key," Arthur said, the realization settling like lead in his stomach.

"Precisely," Thomas said. "The storm wind is coming out of the southwest, blowing at a hundred and twenty miles an hour directly down the channel between Loggerhead and Fort Jefferson. Every piece of floating wreckage, every raft, and every waterlogged host that doesn't sink will be driven straight onto your curtain walls."

Thomas gripped Arthur's shoulder, then Drake's.

"Get ashore, gentlemen," Thomas ordered. "We are weighing anchor in twenty minutes. Hold that brick redoubt until we return."
