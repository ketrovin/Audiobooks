CHAPTER 23: THE GULF PASSAGE

The withdrawal from Pier 14 was executed in fire and smoke.

When the landing craft's fuel tanks reached ninety-five percent capacity, Brenda sheared the cam-lock safety pins with a cold chisel, while Sergeant Kowalski activated the timed thermite charges they had lashed to the primary manifold station.

As LCU 1643 reversed its twin screws, pulling away from the concrete pier into the dark waters of Hampton Roads, the thermite ignited. Three thousand degrees of incandescent liquid iron melted through the ten-inch high-pressure fuel lines, igniting the residual diesel vapors into a massive, rolling firewall that swept five hundred feet across Pier 14, incinerating the charging swarm in an ocean of white-hot chemical fire.

Two hours later, the fuel was transferred into the main storage bunkers of the USS *San Antonio*, and the task force turned south, clearing the Virginia Capes and heading into the open Atlantic.

For the next four days, the convoy steamed south along the edge of the Gulf Stream.

The weather changed with violent, tropical suddenness. The freezing rain and sleet of the North Atlantic gave way to heavy, humid air that smelled of ozone, rotting seaweed, and the warm, thick salt of the southern latitudes. The sky turned from leaden grey to an intense, blinding azure, but on the horizon, massive anvil-headed cumulonimbus clouds gathered every afternoon, flickering with silent sheets of heat lightning.

They rounded the tip of Cape Hatteras, passed the dark, silent coastline of the Carolinas, and steamed past Georgia. Everywhere, the story was the same. The coastal citiesCharleston, Savannah, Jacksonvillewere dark ruins, their harbors choked with burning wrecks and derelict barges drifting without crews.

On the fifth morning, the task force slipped through the Straits of Florida, rounding the submerged reefs of the Florida Keys.

Key West was burning. Even twenty miles offshore, the stench of burning mangrove swamps, sulfur, and decomposing matter drifted across the turquoise water in thick, sickening waves. The navy radar painted hundreds of civilian craft clustered around the shallows, their engines silent, their passengers gone.

Inside the chart room of the *San Antonio*, Captain Jonah Drake, Marcus Vance, Arthur Pendelton, and Dr. Sarah Mercer stood around the central plotting table.

Dr. Mercer looked exhausted, but her dark eyes were bright with a fierce, quiet intensity. "We completed the first centrifugal separation on the index samples," she announced, holding up three glass ampoules containing clear, amber fluid. "The pathogen's protein sheath is stabilized by calcium-binding lipid layers. In salt waterwith sodium concentrations above three point two percentthe outer sheath degrades within seventy-two hours. The marine environment is our greatest physical barrier. The virus cannot survive immersion in deep ocean water."

"That's why we're going where we're going," Marcus said, pointing at a small, isolated cluster of coral keys on the nautical chart seventy miles west of Key West.

"The Dry Tortugas," Captain Thomas Vance said, leaning over the chart. "Garden Key. Fort Jefferson."
