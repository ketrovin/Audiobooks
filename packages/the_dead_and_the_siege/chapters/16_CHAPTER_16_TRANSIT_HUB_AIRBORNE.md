CHAPTER 16: TRANSIT HUB AIRBORNE

The flight south down the East River was a journey through a graveyard of steel and stone.

The two Bell 412 helicopters flew in tight trail formation, three hundred feet above the dark, churning water, keeping beneath the low overcast that was now dropping heavy sheets of sleet across the harbor.

Inside the lead aircraft, the cabin was dead silent, save for the steady, reassuring drone of the twin turbine engines and the clicking of the navigation instruments in the cockpit.

Thomas Kincaid had finished dressing the wounds on Marcus Vance's arms. Marcus sat on the canvas bench, leaning his head back against the fuselage, his twelve-pound track wrench resting securely between his knees.

Arthur Pendelton looked across at Marcus. The two older menPort Authority supervisor and MTA track inspectorexchanged a long, silent nod of mutual recognition. Between them, they represented nearly sixty years of keeping the city's infrastructure running through blizzards, blackouts, and transit strikes.

Now, that infrastructure was gone.

"Where is the fleet staging, Captain?" Arthur asked, leaning forward so his raspy voice carried over the engine noise.

Drake pulled down a laminated nautical chart of the New York Bight, spreading it across his knees. He tapped a grease-pencil circle drawn five miles south of the Sandy Hook peninsula, in the deep-water anchorage of the Atlantic.

"Naval Amphibious Task Force 8," Drake said. "Led by the USS *San Antonio*, an amphibious transport dock, and the USS *Kearsarge*, a full-deck assault carrier. They were deployed from Norfolk forty-eight hours ago under executive continuity orders. They have five hundred medical personnel, heavy desalination plants, and enough closed-circuit hydroponic seed stock to sustain three thousand people for two years."

"Can they hold the ships?" Dr. Sarah Mercer asked, clutching the insulated cryo-pack on her lap. "If the virus reaches the lower decks of an amphibious carrier, it becomes a steel slaughterhouse."

"The ships have strict boarding quarantine protocols," Drake answered. "Every civilian, every soldier, every technician undergoes a seventy-two-hour thermal and behavioral isolation cycle in the well deck before they are permitted onto the upper tiers. The fleet commander is Captain Thomas VanceTommy's and Marcus's cousin."

Marcus looked up, a faint, tired smile touching the corners of his mouth. "Tommy Vance is running the *San Antonio*?"

"He's the executive officer," Drake said. "And he's waiting for us. Once we deliver Dr. Mercer and her viral isolates to the research lab aboard the *Kearsarge*, the fleet is weighing anchor. We steam south along the Atlantic corridor, bunkering fuel at the Norfolk naval annex, and then we head for the Gulf."

"To where?" Pete asked from the corner, his eyes wide. "Where in the Gulf is safe?"

Drake looked at Marcus, then at Arthur.

"Seventy miles west of Key West," Drake said quietly. "In the middle of the Dry Tortugas. A nineteenth-century coastal fortress built out of sixteen million solid red bricks, surrounded by a seventy-foot-wide tidal moat and four miles of open, deep salt water. Fort Jefferson."

Pete frowned. "A fort? You mean like a historical monument?"

"I mean the strongest physical redoubt on the North American continent," Marcus said, his voice deep and certain as he ran a calloused thumb over the iron jaw of his wrench. "No roads. No bridges. No power grid to fail, and no subway tunnels for them to crawl through. Just deep water, brick walls eight feet thick, and people who know how to hold a gate."

The helicopter banked gently to the south, clearing the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge.

Below them, in the dark waters of the Lower Bay, the riding lights of the naval task force appeared through the raina cluster of grey, armored steel hulls riding the Atlantic swells, their deck cranes swinging, their flight decks illuminated by dim red tactical lamps.

They had survived the fall of the city. They had pulled the science and the engineering from the wreckage.

Ahead of them lay the open Atlantic Ocean, the long voyage south, and the fortress that would decide the survival of their species.
