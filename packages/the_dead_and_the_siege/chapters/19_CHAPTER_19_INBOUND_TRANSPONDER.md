CHAPTER 19: INBOUND TRANSPONDER

The vessel adrift in the mouth of Delaware Bay was the *Cape Henlopen*, a three-hundred-foot double-ended roll-on/roll-off passenger and vehicle ferry that had operated for decades between Lewes and Cape May.

Her green and white hull was stained with streaks of orange rust and black exhaust soot. She sat low in the choppy grey chop, her car deck doors half-open, waves slopping over the steel ramps with every passing swell. Through the fog, the vessel's diesel air horn let out a short, dying blast every ninety secondsa mechanical emergency relay running on the last dregs of an auxiliary pneumatic tank.

The *San Antonio* slowed to five knots, dropping a twenty-four-foot rigid-hulled inflatable boat (RHIB) into the water from her starboard crane.

Captain Jonah Drake stood in the bow of the inflatable, holding an M4 carbine with a suppressed barrel. Beside him were Sergeant Kowalski, Marcus Vance carrying his iron track wrench, and Thomas Kincaid with an emergency medical trauma kit.

"Why are we boarding a derelict ferry, Drake?" Marcus yelled over the roar of the twin outboard motors as the small boat slammed through the freezing chop.

"Her manifest transponder indicates she cleared the Cape May refinery terminal before dawn," Drake answered, keeping his eyes fixed on the ferry's lower vehicle deck. "She was carrying two forty-ton commercial tanker trailers loaded with ultra-low-sulfur diesel fuel. Our naval task force needs every gallon of refined diesel we can scavenge if we are going to maintain convoy speed all the way to Key West without stripping the destroyer escorts."

They pulled alongside the ferry's low-freeboard rescue station. Kowalski hooked a magnetic boarding ladder to the steel hull, and within ten seconds, the four men had scrambled up onto the vehicle deck.

The car deck was an enclosed, drafty cavern of steel and grease, smelling of salt water, diesel exhaust, and raw panic. Forty passenger cars, pickup trucks, and minivans were jammed bumper-to-bumper across five lanes. Suitcases lay ripped open on the corrugated deck plates; children's toys and blankets were trampled into slicks of motor oil.

At the center of the deck sat the two massive aluminum fuel tankers, their landing gear locked down, their tires chained to the deck tie-down rings. The tank valves were intact.

"The fuel is here," Marcus whispered, inspecting the heavy bronze discharge manifolds on the lead trailer. "Full forty thousand gallons of clean diesel. But look at the cars, Drake."

Drake raised his weapon, sweeping the weapon light across the rows of stalled vehicles.

Inside a blue Ford station wagon ten feet away, two figures were pressed against the rear window. Their faces were grey, their eyes milky, their fingers scratching tirelessly against the glass, leaving five-pointed tracks of dried blood. In the vehicle beside it, a man in a business suit sat behind the wheel, his head thrown back against the headrest, his throat torn open to the cervical vertebrae, his hands still clenched around the steering wheel.

A metallic click echoed from the passenger stairwell leading to the upper promenade deck.

A figure emerged from the shadow of the steel staircase. It was a man of sixty in greasy coveralls, wearing a marine engineer's watch cap. He held a heavy pipe wrench across his chest, his eyes wide and bloodshot, his beard caked with salt spray.

"Hold your fire!" the man croaked, raising his free hand. "Hold your fire! I'm the chief engineer! My name is Gus Halloway!"

Drake kept his rifle trained on the man's chest. "Step into the light, Halloway! Roll up your sleeves! Both of them!"

Halloway complied immediately, pushing back the dirty canvas sleeves of his coveralls. His forearms were covered in soot and grease, but there were no puncture wounds or lacerations.

"I've been locked in the auxiliary steering compartment for twenty-four hours," Halloway gasped, his voice trembling with relief. "The passengers on the upper lounge went mad two hours out of Cape May. They broke through the glass cafeteria doors and came down the stairwells. I managed to dog the hatch on the emergency fuel pump room. The fuel tankers are untouched. I have the high-pressure transfer hoses rigged and ready."

"How many infected are on the upper deck?" Drake asked.

"Eighty," Halloway whispered. "Maybe a hundred. They're trapped in the passenger lounge. The automatic doors jammed when the emergency generator dropped a phase. But they're beating against the safety glass, and the frames are buckling."

Drake turned to Kowalski. "Signal the *San Antonio*. Have them lower the floating fuel transfer lines. Marcus, get the tanker manifolds open. We pump the fuel, take Halloway, and we blow the mooring lines."
