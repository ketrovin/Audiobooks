CHAPTER 13: ELEVATOR CHIMNEY

The Bell 412 did not land on the roof of the annex. The roof was too small, surrounded by high-voltage electrical conduits and rusted radio masts that made a skid touchdown impossible in the gusting thirty-knot wind.

Instead, the aircraft hovered ten feet above the gravel, its rotor wash whipping the gravel into a miniature sandstorm that stung Marcus's face.

The side door slid open, and Captain Jonah Drake appeared, strapped into a heavy nylon crew harness. He leaned out into the rotor wash, pointing toward the north facade of the adjacent MetLife building across 34th Street, then pointed down at Marcus and gave a sharp, slashing motion across his throat.

Through his tactical headset, Drake's voice crackled over Marcus's short-range radio. "Negative on roof extraction! The wind shear off the midtown towers is too severe! Move across the pedestrian skybridge into the MetLife service shaft! We will extract you from the 60th-floor mechanical deck!"

"Copy that!" Marcus yelled back into his collar mic. "Move to the bridge!"

The pedestrian skybridge was an enclosed glass-and-steel tube connecting the eighth floor of the annex to the massive commercial tower across the street. Marcus led the way, smashing through the reinforced glass fire door with his track wrench.

They sprinted through the enclosed walkway, eighty feet above the street. Beneath their boots, through the plate-glass floor panels, they could see the terrifying spectacle of First Avenue: thousands of infected pouring down the street, their bodies illuminated by the orange glow of burning municipal buses.

They reached the main mechanical core of the MetLife building. Here, in the center of the skyscraper, sat the primary elevator chimneyan eight-hundred-foot vertical concrete shaft that housed the high-speed express elevators serving the upper tiers.

The elevator cars were stuck on the ground floor, their power severed. To reach the 60th-floor mechanical deck, there was only one path: the vertical iron maintenance ladder bolted into the concrete corner of the shaft.

"Eight hundred feet straight up," Pete whispered, staring into the dark, echoing abyss of the shaft. "Marcus... I can't climb that. My arms are shaking. I won't make twenty floors."

Marcus grabbed the boy by the collar of his denim jacket, pulling him so close their noses almost touched. "You will climb it, Pete. You will put your right hand on the rung, then your left hand. You will not look down. You will not think about the ground. You will think about breathing. If you slip, I am right behind you on the rungs. Now get on the ladder!"

Pete swallowed hard, gripped the cold iron rungs, and began to climb.

Brenda followed, her tool sack secured to her shoulders with heavy nylon webbing. Marcus brought up the rear, his track wrench looped through a heavy leather strap across his back.

The climb was an endurance test in absolute darkness. Every ten floors, a small concrete maintenance platform jutted out into the shaft, providing a momentary resting place where they could lean against the cold wall and catch their breath.

By the thirtieth floor, Pete's breath was coming in dry, wheezing sobs. "Marcus... my fingers are cramping. I can't feel my thumbs."

"Keep moving!" Marcus urged from three rungs below. "Thirty floors down, thirty to go! You're halfway to the deck!"

From the bottom of the shaft, eight hundred feet below, came a horrifying sound: the heavy iron access doors at the lobby level had buckled. The infected were pouring into the pit, piling onto the roof of the stalled elevator cars, and then, guided by the sound of boots scraping on iron, they began to climb.

They did not climb like human beings. They climbed with frantic, mechanical speed, their fingers hooking into the rungs with terrifying strength, their bodies scraping against the concrete walls in a continuous, rising wave of movement.

"They're on the ladder!" Brenda shouted from above. "Marcus, they're thirty feet below you!"
