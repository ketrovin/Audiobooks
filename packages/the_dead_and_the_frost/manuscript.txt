# The Dead and the Frost
## A Sub-Zero Maritime Nightmare
### By Gabriel Simon

---



# 01. The Snowshoe Trail

## Chapter 1: Setup

The woods in the Cape Breton Highlands don't make a sound in January, especially when the power grid has been dead for three months and the temperature has plunged to thirty below. The silence was absolute, a heavy, suffocating weight that pressed against the ears like water. The only sound was the rhythmic, dry creak of raw-hide webbing on snowshoes. The snow lay four feet deep, a vast, unblemished shroud that buried the blueberry barrens and the low juniper bushes. The spruce and fir trees were bent double under the weight of the hard pack, their branches locked in frozen, claw-like shapes. The air was so cold that it felt sharp, cutting the throat with every breath, and the wind off the Gulf of St. Lawrence was a constant, icy razor that threatened to freeze exposed skin in minutes.

Silas Bernard led the line, his stride long and fluid. He was sixty-eight, with weathered skin the color of cured birch bark and eyes that knew how to read the subtle shifts in the hemlock branches. He wore a heavy wool mackinaw jacket that had been patched at the elbows with deer hide, and a pair of traditional ash-wood snowshoes that he had bent and strung himself before the world fell apart. Behind him shuffled three others: his niece Mindy, a young local fisherman named Paul, and a man who called himself "Jim."

"Jim" was actually Julian Vance. Six months ago, Julian was a prominent real estate developer from Boston. He had owned a two-million-dollar modern estate overlooking the Margaree River—a glass-and-steel monstrosity that had required clearing ten acres of old-growth maple. When the world fell apart, Julian's money had bought him nothing but a front-row seat to the collapse. His family had fled south in a private helicopter that crashed somewhere over New York; his private security had abandoned him after looting his pantry. Now, Julian wore a dirty, oversized orange canvas parka he’d stripped from a dead municipal road worker. He had rubbed coal dust into his face and hands to cover his soft, manicured skin. He knew that if Silas and Mindy found out who he was, they would leave him behind. The locals hated Julian Vance, the developer who had bought up their traditional hunting grounds, put up "No Trespassing" signs, and priced their children out of the valley. 

In the pocket of his road-worker parka, Julian carried his leather wallet, stuffed with useless platinum credit cards, stock options for a high-end golf resort project in Inverness, and thousands of dollars in high-denomination treasury bills. During their brief stops, he had stuffed wad after wad of the treasury bills into the heels of his leather boots to try to block the creeping frost. The paper only compressed and froze solid, a useless, stiff lump of paper wood that bruised his heels. The certificates that once represented millions of dollars in capital were now nothing more than crude shims to stop his feet from sliding inside his oversized boots.

"Keep your paces even," Silas warned, his voice a low rumble that barely carried in the freezing air. He did not look back. "In this cold, your breath will freeze on your collar. If you sweat, the moisture will turn to ice against your skin, and you die. Walk steady, and don't stamp your heels."

Julian gasped for air, his lungs burning as if he were inhaling broken glass. His snowshoes—traditional ash-wood frames strung with caribou rawhide, borrowed from Silas’s barn—felt like lead weights on his boots. His thighs screamed with lactic acid. He had spent his life paying others to clear his paths, shovel his driveways, and pave his roads; now, the four feet of pristine, powdery snow was an unyielding wall. 

"Silas," Julian panted, his voice trembling as he leaned on a wooden pole. "How far to the cabin? We've been walking for hours. The wind is picking up, and I can't feel my toes."

"Two miles," Silas said without turning. "If the storm doesn't worsen. If it does, we shelter in the draw."

Mindy, walking behind Julian, adjusted the sling of her Tikka .308 rifle. She wore a heavy canvas coat and moose-hide mitts. "Stop whining, Jim," she muttered, her breath forming a brief cloud that instantly vanished. "You're lucky Silas lent you those shoes. Without them, you'd be buried to your waist in the drift, freezing to death. Walk like a man."

"I'm just pacing myself," Julian lied, trying to sound like a hard-working utility worker. He checked his hands inside his wool mittens. The skin was already blistered and raw from the rough harness straps. He had millions in offshore accounts, but right now, his entire net worth wasn't worth a single dry birch log. 

"Watch the trees," Silas said, stopping suddenly. He pointed a gloved hand toward a dense stand of young firs. "The cold slows them down, but it doesn't stop them. They don't have blood to freeze solid. They just get stiff."

Julian stared into the gray shadows of the forest. The dead were different in the winter. In the summer, you could hear them coming—their wet breathing, their dragging feet, their smell. But in the winter, the dead didn't breathe. They didn't produce body heat, so there were no telltale steam plumes from their mouths. They were silent, icy statues that only moved when they sensed the vibration of a living step.

## Chapter 2: Escalation

The wind howled as they climbed toward the ridge, driving fine, icy crystals of snow horizontally through the air. The whiteout was setting in, reducing their world to a five-yard circle of gray light. The trees groaned under the force of the gale, and the snow accumulated in massive, unstable cornices along the ridge.

Suddenly, Julian's left foot gave way with a sharp *snap*. 

He fell forward, his face burying into the freezing drift. He gasped, swallowing snow, his limbs flailing. When he tried to push himself up, he realized the rawhide webbing on his left snowshoe had severed against a hidden granite boulder beneath the powder. The ash frame was intact, but the tension was gone, leaving the shoe useless.

"Silas!" Julian cried out, panic rising in his chest. "My shoe! The webbing broke!"

The group stopped. Mindy kept her rifle raised, scanning the swirling whiteout, while Silas walked back. The elder knelt in the snow, his thick mittens inspecting the broken ash frame. 

"Rawhide is dry," Silas murmured, his breath frozen to his grey mustache. "You stepped too hard on the heel. You're walking like a city man, Jim. Heavy on the heels, not the toes. You have to slide the shoe, not stomp it."

Julian swallowed hard, his heart hammering against his ribs. "Can you fix it?"

Silas pulled a coil of dried spruce root twine from his pack. "I can patch it temporarily. But it won't hold if you keep stamping your feet. Spruce root is tough, but the cold makes it brittle."

As Silas worked, his fingers moving with practiced efficiency despite the sub-zero cold, Mindy stepped closer, her eyes locked on Julian's hands. Julian had pulled off his mittens to help hold the twine, revealing his fingers. 

"You've got soft hands for a road crew worker, Jim," Mindy said, her voice sharp and suspicious. "No calluses. And that's an expensive gold band under that dirt on your ring finger. I know a designer ring when I see one."

Julian quickly tucked his hands back into his pockets. "I... I was a supervisor. Spent most of my time in the truck, writing reports. The ring was my father's. It's the only thing I have left of him."

Mindy didn't look convinced, but before she could press him, Silas stood up. "The patch is done. Walk light. If you break it again, we don't stop."

But Silas didn't move. He stood perfectly still, his head tilted toward the wind. 

"What is it?" Paul asked, his hand tightening on his wood-splitting axe.

The wind dropped for a brief second, leaving a pocket of eerie quiet. In that silence, they heard it: a dry, rustling sound, like frozen leaves scraping against bark. 

From the whiteout, a figure emerged. It was a man, walking with a slow, jerky gait. He wore a bright red, high-end Arc'teryx winter jacket—a coat that Julian recognized instantly. It was Arthur, his former business partner from Boston. Arthur's face was white, covered in a thick layer of hoarfrost. His eyes were wide and milky, frozen open. His jaw was locked in a stiff, open gape, and his limbs moved with a mechanical, crackling sound as the frozen joints forced their way through the ice. There was no steam from his mouth; he was a walking block of ice.

He didn't breathe. He didn't growl. He just stepped forward, his stiff, blue fingers reaching out.

"Arthur," Julian whispered, stepping back in horror. 

"He's sluggish," Silas said, his voice calm. "The frost has locked his joints. But if he grabs you, his grip is frozen iron. Step around him. Do not waste a bullet. The sound will draw more from the valley."

They began to back away, but the movement triggered the dead man's vestigial instincts. Arthur lunged with surprising speed for a frozen corpse, his boots crunching heavily on the crust of the snow. Behind him, two more frosty shapes materialized from the whiteout—landscapers in stiff, frozen overalls, their skin covered in ice.

## Chapter 3: Climax & Defeat

"Go! To the ravine!" Silas shouted, guiding the group toward the steep slope where the hemlocks could offer shelter. 

Julian ran, but his patched snowshoe was a disaster. The spruce root twine held, but the ash frame twisted in the deep drifts. He sank knee-deep with every second step, his breath coming in ragged, desperate screams. Behind him, the three sluggish dead followed. They did not fall through the crust; their weight was distributed differently, their frozen muscles allowing them to shuffle across the hard pack where a living man’s panicked stride broke the surface. 

Julian looked back and saw Arthur's frozen, white face closing the distance. He could see the dark, frozen blood on Arthur's collar where he had been bitten months ago during the flight from the city. 

"Help me!" Julian screamed, his foot sinking into a pocket of soft snow near a buried hemlock trunk. He was trapped to his thigh, his patched snowshoe wedged beneath a root. 

Paul turned to help, but Mindy yelled, "He's too heavy, Paul! Get to the ridge! The wind is rising!"

Julian, consumed by pure survival panic, reached out and grabbed Silas's sleeve as the elder tried to guide him up. The sudden pull caught Silas off guard on the icy slope. Silas's snowshoe slipped, and both men tumbled down the steep bank, crashing through the hemlock branches and landing in the deep drift at the bottom of the ravine.

Silas landed hard, his head striking a log. He lay still, his knitted cap knocked off, a trickle of dark blood freezing instantly on his temple. 

"Silas!" Mindy screamed from the top of the ridge. She raised her rifle, aiming at Arthur, who was sliding down the bank toward the fallen elder. She pulled the trigger. 

*Click.*

The rifle didn't fire. The extreme cold had frozen the firing pin grease, rendering the modern weapon useless. She frantically cycled the bolt, but the mechanism was jammed with ice. 

Julian scrambled to his feet. He looked at Silas, who was beginning to stir, and then at Arthur, who was only ten feet away, shuffling through the snow. If Julian stayed to drag Silas, the dead would have them both. He looked at the old man, then at the frozen monster.

"Sorry," Julian whimpered. 

He abandoned the elder, clawing his way up the opposite side of the ravine. But without snowshoes, he was helpless. He sank to his chest in the powder, his limbs flailing uselessly. The orange road worker's jacket caught on a branch, trapping him in place. 

He looked across the valley. Through the shifting veil of the blizzard, he could see the silhouette of his summer mansion on the far hill—dark, silent, and empty. A monument to a life of useless accumulation. 

Below him, Silas opened his eyes. With his last strength, the elder rolled over, pulling a small hand-axe from his belt, preparing to defend himself. But Arthur and the other two frozen dead were already on him. Their stiff, gray hands clamped onto Silas, their teeth tearing into his heavy wool coat. Silas didn't scream; he only let out a long, heavy sigh that turned to frost in the air, his eyes looking up at the sky. 

At the top of the ridge, Mindy and Paul retreated into the whiteout, their snowshoes carrying them away into the silent safety of the forest. They did not look back. Silas had taught them that survival required making hard, logical choices; staying to fight with a jammed weapon meant dying alongside those who could not keep up. They moved through the heavy firs, their eyes scanning the path, their hearts heavy with the loss of the elder who had kept their community alive.

Julian was left alone, pinned in the drift. He watched the three dead figures slowly turn their milky, frozen eyes toward him. Arthur began to crawl up the bank, his frozen limbs dragging through the snow with a dry, rustling hiss. Julian reached into his pocket, his hand brushing against his useless leather wallet, filled with platinum cards and thousands of dollars in cash. He pulled it out and threw it at the approaching corpse. 

"Take it!" Julian screamed, his voice cracking. "Take the money! Take the land! I'll sign it all over to you!"

The wallet, corporate papers, and cash fluttered in the wind, landing in the snow between them, the paper currency getting swept away by the gusts. Arthur didn't look down. He pressed forward, his blue, frostbitten fingers reaching for Julian's boots as the blizzard closed in, erasing the last of the tracks and freezing Julian's screams into the silent winter night.

An hour later, Mindy and Paul reached the shelter of the hemlock draw. They found the small, log cabin Silas had described, a low structure hidden in a depression between two rocky ridges. Inside, they built a tiny, smokeless fire in the cast-iron stove, using dry birch bark and some old surveyor blueprints to catch the spark. They sat in the dark, shivering as the heat began to return to their hands, their eyes fixed on the flickering orange flame. They knew that tomorrow they would have to check the traps and the woodpile on their own. Silas was gone, but the knowledge he had shared—the traditional ways of the wood, the construction of the snowshoes, the reading of the wind—lived on in them. Outside, the storm raged on, covering the valley in a fresh layer of pristine white snow, sealing the mountain pass and leaving the valley and the dead behind under the cold northern stars, where only the fire kept the dark at bay.

# 02. The Cedar Bough Shelter

## Chapter 1: Setup

The blizzard had erased Silas’s tracks within minutes of his fall. Mindy’s lungs burned as she climbed, each breath a dry, rattling gasp. Tears had frozen into tiny ridges of ice on her eyelashes, blurring her vision. Beside her, Paul walked in silence, his head hung low. They had lost the elder. Silas, who had known every stream, ridge, and game trail in the Margaree highlands, was gone, torn down in the ravine by the silent, sluggish dead. The loss was a physical weight, pressing against their chest plates as they fought the rising gale. The wind was a roaring beast now, sweeping off the highlands and driving the snow in blinding sheets that reduced visibility to a few feet. The sub-zero temperature was dropping fast, turning their sweat-dampened clothes into rigid, icy cages that restricted their movement. Every breath felt like inhaling ground glass, and the moisture from their noses froze instantly, sticking their nostrils together.

"We can't make the cabin, Mindy," Paul said, his voice muffled by his thick wool scarf, which was stiff with a crust of frozen breath. He stopped, leaning heavily on his wooden walking staff. "The wind is crossing sixty knots. If we stay on the ridge, we’ll freeze before midnight. The cold is creeping into my boots. My heels are numb."

Mindy looked back. The forest behind them was a swirling wall of white, erasing the world they knew. She remembered Silas's voice from winters past: *When the wind screams off the water, you get off the ridge. You go to the draw where the old trees stand. They protect you. The hemlocks have thick branches that catch the snow, and the ground underneath is always dry.*

"We drop into the draw," she said, her voice tight with grief and resolve. "The old-growth hemlocks and cedars. Silas always said the canopy there cuts the wind. We build a shelter. If we stay in the open, the storm will have us."

They descended the slope, their snowshoes sliding through the deep, powdery drifts. As they entered the shelter of the massive, ancient trees, the screaming of the wind softened to a deep, rushing sigh. The air down here was slightly warmer, sheltered by the dense canopy of old-growth hemlocks, but the cold was still a physical weight, pressing against them.

Suddenly, Paul gasped, pointing his staff toward the base of a hollow cedar. "Mindy, look. Someone's there."

A figure sat slumped in the snow, half-buried by the drift. He wore a heavy, designer sheepskin coat, but his legs were wrapped in nothing but thin denim jeans, soaked through and frozen stiff as sheet metal. He had no snowshoes. His bare hands, blue and swollen, were tucked into his armpits, and his face was grey with frostbite, his breath coming in shallow, ragged puffs.

Mindy knelt beside him, brushing the snow from his collar. "Hey. Can you hear me? Speak to me."

The man groaned, his eyelids fluttering open to reveal bloodshot, terrified eyes. "Cold," he whispered, his jaw clicking like castanets. "My... my cabin. I couldn't get the generator started. The pipes burst. The heating failed. Everything stopped."

"What's your name?" Mindy asked, checking his pulse. It was slow and weak, the signs of stage-two hypothermia. 

"Rick," the man lied, his voice thin and reedy. "I'm a... I'm a carpenter from Sydney. Fled when the town fell."

Mindy looked at his sheepskin coat—worth more than Paul’s fishing boat used to be—and his designer leather boots, completely unsuited for the highlands. His hands, though swollen, had no scars, no rough skin, no signs of manual labor. As she searched his pockets for matches or tools, she pulled out a thick leather folio. Inside were stock certificates for a timber acquisition company, land deeds, and bundles of high-denomination utility bonds. 

Richard’s boots were lined with several crumpled pages of these certificates, stuffed into the toes to act as extra shims to stop the frost from biting. 

"Carpenter, right," Mindy muttered, a cold sneer on her lips. "And I'm the Queen of England. You stuffed your boots with timber stocks, 'Rick.' Let's get him up, Paul. If he stays here, his blood will stop flowing in an hour. We can use his worthless stocks to plug the gaps in the shelter."

Together, they dragged "Rick" toward a dense cluster of young hemlocks. Rick was actually Richard Sterling, a senior partner in a Boston private equity firm that had bought up thousands of acres of local timberlands for luxury developments. When the cities collapsed, Richard had retreated to his private estate, believing his steel gates and propane tanks would keep him safe. But the propane had run out, the dead had surrounded his gates, and he had fled into the woods with nothing but his expensive coat and a pocket full of diamond rings. He knew that if these locals recognized him as the man who had closed off their traditional hunting paths, they would leave him to the wind.

## Chapter 2: Escalation

"We need a fire," Richard whimpered, his teeth chattering uncontrollably as Paul propped him against a fallen log. "Please. I can't feel my fingers. We have to light a fire. I have matches in my pocket. There is dry wood under the cedar."

"No fire," Mindy said flatly. She began clearing the snow from the forest floor using her snowshoe as a shovel, digging down to the frozen moss. 

"Are you crazy?" Richard gasped, his voice rising in panic. "We'll freeze to death! I have... I have money. I can pay you. Just light a fire!"

"Shut up, Rick," Paul spat, cutting a low branch from a nearby fir with his hand-axe. "You light a fire in this valley, and the smoke rises. In thirty-below air, that hot smoke sits in the canopy like a beacon. The dead might be slow, but they aren't blind. They see the plume, or they smell the woodsmoke, and they'll walk straight to it. And in this storm, we won't see them until they're inside our camp. We survive on body heat, not firewood."

"Silas taught us how to build a debris hut," Mindy said to Paul, ignoring Richard's pleas. "A cedar bough shelter. We use the low hemlock branches for the frame, then layer it with cedar and spruce boughs. Three feet thick. It traps our body heat like a sleeping bag."

"I... I can help," Richard offered, trying to stand, but his frozen denim legs buckled immediately, and he collapsed back into the drift. 

"Just stay there and keep your hands in your pockets," Mindy said. "You're useless to us if you lose your fingers. Paul, hand me those utility bonds from his pack. We’ll use them to bind the frame nodes."

Mindy and Paul worked in silence, their movements coordinated. They laid a thick ridgepole between two hemlock trunks, then leaned shorter ribs against it to form a narrow, A-frame skeleton. Mindy then began cutting cedar branches. Cedar was the key; the flat, scale-like leaves layered perfectly, shedding snow and trapping air like goose down. They used the heavy paper of Richard's stock options to wrap around the frame joints, binding them together.

Richard watched them, his mind racing. He had spent his life managing assets, directing people, making phone calls. Now, he was completely dependent on a young woman and a fisherman who looked at him with silent contempt. He felt a heavy metal object slip in his coat pocket—a gold Patek Philippe watch, worth a hundred thousand dollars. He had shoved it into his pocket before fleeing. He slid his hand down, burying the watch deep in the snow under his hip. If they saw it, they would know he was a liar, and in this wilderness, lies were fatal.

"Layer the bottom with dry moss first," Mindy instructed Paul. "Then the softest cedar tips. We need at least a foot of bedding to keep the cold ground from sucking the heat out of our bodies. Make sure the pine needles point downward so they shed any melting frost. If they point up, the water will pool and freeze under us."

As the darkness fell, the forest grew even colder. The wind screamed through the high canopy, but on the forest floor, the A-frame shelter stood ready, a low, green mound that looked like a natural brush pile. 

"Alright," Mindy said, crawling into the narrow tunnel of the shelter. "Get in, Rick. We wrap ourselves in the space blankets and huddle. No talking. Save your breath. The heat will build up if we stay quiet."

## Chapter 3: Climax & Defeat

Inside the shelter, it was pitch-black and cramped. The smell of fresh cedar resin was thick, mixing with the sour scent of their cold sweat and Richard's shallow, fearful breathing. The three of them lay pressed together, their shoulders touching. The space blankets crinkled loudly with every breath. Slowly, the heat of their bodies trapped within the thick boughs began to warm the air, raising the temperature inside the green tomb to a survivable level.

But Richard couldn't sleep. The cold was still clawing at his toes, and his mind was filled with the memory of the dead partner he had seen in the woods. 

Hours passed in freezing silence. 

Then, a sound broke the rhythm of the wind. 

It was a soft, dry *crunch*. 

Mindy’s hand instantly clamped onto Richard's shoulder, warning him to be still. Beside them, Paul shifted slowly, his fingers wrapping around the handle of his hand-axe. 

*Crunch. Scrape.*

Something was walking outside. It was slow, its footsteps spaced seconds apart. In the sub-zero air, the dead moved like rust-locked machinery. Because they didn't breathe, there was no warning steam—only the physical impact of their weight on the frozen crust. They were silent, ice-covered stalkers.

Suddenly, the roof of the shelter groaned. A heavy, frozen weight pressed down on the cedar boughs directly above Richard's head. The pine needles showered down onto his face. Through the gaps in the branches, Richard saw a gray, frostbitten hand, its fingernails black and split, clawing at the cedar leaves. It was one of the frozen dead, a former logger in a heavy flannel shirt, its jaw frozen open and its tongue a black pebble of ice.

Richard lost his mind. The fear of being buried alive with the monsters broke his sanity.

"Get off me!" he screamed, his voice shattering the quiet of the forest. 

"Rick, shut up!" Mindy whispered fiercely, trying to pin him down. 

But the panic was too strong. Richard thrashed, kicking out wildly with his frozen boots. One of his heavy heels struck the central hemlock support pole. 

With a sickening crack, the ridgepole snapped. 

The heavy roof of cedar boughs collapsed inward. Hundreds of pounds of snow and frozen branches crashed down onto them, pinning Paul's legs and trapping Mindy beneath the heavy frame. The freezing wind rushed into the ruins of the shelter, instantly sucking away their trapped heat. 

Richard, fueled by adrenaline, scrambled out of the collapsed green mound. In the pale starlight, he saw the attacker—a frozen, blood-smeared logger, its jaw locked in a silent gape, reaching for him. Behind it, two more frost-covered dead emerged from the shadows.

"Mindy! Paul!" Richard shrieked, backing away. 

"Help me clear the log, Rick!" Paul shouted from beneath the debris, his legs pinned. 

Richard looked at the collapsed shelter, then at the dark woods. He couldn't help them. He had to save himself. He turned and ran, his leather boots slipping on the crust, his blue hands clawing at the air as he lunged through the drifts toward where he thought his estate lay. 

But without snowshoes, he only made it twenty yards before sinking to his waist in a deep drift. He thrashed, his sheepskin coat catching on a blackberry bramble, pinning him in place. 

From the shadows of the hemlocks, three more sluggish figures emerged. They moved slowly, their limbs crackling like dry twigs, but they were unhindered by the snow. One of them was Arthur, the partner Julian had abandoned hours ago. Arthur's milky eyes locked onto Richard. 

"No! I have money! I have land!" Richard screamed, reaching into his pocket and throwing a handful of diamond rings into the snow. "Take it! Just let me go!"

The diamonds sank into the white powder, vanishing instantly. Arthur didn't stop. The frozen dead closed the distance, their cold, stiff hands clamping onto Richard's throat, silencing his screams. 

Back at the ruined shelter, Mindy managed to wiggle free. Working desperately in the freezing dark, she cleared the heavy branches off Paul, pulling him free just as the wind began to howl again. They couldn't rebuild. 

Mindy looked toward the sound of Richard’s dying screams, then down at the snow where his gold watch lay half-exposed, glinting in the starlight. She kicked snow over it, burying it forever. 

"We have to move, Paul," Mindy said, her voice hollow as she slung her rifle, which had failed to cycle because the lubricant had frozen. "We freeze if we stay here."

They shuffled back into the whiteout, two silent shadows disappearing into the storm, leaving the ruins of the shelter and the dead behind in the freezing woods.

# 03. The Spruce Tea Camp

## Chapter 1: Setup

The cabins of the Crow’s Nest Logging Camp sat huddled in the shadow of Cape Smokey, their cedar roofs buried under three feet of heavy, wet snow that groaned against the rafters. Inside the main bunkhouse, the air was cold and damp, smelling of stale woodsmoke, unwashed wool, and the sharp, coppery tang of human illness. The power grid had been black for months, leaving the camp isolated in the sub-zero winter of the Acadian forest. The camp's pantry was down to cornmeal and salt pork. In the corner of the room, a stack of worthless Canadian bank notes and corporate share certificates lay in a wooden crate, stripped of their power and now used as fire starters or stuffed into the soles of boots for insulation. The wealthy executive who had once owned those shares had died weeks ago, unable to withstand the first frost, leaving behind a pile of paper that was only good for staving off the freezing drafts.

Leila, a Syrian-Canadian pharmacy graduate who had fled the chaos of Halifax, knelt beside a cot in the dim corner. She examined a young boy's mouth, gently pulling back his swollen lip. The boy winced, whimpering softly. His gums were spongy, purple, and bleeding. 

"It’s scurvy, Deandre," Leila said, rising and wrapping her frayed wool shawl tighter around her shoulders. She looked at Deandre, a tall African Nova Scotian man who had been the camp’s heavy equipment operator before the collapse. "His joints are swelling too, and he's complaining of deep muscle pain. Without Vitamin C, the children won't survive the winter. The canned fruit ran out weeks ago, and we are living on dried grains."

Deandre leaned against the heavy pine dining table, his face etched with worry. He had used a sheaf of worthless bank drafts to shim the uneven leg of the table, a silent testament to the new order of things. "The road to the highway is completely blocked with abandoned semi-trucks, Leila. We can’t drive the plow out, and even if we could, the towns along the coast are gone. What are our options?"

"We look to the forest," said Marie-Claire, stepping into the bunkhouse. She was an Acadian forest researcher who had spent years cataloging the flora of the highlands. She carried a bundle of dry spruce firewood, which she dropped onto the floorboards with a heavy thud. "Our ancestors survived the first winters here by learning from the Mi'kmaq. The eastern white pine and black spruce needles are packed with Vitamin C. A tea made from them will stop the bleeding and strengthen the blood."

"The white pine stand is in the old cutover, a mile south," Deandre said, his voice dropping. "But that clearcut is a trap. When the outbreak hit the camp, the loggers ran into the slash, looking for a way out. The cold has slowed them down, but they’re still out there. Stiff, white, and quiet. They don't move unless they hear something."

"We go in, harvest the needles, and get out," Marie-Claire said. "We use the snowshoes to stay quiet and distribute our weight on the crust. If we don't, the kids won't make it to spring."

Deandre nodded, reaching for his snow goggles and a heavy iron fire poker he had sharpened into a spear. "I'll break the trail. Leila, stay here and keep the wood stove low. We don't want the chimney smoke drawing them from the valley."

"No," Leila said, grabbing her coat. "I need to identify the correct needles and make sure we harvest the greenest growth. The older needles have less ascorbic acid. I'm coming with you." She pulled on her mittens, ready to face the freezing air.

## Chapter 2: Escalation

The cold outside was a sharp, physical blow to the face, the sub-zero wind whipping fine, crystalline snow off the hemlock branches and stinging their exposed skin. Deandre led the way, his heavy ash-wood snowshoes packing down the fresh trail, his eyes constantly scanning the white wilderness for any sign of movement. Marie-Claire and Leila followed in his tracks, carrying canvas sacks and hand shears. The forest was a silent cathedral of towering hemlocks and pines, but as they approached the boundary of the clearcut, the trees thinned out. The landscape opened into a vast, scarred plain of stumps, slash piles, and abandoned logs, all buried under a thick, uniform shroud of snow.

"The white pines are down near the creek bed," Marie-Claire whispered, pointing toward a line of tall, tufted trees at the far edge of the clearing. They stepped into the open clearcut, where the wind was much stronger, whipping the snow into white curtains that drifted across the frozen stumps.

"Look out," Deandre said, halting abruptly and raising his arm.

A few yards ahead, a bright orange object stood out against the blinding white. It was a high-visibility safety vest, faded and crusted with frost. Beneath it was a former logger, frozen in a half-kneeling position against a hemlock stump. His hard hat was still strapped to his head, coated in ice. His skin was a waxy, gray-blue hue, his eyes milky and open, staring blankly at the snow. He was perfectly still, looking like a gruesome ice sculpture.

"Is he... active?" Leila whispered, her heart pounding against her ribs.

"They don't breathe, so we can't look for steam," Marie-Claire warned. "In this cold, they don't produce any vapor at all. Walk wide of him. If the snow crust breaks under your shoes, the sound will draw him."

They stepped carefully around the frozen figure, keeping their weight distributed on their snowshoes. As they passed, the logger’s head turned slowly, the frozen muscles and tendons in his neck crackling like dry twigs. He let out a low, dry rattle—not a growl, but the physical sound of dry throat tissues vibrating as he struggled to open his stiff jaw. # 04. The Sound of the Crust

The Black River Bog in winter was a flat, white wasteland stretching for miles, dotted only by stunted, skeletal black spruce trees that clung to the frozen peat. In the summer, it was a treacherous maze of acidic water and sinking peat moss; now, it was a sheet of iron-hard ground covered in snow.

At the bog's edge, Tariq adjusted the bindings of his wooden snowshoes. He was a Sudanese-Canadian environmental engineer who had been working at the highland research station when the grid went dark. Beside him was Chloe, a nineteen-year-old Acadian hunter with a weathered wool coat and a heavy ash-wood walking staff, and Aiden, a sixty-five-year-old local elder who had spent his life trapping these valleys.

Aiden had laughed when he saw him do it, commenting that it was the first time he’d seen a bank roll do any real work. Nearby lay a leather briefcase belonging to a deceased resort developer; it had been kicked open, its utility bonds and corporate stock shares fluttering like dead leaves into the snow drifts, utterly worthless in a world where warmth and food were the only true currency. The storm was coming, and they had to move before they were caught in the open.

The snowshoes will distribute our weight. If we walk light, we stay on top of the crust. "We go single file," Aiden said, his breath pluming in the cold. "Keep twenty paces between us. If one of us breaks the crust, the others don't go down with them. Chloe, you take the lead. You're the lightest."

"Walk light, walk silent," Aiden said, stepping onto the white expanse.

They set off, their snowshoes sliding softly over the glassy crust with a dry *hiss-hiss* sound. The open bog offered no cover, and the gray winter sky hung low, threatening another snowstorm. The wind was a biting blade, carving through their thick wool layers as they marched into the empty white. Each step required focus; they had to keep their knees bent and slide their feet to prevent the wood frames from digging too hard into the thin crust. Aiden checked his own pack, which was lined with old utility company circulars, their promises of quarterly dividends now just trash. "We have about four hours of light," he muttered, pulling his collar up. "If we lose the light, we lose the visual landmarks. In a blizzard, this bog becomes a white room with no exit. ## Chapter 2: Escalation

Halfway across the bog, the wind died down, leaving an eerie, freezing silence. The only sound was their own heavy breathing and the rhythmic, soft sliding of their snowshoes on the ice. The air was so cold it felt sharp inside their lungs, leaving a frost coating on the wool scarves wrapped around their mouths.

Suddenly, a sharp *CRACK* echoed from the forest edge behind them.

Chloe stopped in her tracks, looking back over her shoulder. From the shadow of the spruce trees, three figures emerged. They were sluggish, their bodies coated in hoarfrost, their movements slow and mechanical. They were former researchers from Tariq's station, still wearing their heavy blue parkas.

As the first corpse stepped onto the open bog, his heavy boot stamped down on the crust.

*CRACK.*

The ice broke beneath him, and his leg sank knee-deep into the soft snow. He pulled his leg out with a wet, sucking sound, took another step, and broke through again.

*CRACK.*

The sound was loud, like a pistol shot in the quiet valley.

"The sound is going to draw more of them," Tariq whispered, his heart rate spiking as he watched the corpses drag themselves forward.

"Keep moving," Aiden urged. "Don't look back. Just watch your footing and keep your spacing."

As they hurried, the noise of the breaking ice behind them became a rhythmic, echoing cadence. *CRACK. CRACK. CRACK.* It was the sound of the dead tracking them. The vibrations of the breaking crust traveled through the frozen peat beneath their feet, thrumming up through the wood frames of their snowshoes.

From the north side of the bog, another shape appeared—a frozen hunter, his rifle still slung over his shoulder, his limbs crackling as he stumbled toward the sound of the cracking ice. The dead were using the auditory trail of their own destruction to navigate the wilderness. They did not run; they simply kept moving, their heavy heels shattering the thin sheet of ice over and over, creating a pathway of broken glass.

"The crust is thinning here," Chloe called back, her voice tight. "The ice is gray and bubbled. There’s gas under here."

"Methane hummocks," Tariq said, his analytical mind kicking in despite the terror. "The decaying peat decomposes and releases gas, keeping the ice thin and fragile. Walk only on the white patches, not the gray. The white has snow cover to insulate it and keep the frost deep."

They adjusted their path, weaving between the gray, glassy hummocks. Behind them, the dead loggers and researchers were stumbling into the methane zone, completely unaware of the hazard. One of them stepped directly on a gray patch, and the ice collapsed. He sank to his waist, his frozen hands thrashing in the black, bubbling peat water beneath. He let out a low, wet gurgle, his mouth filling with freezing mud, but he continued to claw at the edges, slowly breaking the surrounding ice crust and making the gap wider. The sound of his struggles echoed across the flat bog, drawing the other sluggish dead toward the hazard, but also warning the living of their approach. They could see more frosty shapes emerging from the tree line, drawn by the noise. Tariq watched the researcher's frozen blue parka swell with swamp water. The corpse didn't struggle in the human sense; there was no panic, no frantic gasping for air. It simply kept clawing at the ice edge with mechanical repetition, its fingers snapping off one by one against the frozen rim until only bony stumps remained. The relentless, quiet nature of their pursuit was far more terrifying than any feral speed. It was a slow, crushing inevitability.

## Chapter 3: Climax & Defeat

They were within a hundred yards of the eastern ridge when the wind returned with a vengeance, bringing a flurry of dry, blinding snow that stung their eyes. The visibility dropped to near zero in seconds, and the dark pine ridge they had been aiming for vanished into the whiteout.

"Stay close!" Chloe shouted over the roar of the wind.

Tariq took a step, but his left snowshoe caught on a hidden root beneath the snow. The wooden frame twisted, and the raw-hide binding snapped with a loud pop. He stumbled, his boot coming off the snowshoe and stamping directly onto a thin patch of gray ice.

*CRACK.*

The crust shattered. Tariq fell forward, his leg plunging through the ice and deep into the freezing, acidic bog water. The cold was an instant, agonizing shock, like a thousand needles pricking his skin.

"Tariq!" Chloe cried, turning back through the swirling snow.

"My leg is stuck!" Tariq gasped, his teeth instantly chattering. "The peat... it's like wet cement under the ice. I can't pull it out!"

Behind them, through the veil of snow, the cracking sound grew louder. The dead hunter was closing in. He had fallen through the ice several times, his legs covered in frozen black mud, but he didn't feel the cold. He dragged himself forward, his stiff fingers clawing at the snow, his frost-rimmed coat rattling.

Aiden knelt beside Tariq, using his heavy ash-wood staff to distribute his own weight on the fragile crust. "Grab the staff, Tariq! Chloe, help me pull!"

From the window, they looked back at the flat expanse of the bog. The blizzard was covering their tracks, but through the whiteout, they could still hear the distant, faint *CRACK* of the ice, as more sluggish dead wandered into the frozen swamp, trapped forever in the cold, black peat.

# 05. The Muskeg Crossing

## Chapter 1: Setup

The James Bay lowlands in January were a vast, flat expanse of white, where the sky and the earth merged into a single, blinding gray horizon. The temperature sat at thirty-five below, and the wind off the bay carried a bone-chilling dampness that cut through the heaviest layers of wool. It was a cold that did not just chill the skin; it crept into the bones, aching in the joints and slowing the heartbeat. To make matters worse, the sun had not been seen in days, replaced by a permanent pall of leaden clouds that made the midday sky look like twilight.

Thomas, a fifty-eight-year-old Cree hunter, stood at the edge of the muskeg swamp, his hand resting on a long, fire-hardened birch pole. Beside him was Kateri, a twenty-eight-year-old Cree wildlife researcher, and David, a Chinese-Canadian geologist who had been working at the local mine before the world collapsed. They wore heavy wool coats, moosehide mitts lined with beaver fur, and traditional snowshoes webbed with caribou rawhide.

They were trying to reach a winter camp on the Moose River. The only route lay across the muskeg—a massive peatland that, in the summer, was a waterlogged sponge of moss and spruce. Now, it looked like a solid, snowy plain. But the snow was a dangerous deceiver.

"The snow is thick this year," Thomas said, tapping the ground with his pole. "It acts like a blanket. It insulates the ground. Underneath these drifts, the peat mud and the water aren't fully frozen. If you step in the wrong place, you’ll break through the thin crust and sink into freezing black mire."

David adjusted his snow goggles, his breath freezing on the edges of his hood. "How do we know where the ice is thick enough to hold us?"

"We look at the trees and the shrubs," Kateri explained, pointing to the stunted black spruce and the patches of dwarf willow. "Where the dwarf willows grow, the peat is active and warm. The ice is thin there. We walk where the spruce are small and crowded. Their roots bind the peat, and the ground there freezes deeper."

"And keep the birch poles horizontal," Thomas added, demonstrating by holding his pole across his chest. "If you fall through, the pole will bridge the gap and keep you from going under. Walk light, David. You're carrying too much gear."

David nodded, adjusting his pack. He wasn't a wealthy executive; he was a working-class technician who had spent his life analyzing soil samples. He knew the chemistry of the muskeg, but he didn't know how to read the moss like Thomas and Kateri did. In his pack, David carried a bundle of corporate ledger papers he had salvaged from the mine manager's office. The manager, a wealthy transplant from Toronto who had spent his life in heated offices, had tried to bribe Thomas with gold watches and bearer bonds to guide him out when the cold hit. Thomas had refused; watches and bonds couldn't heat a shelter or catch a rabbit. The manager had frozen to death in his office two days later, surrounded by leather chairs and empty ledger books. Now, David used those very ledger pages as boot insulation and wipes, the high-gloss corporate logos rubbed away by dirt and grease. The paper was stiff and crinkled in his boots, a constant reminder of the uselessness of the old world's wealth.

## Chapter 2: Escalation

They began the crossing, Thomas breaking the trail. The silence of the lowlands was heavy, broken only by the dry, paper-like crunch of their snowshoes on the wind-packed drifts. Every ten yards, Thomas drove his birch pole into the snow, testing the resistance of the ground beneath. Sometimes the pole struck solid ice with a reassuring, metallic-like ring; other times it slipped through into soft, wet mud, and they had to detour around the pocket.

By midday, the wind picked up, swirling the loose snow around their boots and reducing visibility. David’s legs were aching from the unaccustomed effort of walking on snowshoes, his muscles burning in the intense cold. Through the white haze, Kateri spotted movement to the west.

"Thomas," she whispered, raising her hand to halt the group.

Three figures were stumbling through a stand of stunted larch trees. They were former mine security guards, still wearing their heavy, navy-blue parkas and steel-toed boots. Their faces were gray and covered in a thick layer of frost, their eyes cloudy and sightless. They moved with a slow, jerky gait, their limbs stiff from the extreme cold. They didn't breathe, leaving no telltale puffs of vapor in the freezing thirty-five below air. They were like mechanical dolls whose springs had wound down in the frost, but their focus was locked onto the living.

"They're sluggish," Kateri said, her voice barely a murmur. "The cold has locked their joints. But they’re heading toward our trail. They must have heard the crunch of our shoes."

"Walk quiet," Thomas said. "We don't want to fight them here. The ground is too unstable. One heavy step and we all go down."

As they watched, one of the dead guards stepped off the ridge of stunted spruce, heading directly toward the sound of their snowshoes. He stepped into a patch of dwarf willow.

*WHOOSH.*

The thin ice crust beneath the snow gave way instantly. The heavy, stiff corpse plunged waist-deep into the unfrozen muskeg trap. He thrashed, his frozen hands clawing at the snow, but the black peat mud beneath was like quicksand. The cold water offered no buoyancy to dead flesh, and within seconds, the mire pulled him down until only his blue-hooded head remained above the snow, his jaws still snapping silently in the freezing air. The other two guards continued their slow, clumsy march, completely ignoring their trapped companion, their heavy boots breaking the crust with a wet, hollow crunch.

"That could be us," David whispered, his face pale as he stared at the sinking hood.

"Only if you don't watch the willow patches," Kateri said, guiding him forward. "Keep your weight forward on your shoes. Don't look back at them."

They moved in a wide arc around the willow patch, giving the trapped miner a wide berth. The other two dead miners were still moving, their stiff limbs dragging through the drifts. One of them caught his coat on a dead spruce branch, his sleeve tearing open to reveal gray, bloodless flesh that didn't bleed or react to the sharp wood. He didn't try to untangle himself; he simply kept pushing forward until the branch snapped, his mechanical momentum carrying him slowly along. Kateri noted that the cold was their only ally; in warmer weather, these creatures would have moved with much greater fluidity. Here, they were hostages to the winter, their dead muscles freezing solid. Thomas kept his eyes on the horizon, searching for the bend in the river that marked their safety.

## Chapter 3: Climax & Escape

The storm worsened, the wind howling off James Bay and reducing visibility to less than ten feet. The stunted spruce trees vanished into the whiteout.

Thomas stopped, his pole extended. "We’ve lost the tree line. We must navigate by the slope of the snow drifts. The wind blows from the north; the drifts crest to the south."

David stepped forward, trying to keep close to Kateri. But his foot slipped on a hidden hummock. He lost his balance, his snowshoe twisting, and his leg punched through the snow crust.

He fell forward, but his birch pole wasn't held horizontally—he had used it to stabilize his fall. The pole slid into the snow, and David’s chest hit the soft drift.

*CRACK.*

The ice collapsed beneath him. Freezing, black peat water rushed over his waist, soaking his trousers. The cold was an immediate, paralyzing shock that made him gasp, his lungs seizing. The mud at the bottom of the bog gripped his boots like wet cement, pulling him downward.

"Thomas! Kateri!" David screamed, his hands clawing at the slippery edges of the ice hole. "I'm sinking! The mud has my boots!"

Kateri immediately dropped to her knees, sliding her birch pole across the snow toward him. "Grab the pole, David! Keep your weight flat! Don't struggle against the mud!"

David grabbed the birch wood, his fingers numb and stiff inside his beaver-lined gloves.

From the whiteout behind them, the remaining two undead guards emerged, drawn by David's screams. They walked slowly, their boots crunching on the snow, their frost-bitten fingers reaching out. One of them was only twenty feet away.

Thomas moved with surprising speed for his age. He stepped onto a ridge of frozen spruce roots near the hole, raising his heavy iron-tipped ice chisel. As the first dead guard lunged toward David, Thomas drove the iron blade of the chisel into the guard's forehead. The skull cracked, and the corpse fell limp, sliding into the muskeg hole beside David, its weight threatening to pull David down.

"Pull, David! Pull!" Kateri shouted.

Working together, Kateri and Thomas hauled on the birch pole. David heaved, his frozen boots slipping out of the thick peat mud. With a wet, sucking sound, he was pulled onto the stable snow crust.

Thomas grabbed David’s arm, hauling him to his feet. "We must run. The camp is just across the ridge."

They scrambled up the snowy bank of the river, David shivering violently, his frozen trousers crackling with every step like sheets of metal. They burst through the door of the winter lodge, where a wood stove was burning hot.

Kateri immediately helped David strip his frozen gear. As he pulled off his boots, the wet, shredded pulp of the corporate ledger pages fell onto the floor—worthless record sheets that had failed to protect him from the freezing swamp. He kicked the wet paper aside into the fire wood pile. Kateri wrapped him in heavy wool blankets and gave him a cup of hot labrador tea to drink, the steaming brew smelling faintly of damp pine needles and earth. David sat near the stove, his face turning red as the warmth returned, his hands shaking as he held the tin mug, feeling the painful pins-and-needles of thawing blood in his fingertips.

"Thank you," David whispered.

Thomas stood at the window, looking out into the swirling whiteout. Outside, the cold wind howled across the James Bay lowlands, and the frozen muskeg stood as a silent, deadly barrier, keeping the living safe and the dead trapped in the black mud beneath the snow.

# 06. The Smoked Whitefish

Aki worked with quiet, rhythmic precision, a skill his grandfather had taught him on the shores of Lake Nipigon. He looked at Sarah, who was struggling to copy his smooth cuts, her fingers stiffening from the draft creeping under the door.

The smoke in the room was growing thicker now, escaping through the broken vents and making their eyes water. The fire pit crackled, the embers glowing a fierce orange in the dimness. # 07. The Silent Snares

# 08. The Birch Bark Ledger

But the warmth and the noise of the steam boiler did not stay contained. The heat venting from the chimney melted the snow on the shed roof, and the smell of the coal smoke drifted across the frozen museum yard. Through the cracked wooden doors of the shed, Kateri saw movement.

Drawn by the heat and the smoke, they shuffled toward the shed, their boots crunching on the snow crust. They moved slowly, their limbs stiffened by the sub-zero temperatures, but their direction was certain. The heat was a beacon, drawing them toward the warm cabin. They were former museum volunteers and railway employees, their bodies preserved by the cold.

"Albert," Kateri whispered, her hand tightening on the shovel. "They're coming. At least five of them. They’re at the doors. They’ve smelled the coal smoke."

"We can't stop the fire, Kateri," Albert said, his face wet with sweat under his wool hat. "If we dump the fire now, the boiler cools down, and we’re trapped in the dark. Shovel more coal. We need that steam pressure. I’ll prepare the cylinder valves."

## Chapter 3: Climax & Escape

The first of the sluggish dead—a former museum guide in a frozen uniform—slammed against the wooden doors of the shed. The dry wood splintered under the weight, and the door creaked open, admitting a blast of freezing wind and a flurry of snow. "Albert! The pressure!" Kateri shouted, shoveling more coal as a second sluggish figure entered the cab, its gray hands reaching for her legs. 

Albert checked the gauge. The needle sat at seventy pounds. "Close enough!"

He grabbed the cylinder cock lever, throwing it forward, and opened the throttle valve. 

A massive blast of superheated steam vented from the cylinders at the front of the locomotive, filling the shed with a deafening, white roar. The hot steam hit the approaching zombies, instantly melting the ice on their clothes and skin. In the sub-zero air, the moisture refroze immediately, locking their joints and blinding their eyes in a crust of ice. They stumbled and fell, frozen solid by their own melted armor. They were turned into rigid, ice-locked statues, unable to move.

Albert threw the heavy reverse lever with a loud metallic clank. The steam cylinders hissed, and the heavy iron wheels ground on the rusty rails, throwing up a shower of orange sparks in the dark shed before finding traction.

The steam engine backed slowly out of the museum shed, its heavy steel tender breaking through the frozen wooden doors with a loud, splintering crash. The locomotive rolled out into the snowy, storm-swept night, the steam whistle blowing a long, triumphant wail that echoed through the hills, announcing their departure. Kateri leaned against the cab wall, her face smudged with soot and sweat, a smile breaking through her exhaustion. 

"We're out, Albert," she said, catching her breath. 

"Yes," Albert said, his hand steady on the throttle as the engine chugged down the line, heading toward the town under the cold northern stars. "The old girl still has a run left in her."

The steam train rolled through the howling blizzard, leaving the museum and the frozen dead behind in the dark, carrying the heat and the hope of the living into the long winter night. They pushed toward the station, knowing they had the physical fuel and the mechanical power to save the town's shivering refugees. They would deliver the coal, keep the shelter's boilers hot, and survive this brutal winter together. Albert watched the snowy tracks ahead, his hand steady on the throttle, his heart filled with the memory of the old rails he had run in his youth, while Kateri rested by the firebox, enjoying the heat of the white-hot coals. The steam whistle blew one last long, screaming wail that echoed across the hills, a message to the town that warmth was on the way, a defiant light against the freezing dark under the cold northern stars.

# 29. The End of the Line

## Chapter 1: Setup

The steam locomotive *No. 42* rounded the final curve of the harbor line, its iron wheels screeching against the frozen rails as Jack applied the brakes. The single, powerful searchlight on the boiler nose cut through the driving snow, illuminating the dark, massive iron framework of the Sydney terminus station. The station stood as a silent monolith against the black sky, its iron-and-glass canopy covered in a thick layer of green spray ice that had blown off the harbor basin. The ocean outside was a churning mass of dark water and ice floes, the salt spray freezing as soon as it hit the rocks along the waterfront.

Inside the cab, the air was tense, filled with the smell of hot coal oil, wet iron, and the steam venting from the pipes. Darnell stood near the coal tender, his arms aching, his shovel resting against the hopper. He had spent the last six hours shoveling bituminous coal to keep the engine moving, and his muscles were burning with exhaustion.

Aditi tapped the water level gauge. The needle was pinned in the lower red section, vibrating with the motion of the engine. "The boiler water is almost gone, Jack. The injectors are starting to draw air. We can't turn back. If we don't take on water at the terminal pump, the boiler will dry-burn and explode, tearing us to pieces." The water was their only shield, and they had run out of choices. The old injector valves, which used high-pressure steam to force water into the boiler, were already starting to sputter, a warning sign that the system was running dry.

"We're here at the terminal," Jack said, his voice flat and tired as he pulled the brass brake valve handle, venting the air lines. The train came to a halt with a long, metallic screech and a deafening hiss of steam that clouded the platform in a thick white mist, condensing immediately into a new layer of slick ice on the cold granite stone of the platform.

They looked out through the soot-streaked cab windows, rubbing the condensation away to see. The terminus station was a scene of eerie, freezing silence under the dim stars. The high iron security gates that surrounded the platform were locked from the outside, wrapped in heavy, rusted chains and secured with massive commercial padlocks. The town’s local defense watch had sealed the station months ago during the initial autumn panic, trying to contain the outbreak and prevent the refugees from flooding the town, turning the beautiful terminal into a locked iron cage. 

Darnell looked at the station master's office window. Inside, stacks of old paper money and corporate bonds were scattered on the floor, useless garbage. He had used a bundle of those same notes earlier to wipe the grease off his hands, laughing at the irony. Wealth was nothing; water was life. In the old world, the executives of this terminal had private cars and special bonuses, but when the freeze hit, none of that could buy a way out. They had died in their office chairs, their vaults full of useless paper.

"The gates are locked," Darnell said, his hand tightening on his shovel. "But the platform isn't empty. Look at the drifts."

As the steam cleared, the yellow searchlight swept across the concrete platform. Dozens of figures stood in the light. They were former townspeople and travelers, wrapped in blankets and heavy winter coats that were now stiff with ice. They stood motionless, covered in hoarfrost, their heads bowed as if in prayer. 

But as the train’s engine idled, the vibrations of the steel rails reached their frozen boots. One by one, their heads snapped up, their milky, white eyes reflecting the searchlight.

## Chapter 2: Escalation

"The platform is a tomb," Aditi whispered, her hand trembling on the steam valve. 

The sluggish dead began to shuffle toward the train. Because they had been locked inside the unheated terminal for months, the cold had hardened their skin and frozen their clothes into an icy, leathery armor. They moved with a slow, mechanical gait, their joints crackling like dry twigs as they stepped over the frozen luggage littering the platform. They were slow, but their relentless pursuit was terrifying. They moved in a wave, their clawed fingers reaching for the metal train. The cold had sun-mummified their skin, making it tough as hide, and the frost coated their eyelashes and lips, making them look like moving ice sculptures.

"If we open the passenger car doors, they’ll flood the coaches," Darnell said, looking at the windows of the passenger car behind them where the survivors were looking out in terror. "But we can't back the train out without water. We’re stuck. If the boiler dries out, we freeze to death in this cab."

"The water standpipe is at the end of the platform, near the boiler room," Jack said, pointing to a red iron pipe that rose from the concrete. "But it’s surrounded. There are ten of them near the valve."

"We use the blow-off valves," Aditi said, her eyes flashing in the dark cab. "We vent the high-pressure steam from the boiler directly onto the platform. It will clear the area around the standpipe long enough for Darnell to hook up the hose."

"It's risky," Jack warned. "If we dump that steam, we lose our remaining pressure. If the injectors don't draw the new water fast enough, we won't be able to restart the engine. We’ll freeze to death."

"We don't have a choice," Darnell said, grabbing a heavy rubber hose from the tool chest. "I'll go. Aditi, watch the pressure valve." He stepped onto the gangway, bracing himself against the wind. He tied a rope around his waist, checking his cleats.

## Chapter 3: Climax & Escape

Darnell climbed onto the iron running board of the locomotive, holding the heavy hose, his cleats digging into the metal walkway. The cold wind from the harbor hit him, freezing the sweat on his face. On the platform below, the sluggish dead had reached the side of the engine, their gray, frost-covered hands reaching up for the running board. 

"Now, Aditi!" Darnell shouted. 

Aditi threw the steam blow-off valve open. 

A deafening roar shook the cab as a massive blast of superheated steam erupted from the side of the boiler, venting directly onto the platform near the water standpipe. The hot white cloud blinded the dead, the heat melting the spray ice on their bodies. In the sub-zero air, the moisture instantly refroze, locking their limbs and pinning them to the concrete floor in a shell of ice. They were turned into rigid, ice-locked statues, unable to move.

Darnell leaped down onto the platform, his boots sliding on the ice. He ran to the red standpipe, his hands shaking as he threaded the heavy hose coupling onto the frozen valve. 

"Open the valve, Jack!" he yelled. 

Jack threw the pump switch in the cab. A loud gush of water rushed through the hose, the cold liquid filling the locomotive's tank. 

But the steam blast was fading, the boiler pressure dropping rapidly. The remaining sluggish dead on the platform, drawn by the noise, were closing in on the passenger cars. A group of three frozen figures began to crawl up the boarding steps of the rear coach. Marcus and Louis stood at the coach door, using their iron bars to keep the dead back, but the frozen zombies ignored the blows, their blue hands clawing at the wood frame. 

"We’re full!" Aditi yelled from the cab window. "Darnell, get back!"

Darnell uncoupled the hose, water spraying over his gloves and freezing instantly. He scrambled up the iron ladder of the cab just as a zombie in a frozen coat lunged for his boots. 

Jack grabbed the throttle, throwing the engine into reverse. The steam cylinders hissed, the wheels spinning on the rails before finding sand traction. Jack operated the sander to drop dry sand on the icy rails. The train began to back out of the terminal, the iron wheels grinding and breaking the rusted chains of the terminal gates with a loud crash that echoed across the harbor. 

They rolled out of the dark station, leaving the frozen platform and the trapped travelers behind under the cold northern stars. Darnell collapsed onto the cab deck, his hands frozen, his chest rising and falling. 

"We're out, Jack," Aditi said, her hand resting on the water gauge, which now sat in the green. 

"Yes," Jack said, his eyes reflecting the warm orange light of the firebox as he looked back at the dark, retreating harbor terminal. "But there's no safe harbor left for us along the coast. The water terminal is a tomb. We have to keep running, heading back into the interior. We keep the steam up, and we keep moving."

The steam train thundered into the dark, storm-swept winter night, heading back toward the high mountain grades. Behind them, the lost city and its silent, frozen inhabitants disappeared into the swirling blizzard, buried under the fresh drifts. Darnell sat on the metal deck plates of the cab, his frozen hands cupped near the warmth of the firebox door, his breath pluming as the feeling slowly returned to his fingers. Aditi checked the steam pressure, making sure the injectors were feeding the boiler smoothly. 

In the coaches behind them, the survivors huddled together, feeling the rhythmic clicking of the wheels and the comforting warmth of the steam pipes. Every mile carried them further from the coast, into the quiet safety of the spruce forests. # 30. The Signal Cabin

## Chapter 1: Setup

The Creignish Signal Cabin was a small, two-story wooden booth perched on tall pine stilts at the highest point of the mountain pass, looking down over the winding tracks and the frozen valley below. The cabin's stilts were anchored deep into the granite bedrock of the hills, but the structure still swayed in the forty-knot wind that swept off the Gulf of St. Lawrence, threatening to tear the wooden booth from its foundations. The wind drove a fine, powdery snow that sifted through the cracks in the siding, piling in small, white ridges on the floorboards. 

Inside, the temperature was a freezing thirty below. Tariq sat at the wooden desk, wrapped in three layers of heavy wool blankets, his breath forming a thick mist in the dim light of a single tallow candle. His fingers were stiff and blue inside his woolen mittens. Before him sat the old brass telegraph key. The modern radio bands were nothing but white noise, but the copper telegraph lines that ran along the wooden poles beside the tracks were still intact, powered by a bank of lead-acid batteries charged by a small wind turbine on the cabin roof. The turbine hummed in the wind, a low, vibrating drone that vibrated through the desk and the floorboards.

To keep the sub-zero draft out, Tariq had spent the previous afternoon stuffing the gaps in the wooden walls with old regional bank notes and corporate bonds. He used his pocket knife to wedge the thick paper into the cracks, laughing at how these papers, which once represented millions in capital for rail expansions and urban transit projects, were now only useful as windbreaks. Class privilege had vanished, leaving only the raw necessity of keeping the cold from freezing his hands.

Down the line, parked on a siding in the valley ten miles away, the steam train *No. 42* was waiting. Aditi, Deandre, and Jack needed Tariq’s signal to proceed. If the harbor terminal was clear, Tariq would receive the confirmation telegraph from the harbor operator and relay it to the train.

*... -- ... / --.- ..- . .-. -.--* (Query to Harbor Terminal).

He waited, his ears straining for the sounder to click. 

The wire was silent, carrying no signal from the coast. The copper line was single-wire, using the steel rails themselves as the ground return, but the heavy frost coating the glass insulators on the telegraph poles caused a faint, electrical leak, making a quiet, rhythmic buzzing sound in the brass receiver. It sounded like the cold breathing of the winter itself.

"Come on, Marcus, don't do this," Tariq whispered, tapping the brass key again with a shaking hand, his teeth chattering in the sub-zero chill. "Answer the line. Just send a simple confirmation. Tell us the harbor is open, the gates are clear, and the shelter is safe. We need to move the train."

## Chapter 2: Escalation

An hour passed in freezing silence. The candle had burned down to a pool of tallow, and the extreme cold was beginning to dull Tariq's thoughts, making his head heavy. He had to force himself to stay awake, knowing that if he fell asleep, the hypothermia would take him before morning. The frost on the window panes had grown into patterns like skeletal feathers, blocking his view of the tracks.

Suddenly, the brass sounder on the desk let out a sharp click. 

*Clack.*

Tariq sat up, grabbing his pencil with stiff fingers. The key began to tap, but it wasn't the clean, rhythmic Morse code of Marcus, the harbor operator. It was a slow, heavy, irregular cadence that lagged and stumbled, sounding mechanical yet broken. 

*Clack... clack... clack-clack... clack...*

Tariq listened, his brow furrowing as he tried to translate the random sequence of characters. It made no sense. It was just a series of random letters, spaced seconds apart, like a child hitting the key. 

Then, he understood. 

It wasn't a message. It was the physical weight of a hand dragging across the key. In the frozen harbor terminal, Marcus’s cabin had been overrun. Marcus had died while clutching the desk, and his sluggish, frozen corpse was now twitching, its fingers dragging across the brass key in a slow, involuntary reflex. The wire was transmitting the rhythmic heartbeat of a grave. The cold was animating the dead muscles in a crude, mindless loop, sending a warning of death along the copper wire.

*Clack... clack...*

At that moment, the wind dropped, and Tariq heard a scraping sound from the wooden stairs below the cabin. 

*Scritch. Slide. Scritch.*
