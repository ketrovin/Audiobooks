# The Dead and the Highway
## Asphalt Ruin
### By Gabriel Simon

THE DEAD AND THE HIGHWAY

CHAPTER 1: THE STOP AND DISPATCH

The asphalt of Interstate 87 stretched north through the Adirondack foothills like a long, grease-smeared ribbon of grey, disappearing into the heavy afternoon mist. It was mid-October, the kind of day where the cold didn't just bite—it clung. The air was thick with the smell of wet pine, damp soil, and the faint, sweet scent of rotting leaves. Every few minutes, a lone semi-truck would roar past, its massive tires throwing up a fine spray of dirty road-grit that rattled against the windshield of State Trooper Trooper Dickkopf's patrol cruiser.

Trooper Dickkopf sat in the driver's seat of Unit 412, the engine idling with a low, mechanical rumble. His hands, clad in black leather driving gloves, tapped a restless, impatient rhythm against the steering wheel. He was forty-two years old, with a face that looked like it had been carved out of a turnip and left in the sun too long—all sour angles, red-rimmed eyes, and a thin, permanent sneer that had grown deeper with every year he remained stuck in this backwater county.

Today had been a bad day. In fact, it had been a bad year. His wife had left him in the spring, the department had passed him over for sergeant in the summer, and just three hours ago, the captain had chewed him out in front of the entire shift for "unnecessary aggression" during a routine speed trap. 

Unnecessary aggression. Dickkopf scoffed, spitting a dark stream of tobacco juice into a paper cup resting in the console. The world was soft. The department was soft. Everyone was looking for an excuse to get offended, and meanwhile, the highway was crawling with scum, smugglers, and out-of-state speeders who treated his stretch of asphalt like their personal drag strip. He needed a win. He needed a big, clean bust—something he could dump on the captain's desk to silence the complaints and prove he was the only real cop left in this state.

A low hiss of static came from the dashboard radio, followed by the dispatcher's bored, nasal voice. "Unit 412, we have a report of a reckless driver northbound near marker 114. Unmarked white commercial transport, double-axle trailer, reinforced steel panels. Reporting party says it's lane-weaving. Over."

Dickkopf's fingers tightened on the wheel. Marker 114 was less than two miles south of his current position.

"Unit 412, copy," Dickkopf drawled into the mic, trying to sound bored, though his heart had already started its familiar, jackhammer beat. "I'm in position at the crossover. I'll monitor and intercept."

He hung up the mic, adjusted his duty belt—ensuring the heavy steel of his Sig Sauer was sitting exactly where it should—and stared into his rearview mirror. 

A minute passed. Then two. The mist was thickening, rolling off the hemlocks and settling into the low hollows of the highway, turning the grey afternoon into a dull, white twilight. 

Then he saw it.

Emerging from the fog was the white truck. It was an ugly, brutal-looking machine. It didn't look like a standard commercial rig; it had no chrome, no logos, and no driver comfort amenities. The cab was painted a flat, industrial white, reinforced with heavy, matte-black steel plating around the grille and the windows. The trailer behind it was even stranger—a massive, seamless container of brushed titanium-alloy, sealed with heavy, hydraulic locking bars that ran vertically along the rear doors. It didn't have a single window, vents, or markings, save for a small, yellow placard near the license plate that read: HAZMAT - CLASS 9 - BIO-SECURITY.

As the rig rounded the sweeping curve near the crossover, its rear wheels drifted slightly across the fog line, tires kicking up a shower of gravel before the driver jerked the massive vehicle back into the center of the lane.

"Got you," Dickkopf muttered.

He threw the cruiser into gear, stomped on the gas, and surged out of the gravel crossover. The light bar on his roof exploded into a swirling strobe of blue and red, casting long, frantic pulses of light against the white fog.

He closed the distance rapidly, pulling up behind the massive, silent trailer. The hydraulic bars on the back doors looked thick enough to withstand a rocket blast. He blared his siren once—a sharp, piercing *yelp* that echoed off the rock cut on the side of the road.

For a long five seconds, the truck didn't respond. It kept rolling forward at a steady fifty miles per hour, as if the cruiser behind it didn't exist. Dickkopf's face flushed hot. The sneer on his lips turned into a hard, angry line. He pulled his cruiser closer, nearly matching the truck's speed, his front bumper inches from the heavy steel impact bar of the trailer.

Finally, the truck's brake lights flared. The massive rig began to slow down, its tires groaning as it pulled onto the narrow, gravel shoulder. It came to a stop near an old, rusted guardrail, steam hissing from its air brakes with a loud, defiant sigh.

Trooper Dickkopf parked his cruiser angled toward the road, blocking the truck's exit. He sat in the cab for a moment, letting his dashcam record the scene, before adjusting his campaign hat, stepping out into the cold, damp air, and walking toward the driver's side door.

***

One hundred and fifty miles away, deep beneath the bedrock of a New Jersey quarry, the lights of Command Center Delta burned with a cold, fluorescent glare.

The room was silent, save for the rhythmic, low-frequency hum of server racks and the quiet tap of keys. Dozens of operators sat in tiered rows, their faces illuminated by the green and amber glow of tactical displays. On the main screen at the front of the room, a high-resolution satellite map of the Northeast Corridor was updated every three seconds. A single, bright blue icon, labeled *CON-TRANS 09*, was slowly crawling north along the blue line of Interstate 87.

Suddenly, a sharp, electronic chime rang out from Console Four. The blue icon flashed yellow.

"Director," Operator Miller said, his voice flat, professional, and entirely devoid of emotion. "We have a telemetry anomaly on *CON-TRANS 09*. Transport has deviated from the route schedule. Speed is zero. Location is I-87 North, marker 116.3."

A tall man in a dark, tailored suit stepped forward from the shadows of the observation deck. Director Graves looked down at the console, his eyes narrowing as he watched the yellow icon pulse.

"Did Frank radio in a mechanical failure?" Graves asked.

"Negative, Director," Miller replied, his fingers flying across the keyboard, pulling up the transport's internal diagnostics. "Engine status is normal. Trailer pressure is stable at ninety-eight percent. But the external cameras are showing a local municipal vehicle behind the rig. Red and blue strobes. It looks like a traffic stop."

Graves's face went rigid. "A traffic stop? In a Level-5 containment corridor? Frank knows the protocol. He should have blown past any local jurisdiction."

"Frank's GPS shows he was blocked," Miller said, switching the screen to a live feed from the truck's front cabin camera. 

On the screen, the view showed the wet, foggy highway through the reinforced glass of the windshield. A New York State Police cruiser was parked diagonally ahead of them, its lights flashing. A lone trooper, his face obscured by the brim of his hat, was walking slow, deliberate steps toward the driver's window.

"He's being detained," Graves whispered. "If that local idiot touches the hydraulic seals..." He turned to the communications officer behind him. "Get Strike Team Romeo on the line. Now."

The comms officer pressed a button. "Romeo-1, this is Control. We have a Level-2 anomaly at marker 116.3. Local law enforcement has intercepted *CON-TRANS 09*. We are declaring a Pre-Breach Alert. You are cleared for immediate dispatch."

Across the hall, in the subterranean hangar where the massive, black-painted Sikorsky UH-60 Black Hawk sat on the tarmac, a klaxon began to wail—a deep, rhythmic groan that shook the concrete walls.

Strike Team Romeo—six men and women clad in matte-black tactical gear, their faces hidden behind dark, pressurized respirator masks—instantly dropped their gear and sprinted toward the helicopter.

Captain Vance, the team leader, was the first to board. He slid into the webbing seat, pulling his rifle—a heavily modified M4 carbine with a thick, oversized barrel shroud—across his chest. He plugged his comms harness into the chopper's internal net.

"Romeo-1 in position," Vance barked over the radio. "What are we looking at, Control?"

"We have a local trooper harassing a containment transport," Graves's voice came through the static, sounding thin but sharp. "The cargo consists of the contained specimens from the upstate outbreak. Frank is transporting them to the secure disposal facility where they are scheduled to be opened securely and destroyed. If the seals are broken out on the road, we are looking at an immediate, localized expansion event. We have a major airport fifteen miles north-northeast of that location. If the anomaly reaches the terminal, we cannot contain it. You have authority to use lethal force on any non-Directorate personnel to secure the vehicle. Do you understand?"

"Understood, Control," Vance said, his face hardening behind his mask. He looked at his squad. "Load up, team. We have a hot one. Check your magazines. I want Shredder rounds in the primary chambers. Cherry Pi in the reserves. Let's go."

The chopper's turboshaft engines screamed to life, the massive rotor blades blurring into a dark disk as the aircraft lifted off the subterranean pad, rising through the massive steel exhaust shaft into the foggy New York sky.

***

Trooper Dickkopf stood on the running board of the truck, his boots slipping slightly on the wet metal. He tapped his heavy, metal flashlight against the reinforced glass of the driver's door.

"State Police! Step out of the vehicle!" he shouted, his voice muffled by the wind and the roar of the idling engine.

Inside the cab, Frank sat with his hands resting flat on the steering wheel, his fingers twitching slightly. He wore a simple, blue mechanic's uniform, but his posture was that of a soldier—shoulders back, chin tucked, eyes cool and analytical. On the passenger seat beside him, a small, black tactical terminal was flashing a silent red warning: *EXTERNAL CONTACT DETECTED. ENGAGE CONTAINMENT PROTOCOL.*

Frank didn't look at the warning. He kept his eyes on the trooper's face, noting the red, burst capillaries on the man's nose, the sweat bead rolling down his temple despite the cold, and the twitching muscle in his jaw.

"Officer," Frank said, his voice calm, flat, and steady as he rolled the window down exactly three inches. "You need to return to your vehicle immediately. This transport is operating under federal security clearance. We are carrying high-priority, volatile hazard materials. Any delay in our transit schedule is a direct violation of national security protocols."

Dickkopf's face darkened. He had heard this kind of talk before from out-of-state cargo drivers trying to dodge a weigh station or a logbook check. But the tone in this driver's voice wasn't defensive—it was dismissive. Like he was talking to a child.

"Federal clearance?" Dickkopf sneered, leaning in closer, the sour smell of his tobacco breath filling the gap in the window. "I don't give a damn if you're carrying the President's personal laundry, mister. You were weaving across the line back there. That's reckless operation, and around here, that means you step out of the cab and show me your license, registration, and cargo manifest. Right now."

"I am not authorized to display the manifest," Frank said, his voice dropping a register, becoming cold, hard, and entirely unyielding. "And I am ordering you, for your own safety and the safety of the public, to step back from this vehicle. Return to your cruiser. I have already alerted my command. They are dispatching a response unit to resolve this situation."

Dickkopf laughed, a short, barking sound that was lost in the wind. "Your command? What, the post office? You think some feds are going to fly in here and tell me how to do my job? You've got ten seconds to unlock this door, or I'm dragging your smart ass out through the window."

He reached down, his gloved hand resting on the butt of his holster, flipping the safety strap off with a quiet, leather *click*.

Inside the cab, Frank's hand moved toward the dashboard, his thumb hovering over the manual release button for the cabin locks. He knew what was about to happen. He knew the type of man he was dealing with—the kind of small-town authority figure whose ego was more fragile than glass. If he resisted, the trooper would shoot or call for backup, creating a massive scene. If he complied, the trooper would try to search the trailer.

"Trooper," Frank said, looking him dead in the eye. "Look at the placard on the trailer. Look at the seals. If those hydraulic locks are tampered with, the internal pressure drops. The containment fails. You do not want to see what is inside that container."

"Is that a threat?" Dickkopf shouted, his temper finally snapping. He reached up, grabbed the door handle, and yanked it. The door was locked, but the heavy steel latch clicked. "Unlock the door! Unlock it now!"

***

In the cabin of the Black Hawk, the wind howled through the open side doors. Captain Vance leaned forward, looking at the tactical tablet mounted to the bulkhead. The red line of their flight path was closing in on the highway marker.

"ETA three minutes," the pilot radioed back. "We're hitting some heavy headwind off the river, but we'll have you on the deck shortly."

Vance turned to his heavy weapons specialist, a massive woman named Stone. "Stone, what's our loadout status?"

"Two hundred rounds of Shredder per man," Stone said, her voice muffled by her respirator. She held up a long, plastic shell filled with jagged, triangular steel darts. "If they get close, these will strip the meat off their bones. And I've got three magazines of Cherry Pi for the group targets. One hit, and the explosive force will blow them to pieces. H2H weapons are locked in the rear rack."

"Good," Vance said. "Remember, this isn't a rescue mission. Our primary objective is the containment of *CON-TRANS 09*. If the driver is clean, we pull him out. If he's compromised, we neutralize him. And if the local trooper has already touched the doors..." He paused, his eyes dark. "We sterilize the scene."

Doc, the team's medical officer, looked up from his kit. "Commander, the nearby airport has five international departures scheduled in the next hour. If even one host slips past our line and makes it to the terminal, the vector is gone. We won't be able to track it."

"We won't let them make it to the terminal," Vance said. "We hold the highway. We block the road, and we keep them pinned against the river."

The chopper suddenly tilted, its nose dipping as it began its steep descent. Through the open door, Vance could see the dark, wooded hills of the Hudson Valley rushing up to meet them, the white fog clinging to the trees like spiderwebs. Ahead, the twin lines of the highway emerged from the forest, marked by the tiny, flashing blue and red lights of a single patrol car.

***

On the shoulder of Interstate 87, Trooper Dickkopf had stopped asking. 

He had walked back to his cruiser, retrieved a heavy, steel crowbar from the trunk, and was now standing at the rear of the truck's trailer. The yellow HAZMAT placard gleamed in the glare of his headlights.

"Trooper! Stop!" Frank shouted, standing on the highway asphalt, his hands raised in a desperate attempt to de-escalate. He had stepped out of the cab to get between the cop and the trailer, but Dickkopf had drawn his weapon, forcing Frank back.

"Keep your distance!" Dickkopf barked, his eyes wild, his breathing ragged. He was completely focused on the trailer. He was convinced this wasn't a biohazard transport. No real federal biohazard truck would be driving down a public highway without an escort. This was a smuggler. A high-value cargo of contraband, drugs, or illegal immigrants. He could smell it. This was the bust that would make his career.

"You're making a mistake!" Frank yelled, his eyes dating to the sky. In the distance, through the thick fog, he could hear the faint, rhythmic *thump-thump-thump* of heavy helicopter rotors. They were coming. But they were too far.

"Shut up!" Dickkopf yelled. He jammed the flat end of the crowbar into the seam of the electronic control box mounted next to the hydraulic lock bars. "I'm checking this cargo. And if you don't like it, you can tell it to the judge."

He threw his weight against the crowbar. The steel groaned. The plastic cover of the control box cracked, exposing a web of colored wires and a small, glowing green LED.

"Don't do it!" Frank warned, taking a step back, his eyes widening. He looked at the side of the trailer, his muscles tensing as he prepared to run.

Dickkopf didn't listen. He raised the crowbar and smashed the control box. 

The green LED went red. 

Inside the trailer, a deep, heavy click echoed through the metal—the sound of the primary pressure seal releasing.

A high-pitched hiss of air escaped from the seams of the doors, carrying a foul, cold blast of air that smelled of decay, wet copper, and old, stagnant water. It was a smell so thick, so heavy, that Dickkopf instantly gagged, dropping his crowbar as he coughed, his eyes watering.

"What the... what is that?" he gasped, covering his nose with his sleeve.

The hydraulic bars began to slide upward, moving with a slow, mechanical hiss.

The door didn't swing open. It was pushed.

A massive, violent force hit the heavy titanium doors from the inside, throwing them open with a deafening *bang* that broke the hinges.

Dickkopf stumbled back, his hand flying to his holster. "State Police! Get back! Get on the floor!" he screamed, his voice rising to a frantic, terrified pitch.

From the dark, cold interior of the trailer, the dead emerged.

They didn't look like people anymore. They were a mass of grey, ruined flesh, their bodies twisted and broken, their skin flayed and dirty from the original containment zones. Their eyes were wide, black, and completely empty of light.

And they were moving fast.

"Oh god," Dickkopf whispered, his arrogance vanishing in a single, icy instant of absolute realization. He raised his Sig Sauer and fired.

*Pop. Pop. Pop.*

The bullets tore into the chest of the first figure—a woman whose face had been torn open to show the jawbone beneath. She didn't slow down. She didn't scream. She simply lunged, her grey, dirt-stained hands clawing the air.

Frank didn't wait. He turned, grabbed the metal rung of the ladder mounted to the side of the trailer, and began to climb, pulling himself up with frantic, desperate speed.

Below him, the highway erupted into a nightmare.

Officer Dickkopf didn't even have time to turn and run. The first wave of the undead hit him like a physical wall, throwing him onto the wet asphalt. He screamed—a high, piercing wail that cut through the fog—as dozens of cold, grey hands tore at his uniform, his hair, his flesh.

"No! No! Please—"

The scream was cut short as a mouth, wet with black blood, clamped down on his throat.

From atop the trailer, Frank looked down. Trooper Dickkopf was gone, buried beneath a writhing, tearing mass of the dead. His cruiser's lights continued to flash, casting long, red and blue strobes across the bloody road.

And in the distance, the helicopter finally emerged from the fog.


CHAPTER 2: THE FEAST AND THE FOG

Frank clung to the cold titanium ribbing at the top of the trailer, his fingers numb and his knuckles white. The metal hummed with the vibration of the truck's still-idling diesel engine, a low, mechanical growl that felt entirely detached from the slaughter taking place ten feet below him. 

The autumn fog was thick now, rolling off the hemlocks and settling into the low hollows of the highway, turning the grey afternoon into a dull, white twilight. It pooled around the tires, spreading across the two lanes of the highway.

Directly below him, the feast was entering its final, horrific stage. 

Trooper Dickkopf's screams had devolved into a series of wet, bubbling gasps, then into a wet, rhythmic rattling, and finally, into nothing at all. There were seven of them on him—men and women who were the contained specimens of the upstate outbreak, their bodies bearing the marks of that previous struggle. They did not eat with the hunger of animals. They tore with a cold, mechanical focus, their grey fingers digging into his tactical vest, ripping through the heavy nylon fabric to get to the warm meat beneath. 

A tall, gaunt host—whose lips had been sliced away and replaced with rusted copper staples—yanked Dickkopf's head back by his hair. With a wet, ripping sound, the creature tore the throat out, releasing a dark fountain of blood that sprayed across the cruiser's hood. The others descended on the torso. The sound of cracking bone, of ribs snapping like green twigs under the weight of grey hands, rose through the cold air.

Frank turned his head away, his stomach heaving. He had been with the Directorate for four years, had driven three separate containment transfers through designated corridors in the Southwest, but he had never seen a breach from this close. The sheer speed of the transition—from a stubborn, arrogant local cop to a pile of wet, white bones—was a terrifying testament to the nature of the containment subjects.

He reached down to his belt, his fingers searching for his tactical radio, his movement slow and deliberate to avoid attracting the attention of the swarm. He pulled the small, reinforced terminal from its pouch. The screen was flickering, the signals weak due to the deep mountain valley.

"Control, this is Operator Frank," he whispered, his voice trembling despite his training. "We have a Level-5 Breach. I repeat, Level-5 Breach at marker 116.3. The local trooper bypassed the lockbox. The container is fully vented. I am currently holding a position atop the rig, but the host cluster is active and dispersing. Requesting immediate tactical extraction."

The speaker hissed, the sound of heavy static roaring through the earpiece. 

"...Frank... Control... signal... weak... repeat... coordinates..."

The signal cut out completely. 

Frank swore under his breath, slipping the radio back into its pouch. The poor reception in the mountain pass was a critical failure. He was on his own.

Below, the grey shapes were beginning to lift their heads from the red asphalt. Trooper Dickkopf's cruiser sat in the center of the carnage, its engine still running, the dashboard camera recording the scene through a windshield spattered with the trooper's blood. The blue and red strobe lights continued to sweep across the road, illuminating the white, gnawed rib cage and the scattered, torn pieces of dark blue uniform that were all that remained of Unit 412.

The undead didn't linger over the bones. Their black, empty eyes scanned the highway, their heads tilting in unison as a new sound drifted through the fog.

From the south, the headlights of an oncoming vehicle cut through the white soup.

It was a family sedan—a dusty blue station wagon, its roof packed with luggage secured by yellow bungee cords. It was moving at a cautious forty miles per hour, its windshield wipers sweeping a slow, rhythmic path across the glass.

"No," Frank muttered, leaning over the edge of the trailer. "Turn around. Get the hell out of here!"

But his voice was swallowed by the wind.

The station wagon slowed down as it approached the flashing lights of the patrol car. The driver, a middle-aged man in a flannel shirt, leaned forward, squinting through the windshield at the dark, jackknifed shape of the transport truck and the debris scattered across the road.

"Is that... is that an accident?" a woman's voice asked from the passenger seat.

The driver didn't answer. He stopped the car twenty yards away, the headlights illuminating the wet, red slick on the asphalt and the dark, grey shapes standing in the mist.

"Daddy, what's that?" a young girl asked from the back seat, pointing at the figure standing near the truck's rear wheels.

It was the woman with the torn jaw. She stood perfectly still, her head tilted at an unnatural, forty-five-degree angle, her black eyes reflecting the car's headlights like twin pools of motor oil.

The driver of the station wagon put the car in reverse, his tires chirping on the wet asphalt. "Martha, get the map. We need to go back."

He never got the chance.

From the wooded shoulder of the road, three hosts lunged. They didn't run like humans; they moved in a series of violent, jerky sprints, their broken limbs resetting with sickening snaps as they closed the distance. The first one—a former construction worker with steel bolts driven through his collarbone—hit the driver's side window with his forehead.

The glass shattered into a thousand spiderwebs.

The driver screamed as the grey hands reached through the broken frame, clawing at his face, his throat, pulling him toward the jagged glass. 

In the passenger seat, the woman screamed, desperately clawing at the door handle, trying to open it and run. But before she could clear the seat, another host—a small, grey child whose chest had been flayed open to show the blackened lungs beneath—shattered the passenger window with a rusted pipe.

The car became a cage of metal and screaming. 

Frank watched in frozen horror as the passenger door was finally kicked open. The woman stumbled out onto the gravel, her face covered in blood, her hands outstretched toward the dark woods. She took three steps before the gaunt host with the copper staples caught her, dragging her down into the ditch.

The headlights of the station wagon remained on, shining into the trees, illuminating the grey, writhing shapes as they worked in the wet leaves.

A second vehicle—a white cargo van—crept out of the fog from the north. The driver saw the flashing lights, the idling station wagon, and the blood on the road, and instantly slammed on his brakes. The van skidded, its rear end swinging wide and slamming into the guardrail with a loud, metallic *crunch*.

The driver, a young man in a delivery uniform, threw the door open and scrambled out, running north along the highway.

"Run!" Frank shouted, his voice cracking. "Keep running!"

The delivery driver didn't look back. He ran with panic-fueled speed, his boots pounding the asphalt. But he didn't see the two figures emerging from the shadow of the bridge. They lunged at him, teeth bared, dragging him down in a flurry of biting. 

He screamed once, a short, sharp cry of agony. When the figures rose a minute later, he lay still on the asphalt before slowly dragging himself to his feet, his jaw hanging loose, his eyes glazed.

He turned back toward the van, his movements already taking on the stiff, mechanical rhythm of the swarm.

The highway was no longer a road. It was a throat, and the container was pouring the dead directly into it.

Above, the thump of the Black Hawk's rotors grew deafening. The massive, black shape of the helicopter cleared the treeline, its searchlight cutting through the white fog like a solid bar of silver.

Frank looked up, raising his hand to shield his eyes from the glare.

Strike Team Romeo had arrived. But as Frank looked down at the highway corridor, he knew they were already too late. The hosts had already breached the tree line, heading northeast through the valley.

Directly toward the lights of the New York airport, gleaming like a constellation of cold stars on the horizon.


CHAPTER 3: THE RACE AGAINST THE SYSTEM

The interior of the Black Hawk was a study in green-tinted shadow and mechanical roar. The vibration of the twin turbine engines felt like a physical pressure in the ears, shaking the metal bulkheads and vibrating through the soles of Captain Vance's boots. 

He sat near the open cabin door, his harness buckled tight, staring down at the glowing screen of the tactical tablet mounted to the bulkhead. The blue dot representing their helicopter, callsign *Romeo-1*, was moving at one hundred and forty knots through the mountainous terrain of the Hudson Valley. But the telemetry coming from the ground was rapidly turning to red.

"Telemetry is flatlining, Captain," Stone's voice crackled through the intercom, her respirator mask breathing heavy and mechanical over the static. "The pressure sensor in the containment tank went to zero exactly forty-five seconds ago. The remote bio-scanners are registering a localized spike. The doors are open."

Vance reached up, pressing the headset cup closer to his ear. "And the driver? What's Frank's beacon status?"

"Active, but stationary," Stone replied. She was holding her rifle, her gloved fingers tracing the seam of a specialized magazine. "He's logged at the truck coordinates. No movement. His cabin camera feed went dark thirty seconds after the lockbox was breached. We're flying blind on the actual ground status."

"Control, this is Romeo-1," Vance called out, switching his radio channel to the New Jersey command frequency. "We are two minutes from the drop site. Telemetry indicates a complete containment failure. Requesting secondary tactical options. Do we have clearance for a thermobaric strike? I repeat, do we have TVS authority to sterilize the highway corridor?"

A long five seconds of static filled the line, the sound of the chopper's blades carving through the wet air.

"Negative, Romeo-1," Director Graves's voice came through, sounding thin, distant, and tight with suppressed tension. "The wind is blowing northeast at twelve knots. The Interstate corridor sits directly downwind of the regional New York airport, less than fifteen miles away. A thermobaric detonation of that scale would create a massive thermal plume that would drift directly into the commercial flight paths. More importantly, the electromagnetic fallout would shut down the entire airspace. The political fallout is non-viable. You are operating under a manual containment mandate. I repeat, big weapons are off the table. You hold the highway on foot."

"Manual containment?" Doc, the medical officer, muttered over the internal net, his head shaking behind his dark visor. "The container had forty hosts, Captain. If they hit the highway traffic, that number doubles in minutes. We have six rifles. That's not manual containment. That's a suicide run."

"We don't need to kill them all, Doc," Vance said, his voice cold and steady. "We just need to block the neck. The highway has steep rock cuts on the east side and the river on the west. If we set down on the south side of the bridge, we can create a hard line. We channel them into our kill zone. Stone, what's our weapon configuration?"

Stone tapped the heavy barrel shroud of her M4. "Shredder rounds are ready in the primary drums. For the new guys on the team, remember the spread: at fifty yards, the Shredder round behaves like a standard sabot, but at twenty yards, the nose cone splits, throwing out thirty-six micro-darts of tungsten-alloy. It's designed to shred the muscle fibers in the thighs and pelvis. You don't try to kill them; you take their legs. If they can't walk, they can't spread."

"And the Cherry Pi?" Vance asked.

"Reserved for the dense clusters," Stone said, gesturing to the three magazines taped with red electrical tape at her chest. "These are high-yield explosive rounds. They are designed to detonate on impact, creating a localized blast that tears hosts to pieces. One round will clear a five-foot radius if they are grouped."

"Keep the Cherry Pi for the container exit," Vance ordered. "If they're still bottlenecked near the trailer, we dump the explosives there. Stone, you and Kowalski hold the left side of the road. Doc, you and Vargas take the right. I'll secure Frank if he's still breathing. If we run out of ammo, we rely on the H2H weapons on our belts. Do not waste ammo on single targets if you can take them with the blades."

He reached down to his hip, his hand resting on the grip of his tactical machete—a heavy, twenty-inch blade of matte-black carbon steel, its edge ground to a razor-sharp, chisel point. 

"Captain, the wind is getting worse," the pilot's voice broke in. "We're losing visibility in the valley fog, and the telemetry from the road is showing multiple vehicular fire signatures. The road is blocked."

"Set us down on the south side of the bridge at marker 116," Vance said. "We get out, we establish the line, and we push north to the truck. Frank is our priority. He has the transport keys. If we can reseal that container, we might have a chance to contain the source."

"Understood, Romeo-1," the pilot said. "Hang on. We're going down."

The Black Hawk tilted sharply, the cabin floor sloping as the aircraft dropped through the thick, grey mist. Through the open door, Vance could see the dark, wet forest rushing past, the pine branches shaking in the downwash of the rotors. And then, the highway appeared—a dark, silent line of asphalt cutting through the trees, illuminated only by the burning wreck of a white cargo van and the flashing blue lights of a single patrol car.


CHAPTER 4: THE OUTBREAK CORRIDOR

The valley fog rolled along the ground, clinging to the asphalt and the guardrails, turning the yellow road lines into dull, brown scars. The hemlocks on either side of the road disappeared into the gloom, their branches dripping with condensation.

The highway was rapidly gridlocking. 

Three hundred yards to the north, the taillights of a dozen vehicles glowed a panicked, solid red in the fog. Word of the obstruction had not traveled down the line, but the physical reality of the pile-up had. A heavy utility truck, attempting to swerve around the skidding cargo van, had clipped a concrete barrier, jackknifing across both lanes. Behind it, a line of commuter cars had ground to a halt, their engines idling, their exhaust pipes sending up plumes of white steam.

Then the screaming began.

It started at the front of the block and traveled south like an acoustic shockwave. The motorists, trapped in their steel cages, had no idea what was emerging from the fog. They only saw the shapes—grey, bloody figures running with a wild, disjointed gait through the spaces between the cars.

"Get out of the cars!" Frank yelled, though his voice was swallowed by the rising roar of a dozen car horns. "Run for the woods! Don't stay in the vehicles!"

A driver in a black SUV threw his door open, stepping onto the asphalt with a heavy tire iron in his hand. He looked toward the jackknifed utility truck, squinting through the fog. 

"Hey! What the hell is going on up there?" he shouted.

He never received an answer. A grey shape—a woman whose chest had been crushed by the hydraulic doors of the container, her left arm dangling by a single strand of tendon—lunged from the shadow of the concrete barrier. She hit him with the weight of a falling log, driving him onto the road. The tire iron clattered against the asphalt.

Inside the SUV, a woman and a young boy screamed as the driver was dragged beneath the chassis. Five seconds later, the driver's door of the SUV was pulled open from the inside. The driver stood up, blood pouring from his face. His eyes were black and dead. He didn't look at his family; he turned toward the sedan behind him, his teeth bared, and lunged.

The conversions were spreading with exponential, geometric efficiency. Motorists attempting to flee were cornered in the gridlock, bitten, and pulled down onto the blood-spattered highway.

Frank watched a young couple sprint from their car toward the western shoulder, trying to reach the steep, rocky slope that fell away toward the Hudson River. They were fast, but the hosts were faster. A group of four grey shapes cut them off at the guardrail. The man tried to fight, swinging a heavy flashlight, but the hosts didn't even flinch at the blows. A woman with rusted wire wrapped around her neck took a full strike to the temple. She didn't fall. She simply reached out, her grey fingers locking onto the man's arm, pulling him down, her teeth sinking into his shoulder.

His partner screamed, turning back toward the highway, but she was met by the rising figure of the delivery driver who had turned only minutes before.

"It's a slaughterhouse," Frank whispered, his hands tensing as he gripped the cold metal of the trailer. He looked down at the cab of his truck. The engine was still running, the dashboard instrument panel glowing a faint, mocking blue through the windshield. If he could get down, if he could slip into the cab and throw the rig into reverse, he might be able to plow through the wreckage and escape.

But the ladder was no longer clear.

Three hosts—including the stapled-lipped host that had torn the throat out of Officer Dickkopf—were standing at the base of the rig. They didn't climb; their coordinate motor control was too crude for the vertical rungs. Instead, they stood in the fog, their black eyes fixed on Frank, their fingers scraping against the white steel of the trailer with a dry, rhythmic sound that sounded like dry pine needles scratching a window.

*Scratch. Scratch. Scratch.*

They were waiting. They knew he couldn't stay up there forever. The cold was rising, the wind off the river carrying a freezing sleet that was already turning the top of the trailer into a slick, icy slide. If his grip slipped, if his muscles cramped from the cold, he would drop straight into their waiting hands.

And then, the sound of the chopper's rotors shifted. 

The heavy, rhythmic *thump-thump-thump* of the Black Hawk descended, the downwash of the blades hitting the fog and blowing it back in a massive, swirling vortex. The searchlight swept across the highway, illuminating the piles of smashed cars, the pools of dark blood, and the grey shapes moving through the fog.

Through the white glare of the searchlight, Frank saw the side doors of the chopper slide back. 

Strike Team Romeo was coming down the ropes.


CHAPTER 5: TACTICAL INSERTION

The Black Hawk flared its tail, its massive rotor downwash flattening the high marsh grass on the western side of the highway as it hovered four feet above the asphalt. The landing gear touched down on the southbound lane with a heavy, mechanical shudder.

"Go! Go! Go!" Captain Vance barked through his throat mic, sliding down the fast-rope before the wheels had fully settled.

He hit the wet road in a crouch, his boots splashing in a shallow pool of water mixed with transmission fluid and fresh blood. He raised his M4, his weapon light cutting a white cone through the swirling fog. Behind him, the rest of Strike Team Romeo dropped in rapid succession—five black-clad shapes moving with the synchronized precision of a single machine.

"Stone, Kowalski—establish the line on the south side of the bridge!" Vance ordered, gesturing toward the narrow concrete span that crossed a deep, rocky ravine. "Doc, Vargas—on me. We push north toward the rig. Watch the gaps between the cars."

"Romeo-1, this is Pilot," the radio crackled. "We have multiple heat signatures closing from the treeline on the east side of the road. We can't stay on the deck. The valley fog is getting too thick. We are dusting off to five hundred feet. Call for extraction when you have the package. Out."

The Black Hawk surged upward, its engine screaming as it climbed back into the soup, the searchlight tilting to illuminate the road ahead of the team before the aircraft vanished from sight.

"Establish perimeter!" Stone rumbled.

She and Kowalski dropped to one knee, their heavy barrels resting on the hood of an abandoned sedan. Fifty yards to the north, the first wave of the hosts emerged from the fog. They didn't move in a line; they swarmed, thirty or forty grey shapes sprinting through the wreckage, their bare feet slapping the wet asphalt.

"Target distance, forty yards," Kowalski muttered, his finger tightening on the trigger of his heavy squad automatic weapon.

"Hold," Stone said, her visor locked on the lead host—a man in a torn business suit whose jaw hung loose, his throat completely black with congealed blood. "Let them group. We want the Shredder spread to count."

Looking at them, Stone felt a chill. These were the same faces from the upstate containment vector three weeks ago. R.E.A.C.T. had swept that town, locked the remaining hosts in the secure transport, and marked the outbreak closed. No one knew how the outbreak had started in that sleepy upstate town, or what had turned those citizens into the undead. But they had contained it. It was supposed to be over.

Thirty yards. Twenty-five.

"Fire!"

Both rifles erupted with a sharp, repeating *thunk-thunk-thunk*. The sound was different from standard gunfire—lower, heavier, the mechanical cycle of the specialized gas blocks working the massive Shredder cartridges.

At twenty yards, the nose cones of the rounds split. 

The air was instantly filled with a horizontal sheet of tungsten micro-darts, a whistling wall of metal that tore into the leading rank of the swarm. The effect was immediate and devastating. The lead hosts didn't fall dead; their legs simply disintegrated, the muscle fibers and bone structures shredded to grey pulp by the high-velocity darts. 

The businessman collapsed mid-stride, his torso hitting the asphalt at twenty miles per hour, sliding forward on his face while his ruined thighs trailed behind him in wet ribbons. Behind him, three more hosts went down in a tangled heap, their hips shattered, their legs no longer able to support their weight. They didn't stop moving—they clawed at the asphalt, dragging their heavy upper bodies forward with their hands—but the momentum of the charge was broken.

"Re-loading!" Kowalski shouted, swapping the drum.

"Vance, we have a breach on the east shoulder!" Stone called out. "They're coming through the guardrail! The other side of the road is completely open!"

Vance didn't look back. "Keep them pinned, Stone! Vargas, with me!"

He, Doc, and Vargas pushed north through the corridor of abandoned cars. Vance kept his weapon raised, his eyes scanning the gaps.

Suddenly, a side door of a station wagon flew open. A grey shape—a woman with her eyelids pinned back by rusted staples—lunged at him from the passenger seat.

Vance didn't fire. He stepped aside, the movement smooth and practiced, and brought the heavy pommel of his M4 down on the bridge of her nose. The bone shattered with a wet *pop*, but she didn't slow down, her grey fingers clawing at his throat. Vance spun, drawing his tactical machete in a single, fluid arc. The black carbon-steel blade sliced through the neck, sending the head bouncing across the road. The body collapsed.

"Cherry Pi in the gap!" Vargas yelled.

She raised her rifle, aiming at a dense cluster of five hosts emerging from the rear doors of a school bus. She squeezed the trigger once.

The round detonated with a sharp, echoing *crack* right in the center of the group. The blast tore through the chests and limbs of the hosts, blowing off legs and arms, throwing them back into the vehicle's interior. The dense bottleneck was instantly cleared by the explosion, leaving only a pile of shattered, twitching remains.

"It works," Doc muttered, firing a Shredder round into a host crawling from beneath a sedan. "But they're slipping past. Look at the opposite lane."

Through the gap between two semi-trailers, Vance saw the horror.

On the eastern side of the highway—the northbound lane—the guardrail had been smashed by a jackknifed flatbed. The undead were streaming through the gap. They weren't heading toward the R.E.A.C.T. defensive line; they were heading northeast, bypassing the bridge completely, their grey shapes running through the deep, rocky ditch and into the thick woods on the far side.

"They're bypassing us," Vance said, his voice dropping a register as the dread set in. "They're not trying to fight. They're just dispersing."

"Frank!" Vargas shouted, pointing toward the white containment truck fifty yards ahead.

Frank was still atop the trailer, his face pale behind his grease-stained blue uniform. He was waving his arms, pointing at the base of the rig where three hosts were attempting to climb the tires, their grey fingers reaching for the low rungs of the ladder.

"Hold the lane, Doc!" Vance ordered. "Vargas, cover the rear. I'm going for the driver."

Vance sprinted forward, his boots pounding the wet road. As he closed the distance, the gaunt host with the stapled lips turned toward him, its mouth opening in a silent, bloody snarl.

Vance raised his rifle, aiming at the creature's chest, and squeezed the trigger. The Shredder round took the host's right hip, tearing the joint apart. The creature spun, falling to the road, but its hands kept clawing, dragging its torso toward Vance's boots.

Vance stepped over it, reached the ladder, and looked up at Frank.

"Get down!" Vance barked. "Now! We're pulling out!"

Frank didn't hesitate. He swung his legs over the edge, grabbed the top rung, and began his rapid descent, his boots slipping on the wet metal rungs as he scrambled down to the road.

"We have the keys, Captain!" Frank gasped, his hand clutching a heavy steel ring with three electronic keycards. "But the container is empty. They're all out. Every single one of them."

Vance looked at the open, dark maw of the trailer doors. The interior was a silent, empty cavern.

"It doesn't matter," Vance said, grabbing Frank by the shoulder and pushing him south. "The highway is gone. The line has failed. We need to dust off."

Behind them, the forest on the eastern side of the highway was alive with the sound of snapping branches and the rustling of wet leaves. 

Hundreds of them were moving through the dark, heading northeast through the valley.

Toward the airport.


CHAPTER 6: CONTAINMENT FAILURE AND DUST OFF

The retreat was not a tactical withdrawal; it was a desperate, clawing fight for yardage through a tightening gauntlet of teeth and grey flesh. 

"Romeo-1, we need extraction at the bridge coordinates!" Vance screamed into his mic, his voice cracking against the static as he fired a final three-round burst of Shredder. "We have the driver. The line is breached. I repeat, the line is breached! We are being enveloped!"

"Romeo-1, Pilot," the reply was nearly buried in static and the high-pitched whine of turbine strain. "We can't... visibility... zero... the fog has settled in... we are losing engine pressure... we have one minute before we are forced to dump the fuel and glide... we are coming down on the south side of the bridge, but you need to be there *now*!"

Vance shoved Frank ahead of him, sending the driver stumbling toward the concrete span. "Vargas! Doc! Fall back! Stone, Kowalski—give us some fire in the center!"

"Out!" Kowalski's voice was a flat, desperate bark. "SAW is dry! I'm on sidearm!"

"I've got three rounds of Shredder left!" Stone shouted, her M4 firing a final, heavy *thunk-thunk-thunk* that severed the knees of a host lunging from the hood of a sedan. The host hit the asphalt, its jaw scraping the road, but its grey fingers still clawed at her boots.

A wave of grey shapes—at least fifty of them—poured out from the gap between the jackknifed flatbed and the concrete barrier. They were no longer running in disjointed patterns. The presence of the R.E.A.C.T. squad, with their flashing weapon lights and the report of their weapons, had acted as a beacon, drawing the horde back toward the bridge.

"Ammo is spent!" Doc shouted, throwing his empty M4 rifle strap over his shoulder. "They're on the rail!"

"Blades out!" Vance roared, his hand ripping his tactical machete from its sheath. "H2H combat! Hold the bridge entrance!"

The transition from firearms to steel was immediate and brutal. The cold, wet air was filled with the sharp, clean chime of carbon steel striking bone, the wet, slicing sound of blades cutting through rotten muscle, and the heavy, dull thuds of tactical clubs.

Stone led the line, her heavy, two-handed breaching axe swinging in a wide, horizontal arc. The steel head caught a host—a former truck driver with wire wrapped around his wrists—in the side of the neck, cleaving through the vertebra with a solid *tock*. The head flew into the ditch, but she didn't pause, using the momentum of the swing to drive the butt of the shaft into the chest of a second attacker, breaking the ribs and throwing it back into the swarm.

To her right, Kowalski fought with a heavy, steel-headed trench club, his swings methodical and crushing. *Crack. Crack.* Every blow was aimed at the knees and the elbows, disabling the host's limbs, rendering them useless. A host with its forearms shattered could still bite, but it couldn't grab, and on a bridge narrow enough to bottle them, a disabled host became a physical obstacle for the ones behind it.

Vance was a blur of black steel, his machete slicing through the grey hands that reached for Frank. He stayed close to the driver, his left hand gripping Frank's harness, dragging him backward across the concrete bridge deck.

"Get to the chopper!" Vance yelled.

At the far end of the bridge, the Black Hawk descended through the fog. The aircraft was barely stable, its tail rotor swinging wildly in the shifting wind, the searchlight flickering as the pilot held the ship in a low hover, the wheels scraping the asphalt, the cabin doors wide open.

"Romeo-1! Get in! Get the hell in!" the crew chief screamed, leaning out of the door, his hands outstretched.

Vargas was the first to reach the cabin, her hands slick with black blood as she grabbed the step and pulled herself up. She turned immediately, drawing her sidearm, firing three quick shots into the face of a host that was crawling over the bridge rail.

Doc threw Frank up next. The driver was gasping, his hands shaking so violently he couldn't find a handhold, but Doc and the crew chief dragged him into the webbing seats.

"Stone! Kowalski! Go!" Vance yelled, his machete blocking a downward swing from a host wielding a broken car mirror. Vance ducked, drove his blade through the host's collarbone, and kicked the body off the bridge into the ravine below.

Stone retreated step-by-step, her axe swinging in short, defensive jabs. A host caught her shoulder, its teeth scraping against the heavy Kevlar plating of her vest, but Kowalski brought his club down on the back of the creature's skull, shattering the bone.

"Up! Get up!" Kowalski grunted, grabbing Stone's harness and hoisting her into the chopper's cabin. He scrambled in behind her, his boots slipping on the bloody floorboards.

Vance was the last on the bridge. 

He took three steps back, his eyes locked on the road behind them. The highway bridge was completely choked with the dead. They were piled three deep, a writhing, snapping wall of grey flesh that was pressing against the narrow concrete rails. They didn't feel pain, they didn't feel fear, and they were moving with a slow, heavy certainty that could not be bargained with.

Vance turned, leaped for the chopper's step, and grabbed the handrail. 

"Pull us up!" he screamed. "Go! Go!"

The turboshaft engines screamed, a high, desperate wail as the pilot pulled the collective. The Black Hawk lifted, its tail swinging wide, its rotor blades clipping the branches of a hemlock with a series of sharp, wood-splintering cracks. The chopper surged upward, climbing away from the bridge into the thick, grey mist.

Vance leaned out of the open door, his hand gripping the safety strap, looking down at the highway.

The searchlight flickered once, then died, leaving the scene illuminated only by the burning wreck of the cargo van and the flashing blue lights of Unit 412.

Through the dim, bloody twilight, Vance saw the truth.

The R.E.A.C.T. squad had held the bridge. They had saved Frank. But their containment line had been a line in the sand against a rising tide. 

Below, the northbound lane was a river of grey shapes. Hundreds of them, thousands of them, were streaming past the abandoned bridge, running through the woods and down the highway shoulders, their movements collective and directed.

They were heading northeast. 

And on the horizon, through a break in the trees, the high, glass towers of the New York airport terminal gleamed in the distance, completely unaware of the horde rolling through the valley toward their gates.

Vance pulled himself back into the cabin, sliding the heavy metal door shut, sealing out the wind.

He looked at Frank, then at his exhausted, bleeding squad.

No one spoke. 

It was a retrieval success. But as Vance stared at the floorboards, his hand slick with black blood, he knew the truth.

Nothing had gone well.
